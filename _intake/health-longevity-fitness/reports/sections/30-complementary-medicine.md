# 30 — Complementary & Alternative Medicine (graded)

Complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) is a patchwork: a handful of real, narrow tools, a large
middle of expensive placebo, a toxicology hazard at the edges, and one failure mode that kills — using
any of it *instead of* a treatment that works. The fair way to sort the patchwork is to put every
modality on the same evidence ladder as mainstream medicine and read the grade honestly: nothing
dismissed for being unfashionable, nothing laundered for being popular.

A modality that helps is graded `works`; one that is inert is graded `placebo-level`; one that is
dangerous is graded `harmful`; and — crucially — **the same modality can be all three depending on the
question and the use.** Acupuncture for chemotherapy nausea is not acupuncture for infertility.
St John's Wort for mild depression is not St John's Wort taken alongside an organ-transplant drug.
"Integrative oncology" massage for anxiety is not "alternative" coffee enemas instead of chemotherapy.
The unit of grading is **modality × indication × instead-of-or-alongside**, never the brand.

_Not medical advice. Placebo and surgical-sham data: §14 §6 and §21; oncology mortality: §25;
supplement/herb evidence rules: §03._

Five honesty rules run through every line below. They are the whole chapter in miniature:

1. **The grade is per-indication, not per-modality.** "Does acupuncture work?" is an unanswerable
   question; "does acupuncture reduce chronic low-back pain more than sham?" has an answer (barely).
2. **Sham control is the truth serum.** The interesting comparison is almost never *treatment vs nothing*
   (that bundles placebo, attention, regression to the mean, and natural history). It is *treatment vs a
   convincing fake*. When the fake works as well as the real thing, the specific theory is dead even if
   the ritual still helps.
3. **Placebo is real but bounded.** Expectation genuinely changes pain, nausea, and other *subjective,
   self-reported, brain-modulated* outcomes (Section 14 §6). It does **not** shrink tumours, clear
   infections, lower blood sugar, or reopen arteries. The harm of CAM is rarely the sugar pill; it is the
   *opportunity cost* of using a sugar pill where a real cure exists — the cost §9.3 puts a number on.
4. **"Natural" is a marketing word, not a safety claim.** Hemlock, arsenic, aristolochic acid, and
   amanita are natural. Herbs are unstandardised drugs with real pharmacology, real interactions, and —
   for some traditional preparations — real heavy-metal contamination.
5. **Integrative ≠ evidence.** Putting an unproven therapy inside a hospital, next to a proven one,
   does not upgrade it. The label "integrative medicine" is an organisational fact, not an evidentiary one.

## 1. The verdict table (modality × indication)

Grade key — best evidence for the specific use, worst evidence for the specific harm:
- **works** — consistent human RCT (randomized controlled trial — the strongest evidence tier) / meta-analysis evidence for a real, specific effect (often narrow).
- **works-for-some-things** — real for a short list of indications, inert/unproven for everything else it's sold for.
- **placebo-level** — performs no better than a convincing sham; the ritual may comfort, the mechanism is absent.
- **no-evidence** — claims outrun the data; effect is mechanistic, anecdotal, or untested in humans.
- **harmful** — direct harm (contamination, interaction, injury) and/or the indirect harm of displacing proven care.

The headline verdicts, before the details: a few things work narrowly (PC6 acupressure for nausea,
peppermint oil for IBS, spinal manipulation for acute back pain, mind–body movement); homeopathy and
energy healing are pure placebo; heavy-metal-bearing traditional preparations are toxic; and using any
of it *instead of* proven care is the one lethal move.

## 2. Acupuncture — the honest Cochrane/IPD picture

**What it claims.** Classical theory: needling specific points unblocks the flow of *qi* along *meridians*,
restoring balance. The traditional framework maps no known anatomical structure; meridians do not correspond
to nerves, vessels, or fascia, and *qi* is not a measured quantity.

**The best evidence.** The strongest, fairest dataset is the **Vickers individual-patient-data
meta-analysis** (Arch Intern Med 2012; updated J Pain 2018), pooling raw data from **39 high-quality
RCTs / ~20,827 patients** across four chronic-pain conditions (back/neck, osteoarthritis, chronic headache,
shoulder). Its findings are the honest centre of gravity for the whole field:

- Acupuncture beats **no-acupuncture** control by roughly **0.5 SD** (half a standard deviation — a clear, moderate effect).
- Acupuncture beats **sham** (fake/placebo needling) by only **~0.2 SD** (a fifth of one — a *small* effect).
- Effects **persist** at 12 months with only ~15% decay (i.e. it isn't a one-day wonder).

Read those two numbers together and the structure is unmistakable: **most of acupuncture's benefit is the
context — the ritual, the attention, the touch, the expectation — and only a sliver is attributable to
where (or whether) the needle actually goes.**

@@FIG:Q09-acupuncture@@

Cochrane reviews land in the same place by condition:
small benefits for **tension-type headache** and **migraine prophylaxis** and **chronic low-back/neck pain**;
the most robust *specific* effect is **PC6-point antiemesis** — acupuncture/acupressure reduces
**post-operative and chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting** (Cochrane Lee & colleagues), one of the few
places the signal survives decent blinding.

**The sham problem (the mechanism debate).** Trial after trial finds that **sham acupuncture — needling the
"wrong" points, or using retractable non-penetrating "placebo" needles — works almost as well as "real"
acupuncture.** If point location and skin penetration barely matter, then the *theory* (meridians, qi,
correct points) is not what's doing the work. The credible mechanistic story is **diffuse noxious inhibitory
control / counter-irritation, descending pain modulation, and a powerful theatrical placebo** — the same
endogenous-opioid machinery documented in Section 14 §6 (naloxone-reversible placebo analgesia). Honest
verdict: **works-for-some-things** — a real but modest tool for some chronic pain and for nausea, riding
mostly on placebo and counter-irritation, **not** on its traditional theory. It is **not** a treatment for
infertility, allergies, addiction cure, or internal disease; claims there are `no-evidence`. Direct harms are
rare but real (pneumothorax, infection from non-sterile needling).

## 3. Herbal medicine — the short real list vs the long hyped one

Herbs are **drugs**: unpurified, variably dosed, often unstandardised, sometimes contaminated, and capable of
real interactions. Grade the herb, the indication, *and* the product. (Cross-ref `03-nutrition-supplements.md`
for the supplement-versus-food honesty rules; the same predictor-≠-lever logic applies — a marker that
forecasts risk isn't automatically a knob that, turned, lowers it — as does dose-matters.)

**The ones with real human evidence:**

- **St John's Wort (*Hypericum perforatum*) — depression. `works` (narrow) + `harmful` (interactions).**
  The **Cochrane review (Linde 2008, 29 trials)**[^linde-sjw] found hypericum extracts are **superior to placebo
  and about as effective as standard antidepressants for mild-to-moderate major depression, with fewer
  dropouts for side-effects than SSRIs.** This is one of the best-supported herbal claims in medicine — *and*
  one of the most dangerous, because St John's Wort is a potent **CYP3A4 inducer and P-glycoprotein inducer**
  (it revs up the liver's main drug-clearing enzyme and the pump that keeps drugs out of cells).
  It can **gut the efficacy of** oral contraceptives, warfarin, ciclosporin (transplant rejection), HIV
  antiretrovirals, and many chemo/cardiac drugs, and it can cause **serotonin syndrome** with SSRIs/SNRIs.
  Honest framing: real medicine, *prescribe-grade* caution. German-language trials skewed more positive
  (a documented heterogeneity caveat).
- **Peppermint oil — IBS. `works`.** Enteric-coated peppermint oil is a smooth-muscle antispasmodic; pooled
  RCTs show a **risk ratio ~2.4 for global symptom improvement** and significant abdominal-pain relief vs
  placebo. It is in mainstream gastroenterology guidance. Main side-effect: reflux/heartburn.
- **Ginger — nausea. `works-for-some-things`.** Modest, real antiemetic for **pregnancy-related nausea**,
  and as an adjunct in post-operative and chemotherapy nausea. Not a cure for anything; effect size moderate.
- **Turmeric / curcumin — inflammation/OA pain. `no-evidence → emerging`.** Strong *in-vitro* anti-inflammatory
  mechanism, but **curcumin is famously near-unabsorbed** (poor solubility, rapid metabolism). Small RCTs in
  knee OA show pain reduction on par with NSAIDs, but trials are short, small, on **surrogate/subjective**
  endpoints, often industry-linked, and use bioavailability-enhanced formulations that muddy "turmeric." The
  honest grade is *promising-but-unproven*, not "miracle anti-inflammatory."

**The ones that fail their headline claim:**

- **Saw palmetto — BPH. `placebo-level` (settled).** The definitive **STEP trial (Bent, NEJM 2006)** and the
  **Cochrane review (Franco 2023)** show saw palmetto is **no better than placebo** for urinary symptoms of
  benign prostatic hyperplasia, even at double and triple doses. A clean, decisive null — and a useful
  case study in how early positive low-quality trials evaporate under rigorous design.
- **Echinacea — colds. `no-evidence → marginal`.** Heterogeneous trials; at best a small, unreliable
  shortening of cold duration; no convincing prevention.
- **Ginkgo biloba — memory/dementia. `placebo-level`.** The large **GEM (Ginkgo Evaluation of Memory) RCT**
  (>3,000 elderly, ~6 years) found **no reduction in dementia, Alzheimer's, or cognitive decline.**

**The risks that apply to all of them.** (1) **Contamination & adulteration** — herbal products have been
found spiked with undeclared pharmaceuticals (sildenafil, steroids, sibutramine) and contaminated with
metals/pesticides. (2) **No standardisation** — "500 mg of herb X" can vary tenfold in active content between
brands and batches. (3) **Interactions** — St John's Wort, ginkgo (bleeding), and others interact with
prescription drugs. (4) **Hepatotoxicity** — kava, comfrey, green-tea-extract megadoses, and others cause
real liver injury. (5) **DSHEA regulation gap** (see §9): in the US these are sold as "supplements," so they
reach the shelf **without proof of efficacy, purity, or safety** and are policed only after harm.

## 4. Traditional systems — TCM and Ayurveda

The honest frame for both Traditional Chinese Medicine and Ayurveda is a **two-layer split**:

**Layer 1 — the system's theory of the body is not biology.** *Qi*, meridians, yin/yang balance, and the
five phases (TCM); the three *doshas* (vata/pitta/kapha), five elements, and humoral balance (Ayurveda) are
**pre-scientific explanatory models**, not descriptions of physiology. They make no falsifiable, mechanism-level
predictions that survive testing, and diagnoses based on them (pulse/tongue reading to a dosha) do not
correspond to measurable disease states. Graded as a *theory*: `no-evidence` / `theoretical`. This is **not**
a culturally hostile claim — it is the same standard applied to humoral medicine, miasma theory, or any other
historical system: respect the lineage, grade the biology.

**Layer 2 — some of the *compounds* have real pharmacology.** Traditional pharmacopeias are vast empirical
libraries, and some entries are genuine drug discoveries:
- **Artemisinin** (from *Artemisia annua*, sweet wormwood, a TCM remedy) — **Tu Youyou's Nobel-winning
  antimalarial**, now a frontline WHO therapy. The plant was a real lead; the cure came from **isolating,
  purifying, dosing, and trialling** the molecule — i.e. by doing pharmacology *to* the tradition, not by
  practising the tradition.
- **Ephedra / ma huang** → ephedrine/pseudoephedrine (real bronchodilator/decongestant — and real
  cardiovascular risk, later restricted).
The lesson is consistent: **the win is the molecule, validated by modern method; it is not a vindication of
qi or doshas.** A traditional system being the *source* of a lead compound is provenance, not proof
(SCHEMA hard rule: a name attached to a claim is provenance, grade the primary source).

**The contamination danger (this one is `harmful`, not academic).** Traditional preparations — especially
**Ayurvedic *rasa shastra*** medicines, which *intentionally* combine herbs with metals and minerals — carry
real heavy-metal risk. **Saper et al. (JAMA 2008)** bought Ayurvedic medicines over the internet and found
**~1 in 5 (US- and Indian-made) contained detectable lead, mercury, or arsenic**, with *rasa shastra* products
worse (~40%); metal-containing products exceeded acceptable daily-intake limits. Documented lead-poisoning
clusters have been traced to these products. Some TCM products carry **aristolochic acid** (potent kidney
toxin + urothelial carcinogen) and undeclared pharmaceuticals. Net: traditional systems are a legitimate
source of drug *leads*, an illegitimate theory of the *body*, and — for metal-bearing preparations — a genuine
*toxicology* hazard.

## 5. Chiropractic & manual therapy

**Two different things wear the same coat.** There is (a) **evidence-based spinal manipulative therapy (SMT)**
— a hands-on technique used by chiropractors, osteopaths, and physiotherapists alike — and (b) the
**"subluxation" theory** that founds traditional chiropractic. They must be graded separately.

**SMT for acute low-back pain — `works-for-some-things`.** Cochrane and the large
**Rubinstein et al. systematic reviews/meta-analyses (incl. BMJ 2019)** find SMT produces **modest,
short-term improvements in pain and function for acute and chronic non-specific low-back pain — about
the same as other recommended active therapies** (exercise, NSAIDs, standard physiotherapy, reassurance).
It is a *reasonable option within* guideline care, **not** superior to it. This matches the broader
back-pain story in Section 21: stay active, avoid imaging for non-specific pain, no single passive
modality is magic.

**The "subluxation" critique — `no-evidence`.** Traditional chiropractic (D.D. Palmer, 1895) holds that
"vertebral subluxations" interfere with "Innate Intelligence" flowing through the nerves and **cause systemic
disease** — so adjusting the spine could treat asthma, ear infections, colic, hypertension, etc. The
chiropractic "subluxation" **is not visible on imaging, has no demonstrated pathophysiology, and the claim
that spinal adjustment treats internal/visceral disease is unsupported.** SMT for back pain is real;
"adjustments for your child's ear infections / your blood pressure / your immune system" are `no-evidence`.
Reputable evidence-based chiropractors and physiotherapists have themselves moved away from the subluxation model.

**The neck-manipulation / stroke issue — the serious caveat.** **Cervical (neck) high-velocity manipulation**
is associated with **cervical artery (vertebral/carotid) dissection**, which can cause **stroke, disability,
or death** in previously healthy young adults. The absolute risk per manipulation is **low** (often cited
around the order of ~1 in tens-of-thousands to 1 in millions, and the *causal* fraction is debated — some
dissections may already be in progress and *cause* the neck pain that sends the person to the chiropractor,
the "reverse-causation/Cassidy" argument). But the **consequence is catastrophic and the benefit for neck pain
is modest and replaceable**, so the honest risk-benefit verdict is: **for neck pain, the marginal benefit of
cervical manipulation rarely justifies even a small risk of arterial dissection** when exercise and
mobilisation carry no such risk. Graded **works-for-some-things, with a load-bearing safety caveat**; for the
neck specifically, prefer lower-risk options.

## 6. Mind–body therapies (cross-ref)

This is where the corpus is most positive about "alternative" practices — because the best of them have
**moved into evidence-based medicine** and are covered in depth elsewhere. The grade splits cleanly:

**The real ones (`works`) — see `02-domains/F2-yoga-meditation-lineages.md`, `_F2-SUMMARY.md`, and
`reports/sections/05-recovery-sleep-stress.md`:**
- **Meditation / MBSR (mindfulness-based stress reduction):** moderate real effects on anxiety, depression,
  and pain (meta-analytic, e.g. Goyal JAMA Intern Med 2014). Not a panacea; real.
- **Yoga:** RCT-supported for chronic low-back pain, balance, and general fitness — part "stretch + strength +
  breath," part attention/stress regulation.
- **Tai chi / qigong (as movement):** strong evidence for **balance and fall prevention in older adults** and
  benefits in fibromyalgia/OA — graded for the *movement*, agnostic about *qi*.

**The placebo-level ones (`placebo-level`):**
- **Reiki, Therapeutic Touch, "energy healing," external qigong:** these posit a manipulable human "energy
  field." Sham-controlled trials show **no specific effect beyond placebo**, and the field itself has never
  been demonstrated — most famously, **9-year-old Emily Rosa's JAMA 1998 experiment** showed Therapeutic Touch
  practitioners **could not even detect the "energy field"** they claimed to manipulate (no better than chance).
  Any benefit is relaxation, attention, and kind human contact — real comfort, absent mechanism. Harmless **as
  long as it is alongside, not instead of, real care.**

The dividing line is mechanistic honesty: **mind–body practices that work do so through documented
physiology** (parasympathetic tone, attention, graded movement, stress-axis down-regulation — Sections 5, 14),
**not** through undetectable energy fields.

## 7. Homeopathy — the settled verdict

**Be definitive: this one is closed.** Homeopathy (Hahnemann, ~1796) rests on two axioms — "like cures like"
and "potentisation by serial dilution," where a substance is diluted so extremely that **typical remedies
(30C = a 10⁻⁶⁰ dilution) contain, on average, not a single molecule of the original ingredient.** The claim
that water "remembers" the vanished solute has **no basis in chemistry or physics** (it violates basic
stoichiometry and Avogadro's number) and **no demonstrated mechanism.**

The clinical evidence matches the chemistry:
- **Shang et al. (Lancet 2005)** — large comparative meta-analysis of homeopathy vs conventional medicine
  trials — found homeopathy's effects **consistent with placebo**, shrinking toward null as trial quality rose.
- **Australia's NHMRC (2015)**, after reviewing **176 studies across 61 conditions**, concluded **"there are
  no health conditions for which there is reliable evidence that homeopathy is effective."**
- The **UK House of Commons Science and Technology Committee (2010)** concluded homeopathy is **no better than
  placebo** and that the NHS should stop funding it.

Honest verdict: **`placebo-level`, settled.** Homeopathy is **chemically empty and clinically equivalent to a
sugar pill.** Its specific danger is twofold: (1) **opportunity cost** — it is marketed for serious conditions
(asthma, infections, even as malaria/vaccine "alternatives"), and choosing it *instead of* real treatment kills;
(2) a few "homeopathic" products have been **adulterated or wrongly compounded** (e.g. teething tablets with
variable belladonna), causing real harm. There is no scientific controversy here — only a regulatory and
consumer-protection one.

## 8. Naturopathy & "functional medicine"

The honest grade here is **a genuinely mixed bag**, and conflating the two halves is how both the boosters and
the debunkers get it wrong.

**The good half (`works`).** Naturopathic and functional-medicine practitioners often spend **real time on
diet, exercise, sleep, stress, and smoking/alcohol** — the highest-leverage, most under-delivered
interventions in all of medicine (the foundations of this entire manual). A practitioner who gets a patient to
eat better, move more, sleep, and de-stress is doing evidence-based work, whatever the label on the door. Some
of CAM's appeal is simply that it **supplies the unhurried, whole-person consultation that rushed primary care
often cannot** — a real failure of conventional delivery, not a vindication of magic.

**The unproven half (`no-evidence` → sometimes `harmful`).** The same encounter frequently bundles in:
- **Proprietary/invalidated testing** — IgG "food sensitivity" panels (don't diagnose food intolerance),
  "adrenal fatigue" (not a recognised endocrine entity — see `13-endocrine-hormones.md`), live-blood analysis,
  hair "mineral" analysis, "leaky gut" panels, sprawling micronutrient panels.
- **Unproven treatments** — high-dose IV vitamins, **chelation therapy** for non-poisoning indications (no
  cardiovascular benefit; real risk), "detoxes" and colonics, megadose supplement stacks, prolonged
  antibiotics for "chronic Lyme" (cross-ref `24-disease-neuro-rheum.md`: no durable benefit, real harm),
  bioidentical-hormone "optimisation" outside indications.
- **Manufactured diagnoses** that turn a healthy or anxious person into a lifelong customer.

So: **keep the lifestyle counselling, decline the proprietary tests and treatments.** "Functional medicine"
is an *organisational/marketing* category, not an evidentiary tier — grade each test and therapy it offers on
its own merits, the same as anything else.

## 9. The big honest points

These are the load-bearing conclusions; everything above is detail underneath them.

**9.1 Placebo is real — and bounded.** Expectation, ritual, and meaning produce **measurable** changes in
**subjective, brain-mediated** outcomes — pain (naloxone-reversible endogenous-opioid release, Section 14 §6),
nausea, fatigue, mood, and self-reported function. This is why so much of CAM "works" in the room and in
uncontrolled trials. **But** the landmark **Hróbjartsson & Gøtzsche analysis ("Is the placebo powerless?",
NEJM 2001; updated Cochrane)** found that, against true *no-treatment* controls, placebo has **little
reliable effect on objective/binary outcomes** — it does **not** shrink tumours, clear infection, lower HbA1c,
heal fractures, or reopen arteries. **Placebo modulates the experience of illness, not the pathology.** A
modality whose entire effect is placebo can be a humane comfort *alongside* real care and a lethal substitute
*instead of* it.

@@FIG:R08-placebo-bounded@@

**9.2 "Natural" ≠ safe.** Naturalness predicts nothing about safety, purity, dose, or interactions. Herbs are
unstandardised drugs; "supplements" can be adulterated (§3); traditional metal-bearing preparations can poison
(§4); and the most dangerous interactions (St John's Wort, ginkgo, grapefruit-class effects) come precisely
from "natural" products taken alongside prescriptions.

**9.3 The real killer is *instead-of*, not *alongside*.** This is the single most important number in the
chapter. **Johnson et al. (JNCI 2018)** matched patients who chose **alternative medicine as the *sole*
treatment** for curable breast, prostate, lung, or colorectal cancer: **5-year survival 55% vs 78%**, with a
risk of death **HR 2.50** (hazard ratio — two and a half times the rate of dying) overall and **HR 5.68**
(nearly six times) for breast cancer. The companion paper
**(Johnson et al., JAMA Oncology 2018)** clarifies the mechanism: patients who used **"complementary" medicine
were far more likely to *refuse* conventional treatment (chemo, surgery, radiation, hormone therapy)** — and
**once that refusal was accounted for, the excess mortality disappeared.** Read together: **CAM *alongside*
proven treatment did not itself shorten survival; CAM *instead of* proven treatment more than doubled the
death rate — by causing people to skip or delay the cure.** (Cross-ref oncology, `25-oncology.md`.) The harm of
alternative medicine is overwhelmingly an **opportunity-cost harm**, and it is largest exactly where
conventional medicine works best.

@@FIG:Q08-alt-conventional@@

**9.4 The regulation gap.** In the US, the **Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA, 1994)** lets
supplements, herbs, and homeopathic products reach the market **without pre-market proof of efficacy, purity,
or even safety**; the FDA must prove harm *after* the fact. The result is the contamination, mislabelling, and
overstated-claim landscape documented above. "Sold in a pharmacy" and "has a barcode" are **not** evidence of
testing.

**9.5 Integrative ≠ evidence (and grading *is* the fair treatment).** Co-locating an unproven therapy with a
proven one — "integrative oncology," a hospital acupuncture clinic — is an institutional choice, not an
evidentiary upgrade. The fair, non-dismissive way to treat CAM is **exactly the corpus doctrine**: put it on
the same ladder as everything else and read the grade honestly. Done that way, CAM is neither the conspiracy
its detractors fear nor the suppressed miracle its boosters claim. It is a **patchwork**: a handful of real,
narrow tools (a few herbs, manipulation for acute back pain, mind–body practice, PC6 antiemesis, acupuncture's
modest pain effect); a large middle of **expensive placebo** (homeopathy, energy healing, most of the
supplement aisle); a real toxicology hazard at the edges (heavy metals, hepatotoxic herbs, drug interactions);
and one genuinely dangerous failure mode — **using any of it *instead of* a treatment that works.**
