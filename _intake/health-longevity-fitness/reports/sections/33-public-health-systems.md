# 33 — Public Health, Systems & Access

This is the section the rest of the manual is quietly standing on. Every protocol, supplement, wearable, and
longevity hack indexed elsewhere is a *clinical* or *individual* lever — and clinical/individual levers
account for a **minority** of how long humans actually live. The majority was bought, once, at the population
level: clean water, sewers, vaccines, safe food, refrigeration, smoke-free air, seatbelts. You are not
optimizing in a vacuum. You are optimizing on top of a public-health substrate that someone else built and
that you mostly cannot buy à la carte.

**The one-line verdict up front:** human lifespan roughly **doubled** in two centuries (≈30–40 years in 1800
to ≈73 globally / ≈79 in rich countries today), and the honest accounting credits **sanitation, clean water,
food security, vaccines, and antibiotics** for most of it — **not** high-tech clinical medicine, which earns
maybe **10–20%** of the modern gain. Where you sit in the **social hierarchy** (Marmot) and which **country
and zip code** you were born in outweigh almost every personal choice in this manual. Individual optimization
is real, but it is the *last* and *smallest* tier of the health-impact pyramid — a privilege built on
infrastructure.

**Why read this if you can't pass a clean-water law?** Because it re-sorts your own priorities. The honest
personal hierarchy that falls out of this section: *(a)* be grateful you live atop functioning public health
and *use* it — vaccinate, drink the safe water, wear the seatbelt, the highest-return moves you'll ever make;
*(b)* work the ~40% behavioral bucket seriously (the rest of this manual); *(c)* hold individual optimization
in proportion — valuable, but the smallest tier; *(d)* if you care about life-years per dollar, the most
powerful health philanthropy on Earth is global primary public health, not your supplement stack. §8 is the
full version.

This section holds itself to three honesty rules of its own:

1. **System ≠ self.** The largest health gains in human history were *structural*, not behavioral or
   clinical. A map that indexes 350 individual atoms and zero population infrastructure is not "complete" —
   it is biased toward what an individual can buy.
2. **Predictor ≠ lever, at the system scale too.** That a country with single-payer healthcare lives longer
   does not prove the financing model *caused* it (diet, inequality, guns, cars, and obesity confound every
   cross-country comparison). We grade ecological comparisons as ecological.
3. **Cost-effectiveness is a moral fact, not just an accounting one.** When the marginal dollar buys 100×
   more life in one place than another, "where you spend" is an ethical question the manual is obligated to
   state plainly. The longevity industry spends at the flattest part of the curve.

_Not medical advice. Per-test screening grades live in §07._

## 1. The big picture — what actually doubled human lifespan

### 1.1 The scale of the change

For nearly all of human history, life expectancy at birth sat between **25 and 35 years**, dominated by
staggering infant and child mortality (often **a quarter to a third of children died before age 5**). Then,
beginning in the late 18th–19th century in the industrializing world and spreading globally through the 20th,
it **doubled**. Global life expectancy is now ≈**73 years**; high-income countries ≈**79–84**. This is the
single largest change in the human condition in recorded history, and the popular story — "modern medicine
saved us" — is **mostly wrong**.

@@FIG:81-lifespan-over-time,G06-le-doubling@@

### 1.2 The McKeown thesis and what replaced it

Thomas **McKeown** (1970s) made the provocative observation that the great **decline in mortality from
infectious disease — especially tuberculosis — largely happened *before* effective medical treatments
existed.** TB deaths were collapsing decades before streptomycin (1944) or the BCG vaccine reached scale.
McKeown over-credited *nutrition* specifically, and later historians corrected him — but his core
demolition held: **clinical medicine arrived too late to explain most of the mortality decline.** The
credit went instead to **public health and rising living standards.**

The most rigorous modern quantification is **Cutler & Miller (2005)**, who used the staggered roll-out of
water **filtration and chlorination** across U.S. cities as a natural experiment:

| Outcome (U.S. cities, ~1900–1936) | Share attributable to clean water |
|---|---|
| **Total** mortality reduction | **~half (≈43%)** |
| **Infant** mortality reduction | **~three-quarters (≈74%)** |
| **Child** mortality reduction | **~two-thirds (≈62%)** |

The estimated social rate of return on clean-water technology exceeded **23 to 1**, at roughly **$500 per
life-year saved** (2003 dollars) — an efficiency no drug approaches. Clean water alone — one engineering
intervention — bought a large fraction of the entire first-half-of-century mortality decline. *(Cutler &
Miller, **Demography** 2005; replication debate with Anderson–Charles–Rees does not overturn the
direction, only the exact share.)*

### 1.3 The honest ledger of the doubling

The drivers, in rough order of contribution (all `cohort`/`ecological`/historical-demographic tier —
observational population data, not controlled trials):

| Driver | What it did | Era |
|---|---|---|
| **Clean water & sanitation** (filtration, chlorination, sewers) | Broke fecal-oral disease (typhoid, cholera, dysentery, much infant diarrhea) | 1850s–1930s |
| **Food security & agriculture** | Ended endemic famine and chronic malnutrition; raised resistance to infection | 18th c.–present |
| **Refrigeration & food safety** | Cut foodborne illness, pasteurization killed milk-borne TB/brucellosis | 1900s–1950s |
| **Vaccination** | Eradicated smallpox; near-eradicated polio; crushed measles, diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus | 1796 (smallpox) →present |
| **Antibiotics** | First *clinical* lever with mass effect — pneumonia, sepsis, TB, childbirth fever | 1940s→ |
| **Maternal & infant care, contraception** | Collapsed maternal mortality and high-parity child deaths | 20th c. |
| **Tobacco control, road safety, occupational safety** | The *late*-20th-century chronic-disease and injury gains | 1960s→ |

Note the ordering: the first three are **not medicine at all** — they are civil engineering, agriculture, and
food policy. Vaccination is preventive public health. **Antibiotics are the first item on the list that a
doctor administers to a sick individual** — and they arrive in the 1940s, after most of the decline.

@@FIG:27-lifespan-ledger,L15-lifespan-doubled@@

### 1.4 The epidemiologic transition (Omran 1971)

As infectious disease retreated, the *cause-of-death profile* inverted — Abdel **Omran's "epidemiologic
transition"**: societies move from an **"age of pestilence and famine"** (high, volatile mortality,
infectious deaths, short lives) → **"age of receding pandemics"** → **"age of degenerative and man-made
diseases"** (the chronic-disease world: heart disease, cancer, stroke, diabetes, dementia). This is *the*
framing fact for the entire rest of this manual: **the diseases the longevity industry targets are the
diseases of the post-transition world** — they only became the dominant killers *because* public health
already defeated the things that used to kill us first. Optimizing your VO₂max (your aerobic-fitness ceiling) and apoB (the
blood particle that ferries cholesterol into artery walls) is a luxury of a society that already solved
cholera.

@@FIG:L17-epi-transition,97-leading-causes@@

A blunt corollary: **the highest-leverage health work is largely finished in rich countries and largely
*undone* in poor ones** (§7). The marginal life-year is cheapest where the transition hasn't completed.

## 2. Social determinants of health — the upstream causes

The single most important idea in population health is that **the causes of health are mostly not in the
clinic or the gym — they are in the society.** Income, education, housing, neighborhood, food environment,
job security, and social position predict health and mortality *more strongly than most individual choices*,
and they **cause** many of those choices.

### 2.1 The Marmot gradient (cross-ref Domain M)

The load-bearing evidence is the **Whitehall** studies of British civil servants (Marmot — full treatment in
`02-domains/M-psychosocial-determinants.md §3`). The finding that reorganized the field:

- Health follows a **stepwise social gradient** — each rung *down* the employment hierarchy has higher
  mortality than the rung above. **Whitehall I:** lowest grade had **~3× the coronary mortality** of the
  highest.
- Crucially, **this is not poverty vs. not-poverty.** These were all employed, office-working, insured
  British civil servants. *Mid-grade* administrators — comfortable by any absolute standard — still died at
  higher rates than the grade above them. The gradient runs the **entire length** of the social ladder.
- The proposed mechanism is not just access or behavior but **control, status, and chronic psychosocial
  stress** (autonomy, the allostatic-load bridge — Domain M §4).

The Marmot gradient is one of the most replicated findings in all of epidemiology, and it **dwarfs the effect of
nearly every consumer longevity intervention in this corpus.** The biohacking frame ("optimize *your*
inputs") systematically ignores that **position in the hierarchy is itself a dominant input** — one you
mostly cannot purchase.

### 2.2 The causal stack — upstream beats downstream

Public health distinguishes **"causes"** (the proximate biology: hypertension, smoking, LDL) from **"causes
of the causes"** (the social conditions that produce them). Smoking is a cause of lung cancer; **poverty,
stress, targeted marketing, and low education are causes of smoking.** Intervening downstream (telling people
to quit) is weaker than intervening upstream (tobacco taxes, smoke-free laws, advertising bans — §3.3),
because the upstream lever changes the *distribution of exposure for everyone at once* and requires no
individual willpower.

| Upstream determinant | Health pathway | Why it often beats "personal choice" |
|---|---|---|
| **Income / wealth** | Buys time, food quality, safe housing, low stress, care access | The strongest single SES (socioeconomic status) correlate of mortality; gradient is continuous |
| **Education** | Health literacy, employment, agency, delayed childbearing | Each additional year of schooling associates with measurable mortality reduction |
| **Housing** | Cold/damp → respiratory/CVD; crowding → infection; stability → chronic-disease control | Cold-home mortality is real (cross-ref §09.6, ambient temperature) |
| **Food environment** | "Food deserts/swamps" make the healthy choice the expensive, distant one | Individual diet advice fails against a structurally bad default |
| **Neighborhood** | Air quality, green space, walkability, violence, social cohesion | Zip code predicts life expectancy better than genetic code |

A widely-cited U.S. illustration: **life expectancy can differ by 20–30 years between neighborhoods a few
subway stops apart** (e.g., within Baltimore, Chicago, London) — same country, same medical system, same era.
The variable that moved is *social*, not clinical.

### 2.3 Health inequality is the signature, not a footnote

Because the determinants are graded, **health is graded** — and the gradient is the cleanest evidence that
the system, not the individual, is the dominant actor. This is the uncomfortable policy implication flagged
in Domain M's gaps: **the biggest longevity lever (social position) is structural, not personal**, which is
exactly why a market that sells *personal* optimization under-weights it. There is no SKU for "be born richer."

## 3. Prevention — levels, paradox, and the cheapest life-years on Earth

### 3.1 The three levels of prevention

| Level | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Stop disease *before it starts* (remove exposure / raise resistance) | Vaccination, clean water, tobacco tax, seatbelts, fluoridation, folate fortification |
| **Secondary** | Catch disease *early*, while asymptomatic and treatable | Screening (BP, cervical, colorectal — cross-ref §07), early detection |
| **Tertiary** | Limit damage / disability *after* disease is established | Cardiac rehab, diabetic foot care, stroke rehab, secondary-prevention statins |

Leverage **decreases as you move down the list.** Primary prevention is cheapest and reaches the most people;
tertiary is most expensive per life-year and reaches the fewest. The medical system is structured almost
entirely around the *bottom two* (it is paid to treat, not to prevent), which is part of why high-spending
systems buy so little marginal longevity (§4).

### 3.2 Rose's prevention paradox & the population vs. high-risk strategy

Geoffrey **Rose's** *"Sick Individuals and Sick Populations"* (1985) is the most important conceptual tool in
this section. Rose drew the distinction between two strategies:

- **High-risk strategy:** find the people at greatest risk and treat them (the clinical/medical model —
  screen for the hypertensive, statin the high-LDL patient). Intuitive, well-targeted, *but* it leaves the
  bulk of cases untouched.
- **Population strategy:** shift the *whole distribution* of risk a little — lower everyone's blood pressure
  or salt intake by a small amount.

The **prevention paradox**: *"a large number of people exposed to a small risk may generate more cases than a
small number exposed to a high risk."* Most heart attacks happen to people with only *moderately* elevated
risk — because there are *so many* of them — not to the small tail of extreme-risk individuals. Therefore a
tiny shift across the whole population (a 2-mmHg drop in mean BP, a 1 g/day cut in salt) prevents **more total
events** than aggressively treating the high-risk tail.

The paradox's sting: a population measure offers **large benefit to the community but little perceptible
benefit to any one participant** (most people who wear a seatbelt, eat less salt, or get fluoridated water
gain nothing personally — they were never going to be the case prevented). This is why population prevention
is chronically *unpopular and under-funded* despite being *more effective and cheaper* — it is invisible at
the individual level, exactly the level the longevity market operates at. **Tier: `theoretical`/foundational
framework, validated repeatedly in CVD epidemiology.**

@@FIG:G05-rose@@

### 3.3 The Frieden Health Impact Pyramid — leverage, formalized

Thomas **Frieden** (then CDC director) formalized the same insight as a **5-tier pyramid** (AJPH 2010), where
*lower tiers reach more people, require less individual effort, and have larger population impact*:

| Tier (top → bottom) | Type | Effort required | Population impact | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 (apex) | **Counseling & education** | Highest individual effort | **Smallest** | "Eat better, exercise" advice |
| 4 | **Clinical interventions** | High | Small–moderate | Treating hypertension, statins |
| 3 | **Long-lasting protective interventions** | One-time | Moderate | Vaccination, colonoscopy, smoking-cessation Rx |
| 2 | **Changing the context** (healthy default) | Low/none | Large | Trans-fat bans, fluoridation, clean air, iodized salt |
| 1 (base) | **Socioeconomic factors** | None (structural) | **Largest** | Poverty reduction, education, housing |

@@FIG:N05-health-pyramid@@

**One legend the rest of this section leans on.** Three different vocabularies all point the same way — cheap,
broad, and invisible at the bottom; expensive, narrow, and effortful at the top:

- **Frieden tiers** — *tier 1–2* = the structural, context-changing base (poverty, housing, clean air,
  trans-fat bans); *tier 4–5* = clinical treatment and "eat better" advice at the weak apex.
- **Rose strategy** — *population* = shift everyone a little; *high-risk* = find and treat the worst cases.
- **Prevention level** — *primary* = stop disease before it starts; *secondary* = catch it early; *tertiary*
  = limit damage after.

When later sections say "tier-1–2, population, primary," they mean the same corner of every one of these
maps: the boring base that does the heavy lifting.

The brutal punchline for this manual: **almost everything the longevity industry sells lives at the apex
(tier 5) — the tier with the *least* population impact and the *most* required individual effort.** Supplements,
protocols, coaching, and "education" are tier-5 levers. The things that actually doubled lifespan are tiers
1–2. This is not a moral failing of individuals; it is a structural fact about where leverage lives.

### 3.4 The cheapest life-years ever bought

The most cost-effective health interventions in history are almost all **primary, population-level, and
boring** — none of them are sold to you. (Cost is shown per **DALY** — disability-adjusted life year, one
year of healthy life lost; **LMICs** = low- and middle-income countries. Evidence codes: `rct` = randomized
trial, the strongest tier; `meta` = pooled analysis of many trials.)

| Intervention | Mechanism | Cost-effectiveness | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tobacco taxation** | Price ↑ → consumption ↓ (esp. youth & poor) | WHO **"best buy"**; ~US$20–75 / DALY in LMICs | Strong; the single most effective tobacco lever |
| **Salt/sodium reduction** (reformulation, labeling) | Pop. BP shift → fewer strokes/MIs (Rose strategy) | WHO **"best buy"**; ~US$150 / DALY or less | `meta`/modeling; reformulation > advice |
| **Vaccination** (childhood EPI, etc.) | Primary prevention of infectious death | Among the highest-ROI health spending known | `rct`/`meta`; smallpox eradication, polio |
| **Water fluoridation** | Community caries prevention | ~US$1 spent saves ~US$20–50 dental cost; CDC "10 great achievements" | `cohort`/community; large effect, low cost |
| **Seatbelts / road safety** (laws + design) | Injury primary prevention | Massive; among the cheapest life-years in transport | `cohort`/natural-experiment; ~45% fatal-injury reduction |
| **Trans-fat elimination** | Removes a uniquely atherogenic fat from supply | High; whole-population CVD reduction at ~$0 individual effort | `cohort`/policy (Denmark, NYC, then national bans) |

@@FIG:G03-cost-per-ly@@

Every one of these is a **tier-1 or tier-2** Frieden lever, a **population** Rose strategy, and **primary**
prevention. Every one is invisible to the person it protects. None has a Goop SKU. That is the entire thesis
of this section in one table.

## 4. Health systems — models, money, and the honest outcome data

### 4.1 The four archetypal financing models

Every national system is a mix of four pure types (Reid's taxonomy):

| Model | In plain terms | Financing | Provision | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Beveridge** | Tax-funded, government-run care | Govt (taxes) | Mostly public | UK NHS, Spain, Nordics, Cuba |
| **Bismarck** | Insurance funds employers and workers pay into | Mandatory social insurance (employer+employee), non-profit funds | Mostly private | Germany, France, Japan, Netherlands, Switzerland |
| **National Health Insurance** (single-payer) | One govt insurer, private doctors | Govt single insurer | Private providers | Canada, Taiwan, South Korea |
| **Out-of-pocket** | You pay directly, or go without | Patient pays | Whatever you can afford | Much of the low-income world; the uninsured *inside* rich ones |

The **United States is uniquely a mix of all four at once**, sorted by demographic: Beveridge (VA), Bismarck
(employer insurance), NHI (Medicare), and out-of-pocket (the uninsured). This fragmentation is the source of
both its administrative cost and its inequity.

### 4.2 The US-vs-peers data, told without flinching

This is the cleanest natural experiment in health-system performance, and the result is unambiguous. The
**Commonwealth Fund's "Mirror, Mirror 2024"** compared 10 wealthy countries across 70 measures:

- The U.S. ranked **10th of 10 (last) overall** — and *last in health outcomes, access, equity*. (Australia,
  Netherlands, UK led.)
- The U.S. **spent >16% of GDP** on health (≈US$13,000+ per capita, ~**double** the OECD average and headed
  for >20% by 2035) — while peers spent **8–12%**.
- Americans have **the shortest lives and the most avoidable deaths** of the ten.

@@FIG:L16-us-vs-peers@@

The U.S. is the definitive proof of this section's thesis: **it spends the most money on the most advanced
clinical medicine on Earth and gets the worst outcomes among rich nations** — because clinical spending is
the *flat part* of the health-production curve, and because it under-invests in the upstream tiers (1–2) and
leaves tens of millions without access. **Tier: ecological/cross-national (confounded by diet, inequality,
firearms, vehicles, obesity — graded as ecological, not causal).** The confounders matter, but they cut
*toward* the thesis: much of the U.S. shortfall is **social and behavioral**, i.e., *not* fixable by buying
more clinical medicine.

### 4.3 Access and the uninsured reality

Coverage is not a technicality — it is a mortality variable. In the U.S., roughly **25–27 million people (~8%)
remain uninsured** even post-ACA, and tens of millions more are *under*-insured (high deductibles that deter
care). Studies (IOM; the Oregon Medicaid natural experiment) link uninsurance to delayed care, worse chronic-
disease control, financial catastrophe (medical debt is a leading bankruptcy cause), and excess deaths.
Universal-coverage systems eliminate this failure mode by design. The honest nuance: **coverage buys access,
access buys the ~10–20% that clinical care contributes — it does not buy the other 80%** (§4.4). Coverage is
necessary and morally required; it is not, by itself, the dominant determinant of population longevity.

### 4.4 What "good healthcare" actually contributes to longevity

The most consequential number in this section. Multiple independent estimation methods converge:

- **Bunker** (the foundational estimate): of the **~30 years** of life expectancy gained in the 20th century,
  clinical medical care explains roughly **5 years (~⅙, ~15–18%)**; of gains *after 1950*, about **3 of 7.5
  years** — roughly **40%** of that later, smaller slice. The big early gains were public-health; medicine's share
  rose only once the easy structural wins were banked.
- The canonical **determinants-of-health allocation** (McGinnis/Williams-Russo/Schroeder, *Health Affairs*
  2002 / NEJM 2007), used by County Health Rankings — the five shares are in the figure below.

So **medical care ≈ 10–20%** of what determines how long and well a population lives; **behavior +
social + environment ≈ 60%+**, and genetics ~30%. *(Tier: `theoretical`/synthesis — these are
order-of-magnitude allocations, contested at the margins, not precise causal partitions; the **direction**
holds across every method.)* The practical reading for an individual: *seeing a good doctor matters, but it
is the smallest of the modifiable buckets* — and for a *society*, pouring money into clinical care while
ignoring tiers 1–2 is buying the smallest slice at the highest price. This is the U.S. story in one sentence.

## 5. Screening policy — the population-level honesty (cross-ref §07)

Screening is *secondary* prevention, and it is the place where individual intuition ("catch it early, more
testing is better") collides hardest with population evidence. The full graded screening tables live in
**§07 (Clinical & Preventive Medicine)**; the *population-policy* honesty belongs here:

- **A screen only earns its place if it lowers *disease-specific mortality* in an RCT** (randomized
  controlled trial — the strongest evidence tier) — not if it "finds more cancers." Finding more disease can
  *harm* a population.
- **Lead-time bias:** earlier detection moves the diagnosis date earlier without changing the death date,
  manufacturing *apparent* survival gains (5-year survival rises) with **zero** real benefit. Survival
  statistics are systematically misleading for screening; **mortality** is the only honest endpoint.
- **Length-time / overdiagnosis bias:** screening preferentially catches **slow, indolent** disease (it sits
  detectable longer), including cancers that **would never have caused symptoms.** Treating them is **pure
  harm** — surgery, radiation, anxiety, cost, with no life extended. Prostate (PSA), thyroid, and a fraction
  of breast (mammography) and lung screens carry real overdiagnosis (see §07 §3.2–3.3).
- **The Rose corollary applies:** mass-screening a low-risk population generates mostly false positives and
  overdiagnosis (low prior → poor positive predictive value); screening the *right high-risk* group is where
  the mortality benefit concentrates. "Screen everyone for everything" is the secondary-prevention version of
  the high-risk/population confusion.

The policy stance the manual endorses: **a small number of screens with RCT mortality benefit (cervical,
colorectal, high-risk lung LDCT [low-dose CT], targeted breast) — graded by USPSTF letter (the US Preventive
Services Task Force's A–D grades) — and skepticism toward the rest.** Defer to §07's tables for the per-test grades. **Tier: `meta`/RCT for the benefiting screens;
`cohort`/modeling for the bias quantification.**

## 6. Digital health & AI in medicine — the honest state

Digital health is where this manual's *future* tier-3/4 leverage may sit — and where hype most outruns
outcome data. Graded honestly:

### 6.1 Telemedicine

- **What's real:** telehealth massively expanded (forced by COVID-19), and for **follow-up, mental health,
  chronic-disease check-ins, dermatology triage, and access in rural/under-served areas** it delivers
  comparable outcomes with lower cost and friction. It is a genuine **access** lever — which, per §2,
  matters most for the people the system currently fails.
- **What's not:** it does not replace exams/procedures, can *widen* the digital divide (the elderly, poor,
  and non-English-speaking are least able to use it — the very groups with worst access), and the
  hard-outcome (mortality) evidence is thin. **Tier: `cohort`/health-services; strong for access &
  satisfaction, weak for hard endpoints.**
- **For you:** use telehealth for follow-ups, refills, mental-health, and rural access; push for an in-person
  visit the moment something needs to be examined or felt.

### 6.2 AI diagnostics — the actual evidence

The honest state, neither dismissive nor breathless:

- **Narrow, image-based tasks are real and sometimes regulator-grade.** **Diabetic retinopathy** screening
  (e.g., IDx-DR / LumineticsCore) was the **first FDA-authorized autonomous AI diagnostic** (2018). AI for
  **mammography, chest X-ray/CT, dermatology, and pathology** matches or beats average readers on *curated
  benchmark sets*.
- **The gaps are systematic and load-bearing:** (1) **distribution shift** — models degrade on populations,
  scanners, and sites unlike their training data; (2) **dataset bias / shortcut learning** — models latch
  onto hospital-specific artifacts, not biology; (3) **benchmark ≠ deployment** — retrospective AUC rarely
  survives prospective real-world trials; (4) **few RCTs show improved *patient outcomes*** as opposed to
  improved *accuracy metrics*. The field has thousands of "AI matches doctors" papers and a **handful** of
  trials proving patients are actually better off.
- **The realistic near-term role is augmentation, triage, and access-extension** (reading where there is no
  radiologist), not autonomous replacement. **Tier: `rct` for a few narrow tasks (DR screening); mostly
  `retrospective`/benchmark — grade the deployment evidence, not the press release.** (Same rule as the rest
  of the manual: *accuracy on a benchmark is a `mechanistic`-equivalent claim; improved outcomes is the
  `outcome` claim, and most hype lives in that gap.*)
- **For you:** an AI-flagged scan is a second reader, not a verdict — it should prompt a clinician to look
  again, not replace one.

### 6.3 Wearables at population scale

- **What's real:** consumer wearables (Apple Watch, etc.) can detect **atrial fibrillation**, and large
  studies (Apple Heart Study, ~419k participants) show population-scale screening is *feasible* — it finds
  AF. Wearables are a powerful **behavioral nudge** (step counts, sleep awareness) and a research data
  firehose. (Deep treatment in **Domain L2**, wearables/quantified-self.)
- **What's not established:** that mass wearable screening **lowers stroke or mortality** at the population
  level (open USPSTF question — cross-ref §07 §2); that consumer-grade metrics (HRV — heart-rate variability —
  "readiness," sleep stages) are accurate enough to act on; that they help the people who need help most
  (wearables skew wealthy, healthy, and worried-well — a **Rose paradox in reverse**: they reach exactly the
  low-risk population least likely to benefit). **Tier: `cohort` for AF detection; `mechanistic`/surrogate
  for most consumer metrics.**
- **For you:** treat your watch's AFib alert as a reason to see a doctor, not a diagnosis; don't over-trust
  the HRV/"readiness" scores.

### 6.4 The data-privacy reality

Health data is the most sensitive data class and the **least protected once it leaves the clinic.** HIPAA
covers *covered entities* (providers, insurers) — it **does not** cover most consumer apps, wearables, wellness
sites, or data brokers. Period-tracking, mental-health, and fitness apps have repeatedly been caught selling
or leaking data; de-identified health data is routinely **re-identifiable**; and the AI models above are
trained on data whose consent provenance is often murky. The honest framing: **the digital-health convenience
you adopt as an individual is also a population-scale surveillance surface**, and the regulatory floor is far
below what users assume. This is a *systems* risk, not a personal-optimization feature.

**For you:** assume anything you type into a period-, mood-, or fitness-app can be sold, leaked, or
re-identified — HIPAA does not cover it.

## 7. Global health — where the marginal dollar saves the most life

Zoom out from the rich world and the entire calculus inverts. This is the section's moral core.

### 7.1 The rich–poor gap

The **WHO (2025 World Report on Social Determinants of Health Equity)** quantified it: a **~33-year gap in
life expectancy** between the highest- and lowest-life-expectancy countries. Children in poor countries are
**~13× more likely to die before age 5**; **94% of maternal deaths** occur in low/lower-middle-income
countries. *Where you are born* is a larger health input than nearly any choice in this manual — the global
version of the zip-code finding (§2.2). **Tier: `ecological`/WHO global-burden — descriptive, robust.**

### 7.2 The double burden of disease

Low- and middle-income countries increasingly carry **both** burdens at once: the **unfinished infectious
agenda** (childhood diarrhea, pneumonia, malaria, TB, HIV, maternal/neonatal deaths) **and** a rising tide of
**chronic disease** (the epidemiologic transition arriving *before* they finished the first stage, often
fueled by exported tobacco, ultra-processed food, and urban air pollution — cross-ref §09.3). They get the
worst of both eras simultaneously, with a fraction of the health spending. This is why "longevity" looks
completely different from Lagos than from Los Angeles: in one place the lever is a bednet; in the other, an
ApoB.

### 7.3 Pandemic preparedness (cross-ref §26)

COVID-19 was the demonstration that **infectious disease never left — it was suppressed by infrastructure
that can be overwhelmed.** Pandemic preparedness (surveillance, sequencing, surge capacity, vaccine platforms
like mRNA, equitable distribution) is a **tier-2 population lever** with enormous expected value and chronic
under-funding — the textbook *Rose paradox at civilizational scale* (huge community benefit, no perceptible
individual benefit in non-pandemic years, so it gets cut). Detailed treatment in **§26 (Infectious
Disease)**; flagged here as a systems priority, not an individual one.

### 7.4 Where the marginal dollar saves the most life

The most uncomfortable and most important fact in the manual: **the cost per life saved varies by 100–1,000×
across interventions and geographies.** The cheapest life-years on Earth (per GiveWell / DCP3 / WHO-CHOICE
analyses) are overwhelmingly in **global primary public health**:

| Intervention | Setting | Rough cost per life saved / DALY | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Insecticide-treated bednets** (malaria) | Sub-Saharan Africa | ~US$3,000–5,500 / life | `rct`/`meta` |
| **Childhood vaccination** (EPI, measles, etc.) | LMICs | Among highest-ROI health spending known | `rct`/`meta` |
| **Vitamin A supplementation, ORS, deworming** | LMICs | Pennies–dollars per child-year protected | `rct`/`meta` |
| **Oral rehydration therapy** | Diarrheal disease | ~cents per treatment; saved millions of children | `meta` |
| — vs. — | | | |
| **Marginal high-tech clinical care** | Rich world | US$100,000s+ per QALY (quality-adjusted life year — one year in full health) for end-of-line interventions | `rct` |

A dollar spent on bednets or measles vaccine buys **orders of magnitude more life** than a dollar spent on the
last increment of rich-world clinical medicine — or on any supplement in this manual. **This is a graded,
quantitative fact, not an opinion.** It is also the clearest possible statement that *the longevity industry
spends at the flattest, most expensive end of the global health-production curve.*

## 8. The honest framing — individual optimization is a privilege built on infrastructure

This is the section the whole manual needs to read against itself.

1. **Your optimization sits on a substrate you didn't build and can't buy.** Clean water, sewers, safe food,
   childhood vaccines, smoke-free air, road safety, antibiotics on the shelf — these are the reason you live
   long enough for apoB, VO₂max, and rapamycin to be *relevant problems.* They sit at the structural base of
   every framework in §3 — tier-1–2, population strategy, primary prevention — and they are *already paid
   for* in your society. Remove them and every protocol in this corpus is noise.

2. **"Personal responsibility" is a true but small lever, oversold because it's sellable.** Behavior is the
   ~40% bucket — real and worth working — but it is *itself heavily shaped by upstream conditions* (food
   environment, stress, income, education). Telling a person in a food desert with two jobs and no sidewalks
   to "just eat better and walk more" is asking a tier-5 lever to do a tier-1 job. The data say it mostly
   won't. The honest position is **both/and**: do the individual work *and* recognize that the system sets
   the odds.

3. **The biggest longevity levers are not for sale to you.** You cannot purchase a higher rung on the Marmot
   gradient, a cleaner national airshed, a better food supply, or a more equal society — those are *political
   and structural* goods, won collectively or not at all. The market routes attention to the *purchasable*
   levers precisely because they're purchasable, which systematically biases the "longevity" conversation
   toward its least powerful tier. (Same market-gap logic as Domain M: there is no SKU for friendship, job
   autonomy, clean water, or being born in a rich country.)

4. **For the individual reader, the honest hierarchy is:** *(a)* be grateful you live atop functioning public
   health and use it (vaccinate, drink the safe water, wear the seatbelt — the highest-ROI moves you'll ever
   make are the boring infrastructural ones you barely notice); *(b)* work the ~40% behavioral bucket
   seriously (the rest of this manual); *(c)* hold individual optimization in proportion — it is the last and
   smallest tier, valuable but not where the leverage lives; *(d)* if you care about life-years *per dollar*,
   the most powerful health philanthropy on Earth is global primary public health, not your supplement stack.

The deepest claim of this section, stated plainly: **the largest health gains in human history were public
goods, not private purchases — and they still are.** A health manual that only indexes what an individual can
buy is not a complete map. This section is the part of the map that says *you are standing on something.*

## Go deeper — primary sources

1. **Marmot MG, et al. (Whitehall I & II).** *"Health inequalities among British civil servants"* and the
   later cohort — `10.1136/jech.32.4.244` (1978) and *Lancet* 1991 `10.1016/0140-6736(91)93068-K`. The social
   gradient that founded modern social epidemiology. See also Marmot's **WHO Commission on Social
   Determinants of Health, *Closing the Gap in a Generation* (2008)** and **"Fair Society, Healthy Lives"
   (The Marmot Review, 2010)** — the policy translation. *(Cross-ref Domain M §3.)*
2. **Rose G. (1985).** *"Sick Individuals and Sick Populations."* *International Journal of Epidemiology*
   `10.1093/ije/14.1.32` (reprinted with commentary 2001, `10.1093/ije/30.3.427`); expanded in **Rose, *The
   Strategy of Preventive Medicine* (1992).** The prevention paradox and the population-vs-high-risk
   distinction — read this first on *why* population prevention beats clinical prevention.
3. **Frieden TR. (2010).** *"A Framework for Public Health Action: The Health Impact Pyramid."* *AJPH*
   `10.2105/AJPH.2009.185652`. The five-tier leverage hierarchy that places "education/counseling" at the
   weak apex and "socioeconomic factors" at the powerful base.
4. **Cutler D, Miller G. (2005).** *"The Role of Public Health Improvements in Health Advances: The
   Twentieth-Century United States."* *Demography* `10.1353/dem.2005.0002`. The clean-water natural
   experiment — ~half of total and ~three-quarters of infant mortality decline.
5. **Bunker JP. (2001).** *Medicine Matters After All*, and **Bunker, Frazier & Mosteller (1994)**,
   *Milbank Q* — the foundational estimate that medical care explains only ~5 of the 30 life-years gained.
   Pair with **McGinnis JM, Williams-Russo P, Knickman JR (2002),** *"The case for more active policy
   attention to health promotion,"* *Health Affairs* `10.1377/hlthaff.21.2.78`, and **Schroeder SA (2007),**
   *"We Can Do Better,"* *NEJM* `10.1056/NEJMsa073350` — the 40/30/15/10/5 determinants allocation.
6. **Omran AR. (1971).** *"The Epidemiologic Transition."* *Milbank Memorial Fund Q* `10.2307/3349375`. The
   infectious→chronic shift that frames the entire chronic-disease longevity project.
7. **Commonwealth Fund (2024).** *"Mirror, Mirror 2024: A Portrait of the Failing U.S. Health System."* The
   10-country comparison: U.S. last overall, last in outcomes, highest spending. (commonwealthfund.org)
8. **Our World in Data — Life Expectancy & "Why did life expectancy improve?"** (Roser, Ortiz-Ospina, Ritchie)
   and **WHO *World Report on Social Determinants of Health Equity* (2025)**. The most accessible, well-sourced
   visual histories of the doubling and the ~33-year rich–poor gap; start here for the big picture before the
   primary papers.
9. **WHO (2017/2023).** *"'Best Buys' and other recommended interventions for the prevention and control of
   NCDs."* The cost-per-DALY table behind §3.4 (tobacco tax, salt reduction as the cheapest population levers).
10. **McKeown T. (1976).** *The Role of Medicine: Dream, Mirage or Nemesis?* The original (over-stated but
    field-founding) argument that medicine arrived too late to explain the mortality decline — read with its
    modern corrections (Szreter on the role of *public health activism*, not just nutrition).
