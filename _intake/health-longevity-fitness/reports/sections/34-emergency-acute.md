# 34 — Emergency, Acute Care & First Aid

Every other section of this manual is about the long game — how to live well across decades. This one is
about the next ten minutes. A complete map of human health has to include **what kills acutely and what an
ordinary person can actually do about it.** Most premature deaths in the developed world are slow
(cardiometabolic disease, cancer — Sections 22, 25). But a meaningful fraction are **fast and reversible**:
a clot that stops a heart, a peanut that closes an airway, a bleed that empties a body, an opioid that
stops a breath. In those minutes the most decisive medical intervention on Earth is **not** a hospital — it
is whoever happens to be standing next to the person. This section is about being that person competently.

> ## ⚠️ READ THIS BEFORE ANYTHING ELSE — what this section is and is not
>
> **This is health education, not training and not medical advice.** Reading about CPR is to *doing* CPR what
> reading about swimming is to swimming. The single most useful action you can take after reading this is to
> **book a certified, in-person, hands-on course** — Red Cross / American Heart Association / Resuscitation
> Council (UK) / your national equivalent — where you compress a real manikin, handle a real (training) AED,
> and practice an EpiPen trainer. Skills decay; refresh every ~2 years.
>
> **Three rules that override everything below:**
> 1. **CALL EMERGENCY SERVICES FIRST (or simultaneously).** 911 (US/Canada), 112 (EU & most of the world,
>    works from any mobile even with no SIM/signal), 999 (UK), 000 (Australia). Put the phone on **speaker** —
>    modern dispatchers talk you through CPR, choking, childbirth, naloxone, bleeding control in real time.
>    The dispatcher is part of your team, not a delay.
> 2. **Scene safety first — you are no use as a second casualty.** Traffic, fire, electricity, gas, water,
>    violence, fumes. A drowning rescuer who drowns and a bystander electrocuted on the same downed wire are
>    the classic textbook deaths. Look before you leap.
> 3. **Doing *something* competent beats doing nothing, and doing nothing is the common failure.** The
>    dominant problem in cardiac arrest, anaphylaxis, severe bleeding, and opioid overdose is **bystander
>    paralysis**, not bystander error. The legal cover for this in most jurisdictions is the **Good Samaritan**
>    framework: an ordinary person acting in good faith, within their training, without gross negligence, is
>    broadly protected. Fear of lawsuits is a poor reason to let someone die.

**Cross-references (read alongside):** **heart attack** mechanism, atherosclerosis, and the long-game
prevention live in §22 §1.1; **stroke** (the "time is brain" reperfusion revolution,

## 1. The mental model — the chain only works if the first link does

Acute medicine has a small number of organizing ideas. Internalize these and most of the specifics follow.

- **The body has a few non-negotiable supply lines** — a patent **airway**, **breathing** to oxygenate the
  blood, and **circulation** to deliver it (the classic **A-B-C**). Modern lay rescue for collapse reorders
  this to **C-A-B** (circulation first) because the commonest reversible adult catastrophe is a heart that
  has stopped pumping while the blood is still full of oxygen — so chest compressions, not rescue breaths,
  are the first move. Add **D** (disability/neuro — stroke, seizure, head injury) and **E** (exposure/
  environment — heat, cold, poison).
- **Hypoxia time is short.** The brain begins to suffer irreversible injury after roughly **4–6 minutes**
  without oxygenated blood flow. Professional help, in most of the world, takes **longer than that** to
  arrive. That gap — the first 5–10 minutes — is the whole reason laypeople matter. You are not a substitute
  for the ambulance; you are the bridge that keeps the patient *survivable* until it arrives.
- **"Recognize → Act" beats "diagnose → treat."** You are not being asked to make a diagnosis. You are being
  asked to recognize a small set of patterns ("this looks like a stroke," "this looks like anaphylaxis") and
  trigger a small set of actions. Precision is the hospital's job; **speed and pattern-recognition** are
  yours.

## 2. Cardiac arrest & the Chain of Survival — the single biggest lever a bystander holds

**Cardiac arrest is not a heart attack.** A *heart attack* (myocardial infarction, §22 §1.1) is a plumbing
problem — a blocked coronary artery starving heart muscle; the person is usually awake and talking. A *cardiac
arrest* is an electrical problem — the heart's pumping stops, the person **collapses, is unresponsive, and
isn't breathing normally.** A heart attack can *cause* an arrest. The acute action is completely different:
heart attack → aspirin + 911 + rest; arrest → **CPR + AED now.**

Survival from out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is the canonical "the system is the patient" story. The
**Chain of Survival** (an AHA/ILCOR framework) is only as strong as its first links, and **those first links
are laypeople**:

1. **Early recognition & call for help** (you)
2. **Early CPR** (you)
3. **Early defibrillation** (you + an AED)
4. Advanced care by EMS
5. Post-arrest hospital care & recovery

### 2.1 Hands-only CPR — the evidence it works, and that it's easier

For an adult who collapses suddenly, **compression-only ("hands-only") CPR by a bystander is recommended as
the default** — and the evidence is unusually clean:

- **The SOS-KANTO observational study (Lancet 2007)** and subsequent trials found compression-only bystander
  CPR was **at least as good as** conventional CPR-with-breaths for adult witnessed arrest — and far more
  people are willing to do it (no mouth-to-mouth barrier).[^sos-kanto]
- **Bystander CPR is associated with roughly double to triple the survival rate in registry studies.** A nationwide Swedish study (**Hasselqvist-Ax et al.,
  NEJM 2015**)[^hasselqvist] of ~30,000 arrests found
  30-day survival of **10.5% with bystander CPR vs 4.0% without** — and that early CPR was associated with
  survival *independent* of all later links. The intervention with the largest population effect is the one
  any untrained person can deliver.

**How (adult, untrained or rusty — the version worth memorizing):**
> **Push hard, push fast, in the center of the chest. ~100–120 compressions per minute, ~2 inches (5–6 cm)
> deep, let the chest fully recoil between each. Don't stop except to use an AED.** Hum *Stayin' Alive* or
> *Baby Shark* — both are ~100–110 bpm. Switch with another rescuer every ~2 minutes (it's exhausting; poor
> compressions are the silent failure mode).

@@FIG:11-cpr-card@@

For **children, infants, drowning, opioid overdose, and any asphyxial cause**, oxygen has run down, so
**rescue breaths add real value** — if trained, use 30 compressions : 2 breaths. If not trained, **hands-only
is still vastly better than nothing.** This is a course-skill; book the course.

### 2.2 AEDs — early defibrillation is the other half of the lever

Most survivable sudden adult arrests start as **ventricular fibrillation** — a quivering, non-pumping heart.
The only thing that fixes VF is an **electric shock (defibrillation)**, and **every minute without it drops
survival by roughly 7–10%.** An **Automated External Defibrillator (AED)** is built for panicked laypeople:
turn it on, it *talks you through it*, sticks pads where the picture shows, and **it will not shock unless it
detects a shockable rhythm** — you cannot accidentally hurt someone with it.

- **The PAD Trial (Hallstrom et al., NEJM 2004)**[^pad] showed that training community
  volunteers to use **public-access AEDs roughly doubled survivors** of OHCA versus CPR alone.
- Practical rules: **send someone to get the AED while you start compressions** (don't stop CPR to go look);
  map the AEDs near where you live/work (many countries have apps/registries); pads go upper-right chest and
  lower-left side; **resume compressions immediately after a shock.** Wet chest → wipe dry; lots of hair →
  the kit usually has a razor; medication patch → remove it.

### 2.3 The honest survival statistics

Be clear-eyed: **OHCA survival to hospital discharge is only about 8–12% overall**, and even with everything
done right it is not the TV-CPR ~70%. Why say so? Because **(a)** it makes the few high-leverage links —
bystander CPR + early AED, which can lift *witnessed shockable* arrest survival above 30–40% — worth fighting
for, and **(b)** it sets honest expectations: bystanders who do excellent CPR on someone who still dies did
**not** fail. They gave the only chance there was. The failure is the arrest no one touched.

## 3. The time-critical recognitions

### 3.1 Heart attack — including the atypical and female presentations

The "Hollywood heart attack" (clutching the chest, dropping) is real but partial. The honest picture
(cross-ref §22 §1.1):

- **Classic:** central chest **pressure / tightness / squeezing / heaviness** (often described as *not* sharp),
  lasting more than a few minutes or coming and going, often radiating to the **left arm, both arms, jaw,
  neck, or back**, with **cold sweat, nausea, shortness of breath, a sense of doom.**
- **Atypical — and dangerously easy to dismiss** (more common in **women, the elderly, and people with
  diabetes**, whose neuropathy blunts pain): **unusual fatigue, breathlessness, indigestion/"heartburn,"
  jaw or back pain, lightheadedness, nausea** — sometimes with **little or no chest pain at all.** Women are
  more likely to present this way and, partly because of it, are more likely to be under-triaged and to
  delay. **When in doubt, treat it as cardiac.**
- **Act:** call 911 (an ambulance can treat an arrest *en route* — never drive yourself or be driven), rest,
  and **chew (don't swallow whole) 162–325 mg of aspirin** if available, conscious, not allergic, and not
  actively bleeding — chewing speeds antiplatelet action. Don't take a stranger's nitroglycerin.

### 3.2 Stroke — "time is brain" (owned by §24 §3)

A large-vessel stroke destroys on the order of **~1.9 million neurons per minute** (`24 §3`). Recognition is
**BE-FAST** — **B**alance loss, **E**yes (sudden vision loss/double vision), **F**ace droop, **A**rm drift,
**S**peech slurred. The decisive letter is **T**ime: **note the exact time symptoms started and call 911
now** — that timestamp decides eligibility for clot-busting drugs (thrombolysis, ~4.5 h window) and
thrombectomy (up to ~24 h in selected patients).

@@FIG:12-befast-card@@

**Do not give aspirin** for a suspected stroke — until a CT scan rules out a *bleed*, aspirin could be
catastrophic. **Do not give food or drink** (the swallow may be unsafe). Get them to a stroke-capable hospital
by ambulance — the crew pre-alerts the stroke team.

### 3.3 Anaphylaxis — epinephrine FIRST, and the honest truth about antihistamines

Anaphylaxis is a rapid, multi-system, potentially fatal allergic reaction (food, sting, drug, latex). It
typically begins **minutes to ~2 hours** after exposure with some combination of: **hives/flushing/itch,
swelling of lips/tongue/throat, throat tightness or hoarse voice, wheeze or trouble breathing, vomiting/
crampy abdominal pain, a sudden drop in blood pressure (faintness, collapse), and a feeling of impending
doom.** Airway swelling and circulatory collapse are what kill.

**The one thing to know: epinephrine is the first-line, life-saving treatment, and the commonest fatal error
is *delay*.** (Pumphrey's classic case series of fatal anaphylaxis found a median time to respiratory/cardiac
arrest of minutes, with **delayed epinephrine** a recurring theme.) The 2020 Joint Task Force practice
parameter and the 2024 international consensus[^jtf-anaphylaxis] are unambiguous: **epinephrine, intramuscular, into the outer mid-thigh, given
immediately.** It is extraordinarily safe in this setting; there is **no absolute contraindication** when
anaphylaxis is suspected.

@@FIG:13-anaphylaxis-card@@

> **The honest debunk:** **Antihistamines (Benadryl) and asthma inhalers are NOT treatments for anaphylaxis.**
> Antihistamines treat itch and hives — skin symptoms — and act too slowly to touch airway swelling or shock.
> Relying on them while the airway closes is a documented path to death. Give **epinephrine first**; an
> antihistamine, if any, is a distant afterthought, never a substitute.

**Act:** EpiPen to the outer thigh (through clothing is fine) → call 911 → **lie the person flat with legs
raised** (sudden standing/sitting up can cause fatal "empty-heart" collapse) → **repeat epinephrine in 5–15
minutes** if no improvement → **everyone who gets epinephrine still needs the ER**, because a *biphasic*
reaction can recur hours later. If they carry two auto-injectors, that's why.

### 3.4 Sepsis — the subtle one

Sepsis is the body's **dysregulated, life-threatening response to an infection** — the immune reaction, not
the microbe, becomes the threat (cross-ref §15, §26). It's a leading cause of death worldwide and it kills
partly because it is **easy to miss**: it can masquerade as "a bad flu" until organs start failing. The signal
is **an infection PLUS systemic derangement.** Lay-recognizable red flags (the UK "Sepsis Six" / "SEPSIS"
public messaging):

- **S**lurred speech or **confusion** / "just not acting right"
- **E**xtreme shivering, fever, **or abnormally low temperature** (a normal or low temp does NOT rule it out)
- **P**assing **no urine** all day
- **S**evere breathlessness / very fast breathing
- "**I** feel I might die" — a striking sense of impending doom
- **S**kin **mottled, blue, or very pale**

@@FIG:E4-sepsis@@

The **Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 guidelines (Evans et al., Crit Care Med 2021)**[^ssc] anchor management on **early recognition and time-to-antibiotics**; the lay job is
to **escalate fast and say the word "sepsis"** to the dispatcher/triage nurse — it changes how quickly they're
seen. Children: add **not feeding, repeated vomiting, a non-blanching rash** (a rash that doesn't fade under
pressure — the "glass test" — is a meningococcal-sepsis emergency).

### 3.5 Severe bleeding — the modern update

The doctrine here changed with the wars of the 2000s and the **"Stop the Bleed"** public campaign. Old caution
about tourniquets ("last resort, you'll lose the limb") was **overcorrected and is now considered wrong** for
life-threatening extremity bleeding.

- **First and almost always enough: firm, direct, sustained pressure** right on the wound — your gloved hand
  (or a clean cloth, shirt, anything) pressed **hard** and **not lifted to peek.** Pack deep wounds with
  gauze/cloth and keep pressing.
- **If a limb is bleeding so fast it's life-threatening (spurting, pooling, won't stop):** apply a
  **tourniquet** high on the limb ("high and tight," 2–3 inches above the wound, not over a joint), **tighten
  until the bleeding stops** (it will hurt — that's expected), and **note the time.** **Kragh et al. (Ann
  Surg 2009)**[^kragh] showed in combat casualties that tourniquets applied **before**
  shock onset dramatically improved survival, with limb loss being rare and usually due to the injury, not the
  tourniquet. **A properly applied tourniquet saves lives; a loose one just hides the bleeding.** Do not
  remove it — that's the hospital's call.

@@FIG:E2-stop-the-bleed@@

- Get a real **Stop the Bleed** course; the kit (tourniquet + hemostatic gauze) is cheap and belongs anywhere
  trauma is plausible (cars, worksites, schools).

### 3.6 Choking

- **Recognize:** sudden inability to speak, cough, or breathe; the universal **hands-at-the-throat** clutch;
  silence and panic. A person who can still **cough forcefully** has a partial obstruction — **encourage the
  cough, don't intervene.** It's the *silent* choker who needs you.
- **Act (responsive adult/child):** **5 sharp back blows** between the shoulder blades (lean them forward),
  then **5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich):** fist above the navel, sharp inward-and-upward thrusts. **Alternate
  5 and 5** until the object clears or they collapse.

@@FIG:E1-choking@@

- **If they go unconscious:** lower them, **start CPR**, and check the mouth for a *visible* object before
  breaths (remove only what you can see — **no blind finger sweeps**, which push it deeper).
- **Special cases:** **infants** — 5 back blows + **5 chest thrusts** (never abdominal thrusts). **Pregnant or
  very obese** adults — **chest thrusts** instead of abdominal. **Yourself, alone** — abdominal thrusts
  against a chair back or counter edge.

### 3.7 Opioid overdose — the practical epidemic angle

The opioid epidemic made **naloxone (Narcan)** the single most important first-aid drug of this era for
laypeople. Overdose looks like: **unresponsive, breathing slow/shallow/absent, pinpoint pupils, blue/grey lips
and fingertips, gurgling/snoring.** Death comes from **respiratory depression** — they stop breathing.

- **Naloxone** is an opioid antagonist that reverses it within minutes. It's now widely available **over the
  counter as a nasal spray**, is **safe to give even if you're wrong** (it does nothing to a non-opioid
  cause), and is the textbook case for "carry it, the way you'd carry an EpiPen if you knew someone at risk."
- **Walley et al. (BMJ 2013)**[^walley] showed that **community Overdose Education & Naloxone
  Distribution (OEND)** — putting naloxone in the hands of laypeople — was associated with **significantly
  lower opioid-overdose death rates** at the population level. Supply-side reversal works.
- **Act:** spray naloxone into one nostril → **call 911** → support breathing (rescue breaths / CPR as needed)
  → **repeat naloxone every 2–3 minutes** if no response (today's fentanyl-dominant supply often needs
  multiple doses) → **stay with them** — naloxone wears off in 30–90 min and the opioid can outlast it, so
  they can re-overdose. **Good Samaritan overdose laws** in most US states protect callers from drug-
  possession charges — *call anyway.*

## 4. First aid: evidence-based vs. myth

A surprising amount of "common knowledge" first aid is **wrong, useless, or harmful.** The grading principle
of this manual applies: a confident folk remedy is `anecdotal`-tier (a single story — the weakest evidence)
until a real source says otherwise.

### 4.1 Burns

- **Do:** **cool with cool (not ice-cold) running water for ~20 minutes**, ideally within the first 3 hours.
  This is evidence-based — adequate cool-water first aid reduces burn depth, the need for grafting,
  and scarring (supported by clinical/cohort burn-registry data; e.g. cooling-duration analyses). Remove
  rings/watches/tight items before swelling starts. Cover loosely with **cling film or a clean non-fluffy
  cloth.** Treat pain with normal painkillers.
- **DON'T — the classic harmful myths:**
  - **No butter, oil, toothpaste, egg, or "natural" pastes.** They trap heat, seed infection, and have to be
    painfully scrubbed off later. **Butter on a burn is a bad idea**, full stop.
  - **No ice or ice water.** Too cold causes vasoconstriction and *deepens* the injury (a cold-burn on top of
    a heat-burn). **Cool, not freezing.**
  - **Don't burst blisters** (the roof is a sterile dressing). **Don't peel away clothing stuck to the burn.**

@@FIG:E5-burns@@

- **Go to the ER** for burns that are: large (> a palm-size area), deep/white/charred/painless (full-thickness
  kills the nerves), on the **face, hands, feet, genitals, or over a joint**, circumferential, **electrical or
  chemical**, or any **airway/inhalation** burn (singed nostril hair, soot, hoarse voice — the airway can swell
  shut).

### 4.2 Nosebleeds — the head-tilt myth

> **Debunk:** **Do NOT tilt the head back.** That just sends blood down the throat to be swallowed (nausea,
> vomiting, and you can't see if it's stopping). **Lean forward, pinch the soft part of the nose** (not the
> bony bridge) firmly for **10–15 uninterrupted minutes** — no peeking. Sit upright. Seek care if it won't
> stop after ~20–30 minutes, follows a head injury, or the person is on blood thinners.

### 4.3 Sprains, strains & soft-tissue injury — RICE is retired

The old **RICE** (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation) mnemonic has been **superseded** (cross-ref the rehab
section §21). The current sports-medicine framework is **PEACE & LOVE** (Dubois &
Esculier, *Br J Sports Med* 2020)[^peace-love] brief **P**rotection, **E**levation, avoid
anti-inflammatory over-use, **C**ompression, **E**ducation — then, crucially, **L**oad, **O**ptimism,
**V**ascularisation (movement), **E**xercise. The headline shift: **prolonged rest and aggressive icing are
not the goal** — early, graded, optimistic loading is. (Acute first aid for the first day still uses
relative rest, support, and ice for *pain control* — just don't camp there.) Always rule out a fracture:
deformity, can't bear weight/use it, point tenderness over bone, numbness → image it.

### 4.4 Fractures & "is it broken?"

You usually can't be sure without an X-ray, so **treat a suspected fracture as a fracture.** Immobilize it in
the position found (don't try to straighten it unless the limb is pulseless/blue and you're trained), support
above and below the injury, control any bleeding, and arrange transport. **Open fractures** (bone through
skin) and **suspected spine/neck injuries** (don't move them unless they're in immediate danger — fire,
water; keep the head/neck aligned) are emergencies. Suspect spine injury after falls from height, diving,
high-speed crashes, or any unconscious trauma patient.

### 4.5 Head injury — the red flags

Most bumps to the head are minor. **Watch for the bleed-inside signals** in the hours after, and go to the ER
for any of: **loss of consciousness** (even briefly), **repeated vomiting**, worsening or "worst-ever"
**headache**, **confusion/drowsiness/hard to wake**, **seizure**, **unequal pupils**, weakness/numbness,
clear fluid or blood from nose/ears, a **dangerous mechanism** (high-speed, fall >~1 m / 5 stairs), or anyone
on **blood thinners** (bleeds can be slow and large). For sport, the rule is **"if in doubt, sit them out"** —
a second impact before recovery can be catastrophic.

### 4.6 Seizures — what NOT to do is the whole lesson

> **The two cardinal don'ts:** **Do NOT put anything in the mouth** (the "swallow their tongue" idea is a
> myth — you'll break teeth, get bitten, or block the airway) and **do NOT hold them down / restrain them.**
- **Do:** note the **start time**, **ease them to the floor**, **clear hard/sharp objects** away, cushion the
  head, loosen anything tight at the neck, and **once the jerking stops, roll them into the recovery position**
  to protect the airway while they come round (they'll be confused — "postictal" — for a while; stay, reassure).
- **Call 911 if:** it lasts **>5 minutes** or repeats without recovery (status epilepticus — a true
  emergency), it's a **first-ever** seizure, there's injury, it happens in **water**, the person is **pregnant
  or diabetic**, or they don't come round.

### 4.7 The recovery position

For anyone **unconscious but breathing normally** (not in cardiac arrest), roll them onto their **side** so
the tongue falls forward and any vomit drains out rather than into the lungs. It is the default holding
position while you wait for help, and it converts "unconscious on their back" (airway at risk) into
"unconscious and protected." (If you suspect a spine injury and they're breathing, this is a judgment call —
maintaining the airway wins over a theoretical spine concern if they're vomiting.)

### 4.8 Quick-hit myth debunks

| Myth | Reality |
|---|---|
| **Suck the venom out of a snakebite / cut and suck** | Useless and harmful. Keep the limb still and below heart level, remove tight items, **get to antivenom.** No tourniquet, no ice, no incision, no suction. |
| **Pee on a jellyfish sting** | Not supported and can worsen some stings. Rinse with **vinegar** (for many species) or **hot water (~45 °C) immersion**; remove tentacles without bare hands; seek care for box-jelly/systemic symptoms. |
| **Rub a frostbitten or hypothermic limb to warm it** | Don't — friction damages tissue and can trigger arrhythmia. Rewarm gently and steadily; no snow-rubbing. |

_(Other reflexes worth un-learning are covered where the topic lives: nosebleed §4.2, burns §4.1, choking §3.6, seizures §4.6, tourniquets §3.5, drowning §6.4.)_

## 5. Triage: ER vs. urgent care vs. wait — the honest version

People die from under-triage (waiting out a stroke) and waste fortunes / clog the system from over-triage
(an ER visit for a cold). The honest middle:

**Call 911 / go to the ER immediately (the red flags — never "wait and see"):**
- **Chest pain/pressure**, especially with sweating, nausea, breathlessness, or radiating pain
- **Any BE-FAST stroke sign** — face droop, arm drift, speech trouble, sudden vision/balance loss
- **Trouble breathing** — can't speak full sentences, blue lips, gasping, severe wheeze
- **Anaphylaxis** — throat/tongue swelling, widespread hives + breathing/faintness
- **"Worst headache of my life"** / thunderclap headache (possible brain bleed)
- **Severe uncontrolled bleeding**, or **major trauma** (high-speed, fall from height)
- **Sudden severe abdominal pain**, **coughing/vomiting blood**, **black tarry stools**
- **Unresponsiveness, a first or prolonged seizure, sudden confusion**
- **Signs of sepsis** (§3.4), **stiff neck + fever + rash** (meningitis), **suicidal intent** (cross-ref §20)
- A **fever in a baby <3 months**, or a child who is **floppy, blue, hard to wake, or has a non-blanching rash**

**Urgent care / same-day primary care (real but not life-threatening):**
- Sprains and simple suspected fractures (able to bear some weight), minor cuts that may need stitches, mild
  burns, ear/throat/urinary infections, moderate fevers in otherwise-well adults, mild allergic reactions
  (hives only, no breathing/throat involvement), persistent vomiting/diarrhea without red-flag signs.

**Self-care / routine appointment:** colds, minor scrapes, mild headaches, most muscle aches.

> **The override rule:** when the question is *"is this an emergency?"* and you can't tell, **treat
> it as one.** Dispatchers and triage nurses would far rather reassure you than be reached too late.
> Free phone triage exists in many countries (US: poison control + nurse lines; UK: NHS 111). Use it.

## 6. Poisoning, environmental emergencies & special situations

### 6.1 Poisoning & overdose (beyond opioids)

- **Have the number before you need it.** **US Poison Help: 1-800-222-1222** (free, 24/7, expert, anonymous).
  Most countries have an equivalent national poison center. Program it in your phone now.
- **Act:** move them from the source (fumes → fresh air; chemical on skin → flush with water; chemical in eye
  → flush 15+ min). **Do NOT induce vomiting** and **don't give salt water, ipecac, or "neutralizers"** —
  these are outdated and can cause harm (aspiration, caustic re-burn). Bring the **container/label** to the
  phone/ER. For **carbon monoxide** (headache, dizziness, nausea, multiple people/pets sick together, a faulty
  heater) — **get everyone outside into fresh air and call 911**; CO is invisible and odorless (own a CO
  alarm).

### 6.2 Heat stroke & hyperthermia

Heat illness is a spectrum. **Heat exhaustion** (heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache, *normal mental
status*) → move to cool/shade, lie down, cool, hydrate with water/electrolytes. **Heat stroke** is the
emergency: **core temp ≳40 °C with altered mental status** — confusion, agitation, slurring, collapse, seizure
— and the skin may be hot and either sweaty (exertional) or dry (classic). **The brain is cooking; minutes
count.**

> **The single most important fact: COOL FIRST, transport second — and cool aggressively.** **Cold-water
> immersion** (an ice bath/tub) is the standard of care and lowers mortality in exertional heat stroke (military and athletic cooling case-series, `cohort`/`case-control`); if not
> available, **dowse with water + fan, and pack ice at the neck, armpits, and groin.** Don't wait for the
> ambulance to start. Fever-reducers (paracetamol [US: acetaminophen/Tylenol] or ibuprofen) **do not work**
> here — this isn't a fever, it's a
> failure of heat dissipation.

### 6.3 Hypothermia & cold

Hypothermia (core <35 °C) progresses: **shivering and clumsiness → confusion, slurring, apathy ("umbles":
stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles) → shivering STOPS** (an ominous sign of severe hypothermia) →
unconsciousness. Two rules dominate:

1. **Handle gently.** A severely cold heart is electrically irritable — rough movement, or rubbing the limbs,
   can trigger a **fatal arrhythmia**. Move them carefully, horizontally.
2. **Insulate and rewarm the core, not the limbs.** Get them **dry**, out of wind, insulated **including from
   the ground and the head**, and add warmth to the **trunk** (warm packs to chest/armpits/groin, body heat,
   warm sweet drinks **only if fully alert** and able to swallow). **No alcohol** (it dumps core heat), **no
   direct scalding heat**, **no vigorous rubbing.** "Not dead until warm and dead" is a real principle —
   profoundly cold patients have been resuscitated, so CPR continues during rewarming. **Frostbite:** don't
   rewarm if there's any chance of re-freezing (worse than staying frozen); rewarm in ~37–39 °C water once
   safe, and never rub.

### 6.4 Drowning & the cold-/breath-hold-water rules

- **Real drowning is silent and fast** — not the thrashing, shouting Hollywood version. A drowning person is
  usually **upright, quiet, unable to call out**, head low. Watch children near water *constantly*; drowning
  is a leading cause of child death and takes seconds.
- **Drowning is a *hypoxic* (asphyxial) arrest** — oxygen is the deficit. So unlike adult sudden cardiac
  arrest, **rescue breaths matter:** if the person isn't breathing, give **rescue breaths first, then full CPR
  (30:2)**. **Don't waste time trying to "empty water from the lungs"** (Szpilman, *NEJM* 2012)[^szpilman] —
  start ventilation.
- **Rescue without becoming the second victim:** *reach, throw, row — don't go* unless trained. Most
  would-be-rescuer drownings are untrained people swimming out.
- **The breath-hold rule:** **never hyperventilate before underwater swimming/
  breath-holds, and never breath-hold-swim alone.** Hyperventilation suppresses the \(\mathrm{CO_{2}}\) "breathe!" urge
  without adding much oxygen, so the swimmer blacks out underwater (**shallow-water blackout**) before feeling
  the need to surface — and drowns silently. This kills fit, strong swimmers.
- **Cold-water immersion** triggers a **cold-shock gasp reflex** (involuntary inhalation underwater) and fast
  incapacitation — another reason sudden falls into cold water are deadly even for swimmers.

## 7. Preparedness — the cheap insurance

You cannot improvise competence in the moment; you assemble it in advance.

- **A basic first-aid kit** (home + car): gloves, assorted dressings/bandages/gauze, adhesive plasters,
  medical tape, **a tourniquet + hemostatic gauze** (Stop the Bleed), triangular bandage, scissors, tweezers,
  CPR face-shield, thermometer, antiseptic wipes, burn dressing, foil/space blanket, saline for irrigation,
  and a card with **emergency + poison-control numbers.**
- **The drugs worth pre-positioning** *for known risks*: **personal epinephrine auto-injectors** (×2) for
  anyone with a serious allergy — **and make sure people around them know where it is and how to use it**;
  **naloxone** in any household touched by opioid use or for community carry; **aspirin** for adult-cardiac
  use.
- **Know your meds and conditions.** Keep a current list of **medications, doses, allergies, conditions, and
  emergency contacts** — on paper in a wallet and in your phone's **medical-ID / emergency-info screen**
  (accessible from a locked phone). For high-risk conditions, a **medical-alert bracelet** speaks for you when
  you can't.
- **Advance directives (cross-ref §19).** Decide, document, and *share* your wishes — a
  healthcare proxy / durable power of attorney and a living will (and, where appropriate, a POLST/DNR) — so
  that in the worst case the people treating you know what *you* want. This is first aid for your autonomy.
- **Learn it with your hands.** Take and re-take a hands-on course (Go deeper); teach your household. The
  competence that saves a life is rehearsed, not read.
