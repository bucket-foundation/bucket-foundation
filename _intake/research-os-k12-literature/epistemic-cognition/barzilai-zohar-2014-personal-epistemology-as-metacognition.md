# Reconsidering Personal Epistemology as Metacognition: A Multifaceted Approach to the Analysis of Epistemic Thinking

Argues epistemic thinking is a multifaceted construct combining cognitive and
metacognitive components, which should be targeted separately by instruction rather than
as one undifferentiated outcome.

## Key Claims

- Argues one of the central unresolved conceptual issues in personal-epistemology
  research is the relationship between personal epistemology and metacognition, two
  contested and diverse constructs whose intersection is rarely made explicit.
- Proposes epistemic thinking is a multifaceted construct combining cognitive and
  metacognitive aspects, and that epistemic metacognition itself has several separable
  components: metacognitive skills, metacognitive knowledge about persons, strategies, and
  tasks, and metacognitive experiences.
- Draws theoretical, methodological, and instructional implications from this approach,
  including that instruction aimed at epistemic thinking should target these components
  separately rather than treating epistemic cognition as a single skill.

## Research Questions It Leaves Open

- Whether Check's own grounded-verdict step, which Kuhn (1999)'s own card in this corpus
  already asks about, develops epistemic metacognitive skill (knowing how to judge a
  source), epistemic metacognitive knowledge (knowing what kind of evidence a claim
  needs), or an epistemic metacognitive experience (a felt sense of confidence or doubt),
  a distinction this paper draws but no Research OS document currently checks Check
  against.
- Whether the components this paper separates can be measured independently in a K-12
  Production, or whether they are as practically entangled in observed behavior as
  Sandoval (2005) finds formal and practical epistemology to be.
