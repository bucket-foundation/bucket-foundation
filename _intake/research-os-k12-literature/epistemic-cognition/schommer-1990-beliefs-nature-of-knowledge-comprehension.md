# Effects of Beliefs About the Nature of Knowledge on Comprehension

The founding multidimensional epistemological-beliefs instrument, showing a belief that
knowledge is simple and certain independently predicts weaker comprehension of an
ambiguous text.

## Key Claims

- Develops and validates a questionnaire measuring epistemological beliefs along several
  independent dimensions, including belief in the certainty of knowledge, the simplicity
  of knowledge, and the speed at which learning occurs, treating epistemological belief
  as multidimensional rather than a single trait.
- Students who believed knowledge was simple and certain were more likely to draw overly
  simplified, absolute conclusions from an ambiguous passage than students who believed
  knowledge was complex and tentative, a direct link between epistemic belief and how a
  text is comprehended.
- Belief in quick, all-or-nothing learning independently predicted worse comprehension
  monitoring, students who believed learning should happen fast were less accurate at
  judging their own understanding of what they had read.

## Research Questions It Leaves Open

- Whether the specific belief this study finds most consequential, that knowledge is
  simple and certain, predicts how a K-12 learner reads a routed node's own summary and
  prerequisite text, the same comprehension-of-a-source task this study's own ambiguous
  passage measures.
- Whether Check's own support-or-contradiction verdict, a binary judgment, risks
  reinforcing a simple-and-certain epistemic stance in exactly the learners this study
  finds already hold it, rather than developing the complex, tentative stance the study
  associates with better comprehension.
