# Understanding students' practical epistemologies and their influence on learning through inquiry

Argues a gap exists between students' formal beliefs about the nature of science and their
actual "practical epistemologies," the beliefs guiding how they conduct their own inquiry,
and that inquiry instruction ignoring this gap will not build genuine scientific
understanding.

## Key Claims

- US science education's long-standing goal, students leaving with a robust understanding of
  the nature of science, does not happen in practice, decades of research show.
- A gap exists between research on students' formal epistemological beliefs and research on
  their actual inquiry practices; the two lines of research rarely illuminate each other.
- Inquiry instruction that ignores a student's practical epistemology stands little chance of
  building understanding of professional science, since practical epistemology, not stated
  belief, governs how the student actually investigates.

## Research Questions It Leaves Open

- What a K-12 student's practical epistemology looks like when inquiry tools are a
  constrained, four-tool AI workspace rather than the open-ended investigations every
  reviewed study used.
- Whether Check's grounded-verdict step changes a learner's practical epistemology directly,
  or only changes behavior while Check is present without touching the underlying stance.

## How It Bears on Research OS

A sharp complication for any claim that workspace use builds nature-of-science understanding
as a byproduct: formal belief and practical inquiry epistemology are distinct and often
disconnected, so a learner could pass every Check verdict without their epistemic stance
toward evidence changing. Extends overlap-map question 12, and complicates Sadler and
colleagues (2010)'s finding of mixed nature-of-science outcomes from apprenticeship
participation by supplying the mechanism that could explain it.
