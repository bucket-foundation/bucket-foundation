# Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science

The large-scale empirical measurement of how often a published finding replicates, the base rate any AI-assisted hypothesis-ranking or evidence-fusion system has to treat as a prior on a cited claim's own reliability.

## Key Claims

- Direct replications of 100 published psychology studies succeeded, by the strictest significance criterion, in roughly 36 percent of cases, though replication effect sizes were on average about half the size of the original published effect.
- Replication success was not well predicted by the original study's own reported p-value or by the replicating team's own subjective prior confidence in the effect, evidence that publication itself is a weak reliability signal.
- The project's own pre-registration and multi-lab protocol is presented as a methodological template for large-scale replication efforts in other fields.

## Research Questions It Leaves Open

- Whether reproducibility rates differ by sub-field or method type in ways a citation-weighting scheme could learn and apply automatically, a question this project's own psychology-only sample cannot answer for other domains.
- How a system fusing evidence from many cited sources should discount a single study's claim given this base rate, versus the naive treatment of peer-reviewed as sufficient reliability.

## How It Bears on Research OS

Is the direct empirical grounding for why the hypothesis engine's own tier system and detectability-weighted belief fusion decline to treat published as equivalent to true: this project's own roughly one-in-three replication rate for even rigorously peer-reviewed psychology findings is the base rate a tier-and-detectability model is built to price into a source's fused weight, rather than trusting venue prestige alone.
