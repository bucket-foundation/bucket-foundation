# Evaluating the replicability of social science experiments in Nature and Science between 2010 and 2015

Extends market-based replication-forecasting from a single field to social-science
experiments in the two most prestigious general-science venues, finding forecasts again
predicted which studies replicated.

## Key Claims

- Attempted direct replications of a sample of social-science experiments originally
  published in Nature and Science between 2010 and 2015, about three in five of which
  successfully replicated by conventional significance criteria, with replicated effect
  sizes substantially smaller on average than the original published estimates, the same
  pattern Open Science Collaboration (2015) reports for psychology alone.
- Prediction markets and surveys run on the same studies before the replications were
  known forecast which studies would and would not replicate with meaningfully better than
  chance accuracy, corroborating Dreber and colleagues (2015)'s own finding in a different,
  higher-prestige sample.
- Publication in a top general-science venue is treated as an insufficient reliability
  signal on its own, the same conclusion Open Science Collaboration (2015) reaches for peer
  review generally.

## Research Questions It Leaves Open

- Whether the market-forecasting accuracy this paper and Dreber and colleagues (2015)
  both report would hold if the forecasters were an AI ranking system instead of human
  researchers trading on their own beliefs.
- Whether venue prestige should carry any residual weight in the engine's own
  detectability-weighted belief fusion once this paper's own top-venue replication rate is
  priced in, or whether it should be treated as no more informative than any other venue.

## How It Bears on Research OS

Sharpens Open Science Collaboration (2015)'s own base-rate argument for the engine's tier
system: publication in Nature or Science, the two most selective general-science venues in
the corpus's own reference set, still leaves a meaningful share of published findings
unreplicated, so a detectability-weighted fusion model that let venue prestige alone push a
claim's fused weight high would be repeating the same mistake this paper's own forecasting-
market design was built to correct. The market-forecasting result itself is a second,
independent replication, after Dreber and colleagues (2015), that a calibrated probability
estimate over "will this claim hold up" is achievable before the underlying study is
re-run, the exact claim the engine's own ranking label makes about an unreplicated
hypothesis.
