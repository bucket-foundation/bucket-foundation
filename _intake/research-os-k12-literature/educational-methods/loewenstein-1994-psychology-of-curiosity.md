# The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation

Loewenstein's information-gap theory frames curiosity as the drive to close a felt gap between what one knows and what one wants to know, the same shape as Research OS's own frontier-backward routing from a curiosity question ('why is the sky blue') back to what the learner already holds.

## Key Claims

- Curiosity is best explained as arising from a perceived gap between current knowledge and a desired state of knowledge, rather than from novelty or complexity alone.
- The intensity of curiosity tends to increase as the gap narrows and an answer feels within reach, beyond the initial spike when the gap first opens.
- Curiosity produces an aversive, drive-like state that reference points and reward can only partly substitute for, distinguishing it from ordinary preference for information.

## Research Questions It Leaves Open

- How an information-gap account of curiosity should shape the sequencing of a multi-step answer, versus revealing an answer all at once.
- Whether artificially induced information gaps sustain engagement as well as a learner's own spontaneously arising question.

## How It Bears on Research OS

The product's own founding example, a child asking 'why is the sky blue,' is a curiosity question in Loewenstein's exact sense, a felt gap between what the child knows and wants to know; frontier-backward routing's design (resolve the target, find the mastered frontier, walk forward one step at a time) is structured to keep that gap perceptibly narrowing at each step along the path, which Loewenstein's finding suggests should sustain curiosity better than presenting the full answer immediately.
