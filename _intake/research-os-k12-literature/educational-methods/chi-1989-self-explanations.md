# Self-Explanations: How Students Study and Use Examples in Learning to Solve Problems

This paper established self-explanation, generating one's own explanation of why a worked example's steps are valid, as a learning strategy that predicts problem-solving success independent of how much time a student spends studying, directly motivating a workspace that asks a learner to produce their own claim and reasoning rather than read a generated answer.

## Key Claims

- Students who generated more self-explanations while studying worked examples solved subsequent problems more accurately, controlling for total study time.
- Good and poor learners studied the same worked examples for similar durations, but differed sharply in how much self-explanation they generated while doing so.
- Self-explanation helps a learner detect gaps in their own understanding and repair faulty mental models mid-study, before those gaps surface as an error on a later problem.

## Research Questions It Leaves Open

- Whether self-explanation can be prompted or scaffolded on demand in learners who do not generate it spontaneously, and by what mechanism.
- How self-explanation quality should be assessed automatically at scale, since the original study relied on hand-coded verbal protocols.

## How It Bears on Research OS

The Understanding stage's free-response explanation item, graded first by deterministic checks and falling back to an LLM-judge pass, operationalizes self-explanation as a gate a learner must pass rather than an optional study habit; Chi and colleagues' finding that generating an explanation (not just reading one) predicts problem-solving success is the direct justification for the design rule that Check never writes the student's explanation for them, only verifies it.
