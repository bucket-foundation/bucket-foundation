# Teachers as Facilitators: What Autonomy-Supportive Teachers Do and Why Their Students Benefit

Names concrete, teachable teacher behaviors that determine whether students in a required
or structured task stay engaged rather than only compliant.

## Key Claims

- Within self-determination theory's own dialectical framework, students carry inner
  motivational resources that classroom conditions can either support or frustrate;
  autonomy-supportive teachers nurture those resources rather than override them.
- The article names what autonomy-supportive teachers concretely say and do during
  instruction, and argues teachers can learn to be more autonomy-supportive, a trainable
  skill rather than a fixed disposition.
- Students are most engaged when teachers offer high levels of both autonomy support and
  structure together, not autonomy support in place of structure, and an
  autonomy-supportive style is framed as an important part of a high-quality
  teacher-student relationship.

## Research Questions It Leaves Open

- Whether the autonomy-support-plus-structure combination this paper argues maximizes
  engagement has a specific operational form for a Production's own teacher-review step,
  where the reviewer's stance (autonomy-supportive versus controlling feedback) is not
  currently specified by any Research OS document.
- Whether autonomy support delivered by a human teacher reviewing a Production
  transfers the same benefit as autonomy support embedded in the AI-scoped tools
  themselves, a distinction this paper, written before AI-mediated classroom tools, does
  not address.

Grinnell and colleagues (2020)'s misconduct finding and Bangera and Brownell (2014)'s
access argument both treat "required" as a fixed, binary condition, but this paper argues
the controlling-versus-autonomy-supportive manner in which a requirement is delivered, not
only whether it exists, predicts student engagement. A structured, autonomy-supportive
teacher-review step around a mandatory Production, offering rationale, acknowledging the
learner's perspective, and providing meaningful choice within the required structure, is
this paper's own candidate mechanism for a mandatory design that avoids the disengagement
Grinnell and colleagues document.
