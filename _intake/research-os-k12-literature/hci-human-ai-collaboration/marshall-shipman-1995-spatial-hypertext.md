# Spatial hypertext

The founding paper on spatial hypertext, organizing information by position and visual proximity rather than explicit links, the closest existing design pattern to a pannable, zoomable graph canvas used as a thinking surface rather than a fixed diagram.

## Key Claims

- Spatial hypertext lets users express relationships between information items, grouping, proximity, alignment, informally, through position on a canvas, before or instead of committing to an explicit, typed link.
- This informal, spatial organization supports early-stage sensemaking, when relationships are still uncertain or shifting, better than a rigid linked structure, which forces premature commitment to a relationship type.
- The same spatial layout can carry implicit structure a system can later parse, proximity implies relatedness, letting a system infer structure from how users arrange thinking rather than requiring users to declare it.

## Research Questions It Leaves Open

- How much of the design's benefit depends on user-authored spatial layout versus a layout computed for the user, the case a frontier-backward-routed canvas represents.
- Whether a system-computed layout, mastered lit, gap dim, target ahead, still delivers sensemaking benefits when the user did not choose the arrangement.

## How It Bears on Research OS

Frames a design tension the spatial canvas workspace has to resolve: Research OS's own canvas positions nodes by the system's frontier-backward routing rather than by the learner's own spatial arrangement, the reverse of the user-authored spatial hypertext this paper studies, so whichever sensemaking benefit spatial organization provides needs to be tested rather than assumed to transfer to a system-computed layout.
