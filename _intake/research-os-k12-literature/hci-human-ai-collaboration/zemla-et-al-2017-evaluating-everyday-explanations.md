# Evaluating everyday explanations

An empirical study of what features actually predict whether a layperson judges an
explanation as good, and finds people are better at recognizing a good explanation than
generating one.

## Key Claims

- Asks what specific features of an everyday explanation predict whether people rate it
  as good, testing candidate features against real explanations for real-world facts and
  events rather than only abstract or artificial stimuli.
- People's judgments of explanation quality track properties such as how well an
  explanation coheres with what the judge already believes and how much of the target
  fact or event the explanation accounts for, echoing Lombrozo (2006)'s own simplicity
  and breadth preferences with direct empirical measurement.
- People are found to be more reliable at recognizing a good explanation when presented
  with one than at generating a good explanation themselves, an asymmetry relevant to any
  design that relies on self-generated rather than machine-offered explanations.

## Research Questions It Leaves Open

- Whether the recognize-better-than-generate asymmetry this paper finds means a K-12
  learner should be asked to rate the quality of a machine-offered explanation rather than
  write their own from scratch, a different Production design than the current
  citation-and-claim format.
- Whether the coherence-with-prior-belief feature this paper finds predicts perceived
  explanation quality risks rewarding an explanation that confirms what a learner already
  believes over one that is better supported by evidence, a tension Check's own
  support-or-contradiction verdict is built to catch but this paper's own subjects were
  not screened against.

## How It Bears on Research OS

Supplies an operational rubric the engine's own disclosed understanding artifact (PR
#60) could be evaluated against directly: rather than asking only whether the artifact is
accurate, this paper's own measured features, coherence and how completely the
explanation accounts for its target, give a way to score whether the artifact is the kind
of explanation people judge as good, independent of correctness. The
generate-versus-recognize asymmetry also bears on Production's own design: if learners
are better at judging an explanation than producing one, a Production step that asks a
learner to rate a machine-offered explanation's quality, rather than author one unaided,
may better match this paper's own measured human capability.
