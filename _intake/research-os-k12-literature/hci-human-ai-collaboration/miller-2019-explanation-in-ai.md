# Explanation in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Social Sciences

Argues explainable-AI research should build on established social-science accounts of how
humans explain to each other, rather than on researcher intuition about what a good
explanation looks like.

## Key Claims

- There has been a resurgence of explainable-AI research aimed at making algorithms more
  understandable, but most of that work relies only on researchers' own intuitions about
  what constitutes a good explanation rather than on existing research into how people
  define, generate, select, evaluate, and present explanations to each other.
- Reviews philosophy, cognitive psychology and science, and social psychology literature
  on human explanation, arguing people bring specific cognitive biases and social
  expectations to the explanation process that a system explaining itself to a human
  should account for.
- Draws out findings from that literature and proposes ways they could be infused into
  explainable-AI system design, treating human explanation as a starting point rather than
  an afterthought.

## Research Questions It Leaves Open

- Whether the engine's own disclosed, model-generated understanding artifact (PR #60)
  was designed against any of the social-science findings this paper reviews, or against
  researcher intuition of the kind this paper argues against.
- Which specific social-science finding, among the many this survey reviews, most
  directly applies to a K-12 learner's own reading of a Check verdict's rationale, a
  narrowing this general-audience survey does not perform.

## How It Bears on Research OS

Sets the standard the batch's other explanation-quality cards (Lombrozo 2006; Keil 2006;
Zemla and colleagues 2017) sharpen: this survey's own central argument, that XAI research
too often substitutes researcher intuition for an established account of explanation, is
exactly the risk a disclosed, model-generated understanding artifact runs if it was built
without reference to what makes a human explanation good. Krenn and colleagues (2022),
already in this corpus, argues an AI system must let a human internalize a pattern to
count as building understanding; this paper supplies the literature that names what
internalizable explanations look like.
