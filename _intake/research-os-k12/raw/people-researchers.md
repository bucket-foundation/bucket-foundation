## Implications for Bucket

### Knowledge graphs and mastery learning at scale

Bloom's 1984 claim of a 2 SD gain from one-to-one tutoring plus mastery learning was demonstrated in small controlled studies with few tutors and few students. It has not replicated at scale under any delivery mechanism tested since. VanLehn's 2011 meta-analysis put intelligent tutoring systems at about 0.76 SD against no-tutoring baselines, statistically close to human tutors at about 0.79 SD in his sample, both well under 2 SD. Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan's 2020 meta-analysis of roughly 96 high-dosage tutoring RCTs found an average effect of about 0.37 SD, with effects shrinking as programs moved from small efficacy trials to real deployments, and shrinking further when delivered by less-trained tutors at scale. This drop from efficacy trial to at-scale deployment shows up across the field: Carnegie Learning's Cognitive Tutor, ASSISTments, and Khan Academy's mastery-graph exercises report real but moderate gains in controlled studies, smaller gains once rolled out across many schools and teachers with uneven fidelity. The consistent finding across ASSISTments and Cognitive Tutor research is that usage dosage and implementation fidelity, not algorithm quality, set the ceiling on effect size. A self-directed, no-institution-required tool has no teacher or program forcing dosage; Bucket's numbers should be expected to land nearer the 0.3-0.4 SD range of at-scale tutoring programs than the 2 SD figure Bloom reported in a hothouse setting, and retention/usage design deserves as much attention as graph design.

### The five-state model against ICAP, Bloom, and SOLO

Chi's ICAP framework classifies the overt behavior a learner performs during an activity: Passive, Active, Constructive, Interactive, in that ascending order of predicted learning outcome, and this ordering has held up across many domains in meta-analysis. Bucket's five states (Access, Awareness, Understanding, Internalization, Production) classify something different: depth of mastery of a piece of content, closer to Bloom's revised cognitive-process ladder (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create) or to the SOLO taxonomy's structural levels (Prestructural through Extended Abstract). Treating the five states as a competitor to ICAP would be a category error. ICAP better explains the mechanism of a state transition: passive reading can move a learner from Access to Awareness at best, only constructive or interactive engagement plausibly moves Understanding into Internalization or Production. Bucket's design would gain from stating this mapping directly and letting ICAP govern which activities the workspace offers at each state rather than leaving the five states unmoored from an established engagement model.

A second gap: SOLO and Perkins both distinguish a persistent learner state from the quality of one submitted artifact, and the current description of Production does not make clear which one Bucket means. Perkins's "understanding performances" require demonstrated understanding in varied, unfamiliar contexts, not a repeat of the practiced format, to count as evidence. Cognitive Tutor research by Baker and colleagues on "gaming the system" documents students learning to satisfy a tutor's specific checks without acquiring transferable understanding, exactly the failure mode Perkins's framework warns against. Before wide deployment, Bucket needs either a published mapping and validation of its five states against one of these three frameworks, or its own construct-validation study showing that a state classification predicts transfer performance and forward learning rate.

### The AI-limited-to-find-quote-check-organize constraint against RCT evidence

Two 2024 RCTs point in different directions. Tutor CoPilot found gains when AI suggested, rather than generated, a human tutor's response, with the largest gains for less-experienced tutors, evidence for an assistive rather than answer-giving AI design close in spirit to Bucket's constraint. The Harvard physics RCT found a more permissive, directly explanatory AI tutor beat in-person active learning on short-term gains in one narrow, motivated, higher-education setting, a real result that cuts against assuming Bucket's more restrictive design wins on learning-gain measures alone; if it is the right choice, the reason is more likely skill-building and epistemic independence than raw test-score lift.

The stronger justification for the constraint comes from an older, larger literature: worked-example and self-explanation research from Renkl, Kalyuga, and Sweller, combined with newer findings on generative-AI homework helpers, shows a recurring pattern of a short-term performance boost on AI-assisted problems paired with worse performance on later unassisted or transfer tests, an illusion of competence from outsourcing the retrieval or construction step. Bucket's find/quote/check/organize limit targets exactly this failure mode. What none of the cited studies test is Bucket's own condition directly: a find/quote/check/organize-only AI has not itself been through an RCT. It is a reasonable extrapolation from two adjacent literatures, not a validated intervention. The concrete recommendation is a partnered RCT comparing find/quote/check/organize-only AI, a more permissive explain-and-answer AI, and a no-AI control across matched graph nodes, scored on delayed unassisted post-tests rather than immediate assisted performance, before making a learning-benefit claim for the constraint. The Nigeria pilot's facilitator-mediated design, an adult supervising AI access rather than a fully autonomous tool, is a closer real-world analog to Bucket's teacher class view than to an open unsupervised chatbot, and worth citing once its authorship is confirmed.

### Five researchers to contact first

**Zachary Pardos**, UC Berkeley. Ask: a technical review of the frontier-backward routing algorithm against current knowledge-tracing methods, specifically whether the model risks the same interpretability problems he has raised about black-box deep knowledge tracing.

**Wayne Holmes**, UCL. Ask: a critique of the find/quote/check/organize guardrail against his published taxonomy of AI-in-education harms, framed as a short written review rather than a meeting, matched to how academics with policy workloads tend to respond.

**Vincent Aleven**, Carnegie Mellon. Ask: advice on implementing the "check" function using expectation-and-misconception-tailored dialogue methods from CTAT and Mathtutor, and whether a small pilot using CTAT-style components is realistic.

**Neil Heffernan**, WPI. Ask: how ASSISTments funds and operates a free K-12 math tool at scale on IES/NSF grants, as an operating template, and whether dataset or infrastructure interoperability between ASSISTments and Bucket's graph is worth exploring.

**Michelene Chi**, Arizona State. Ask: a single, narrow question, whether Bucket's five learner states map cleanly onto ICAP's four engagement modes or represent a distinct construct that needs its own validation, framed to be answerable in one email rather than a meeting.

All five outreach asks are written as single, concrete, low-effort questions rather than open-ended partnership pitches, since reachability for senior academics tracks with how quickly a question can be answered, not with the size or ambition of the ask.
