## Cluster 6: AI Lab Nonprofit-Hybrid Structure

**OpenAI.** Founded December 2015 as a pure nonprofit; pledged $1B, actually received only $133M by 2021 per tax filings (a useful data point on how far pledge totals diverge from cash actually collected). Restructured 2019 into a "capped-profit" structure (100x return cap) with the nonprofit retaining controlling-shareholder status and fiduciary primacy over the for-profit arm. Restructured again in 2024-2025: completed October 28, 2025 as a Delaware public benefit corporation, profit cap removed. Resulting equity split: **OpenAI Foundation (the renamed nonprofit) 26%, Microsoft 27%, employees/other investors 47%**. The Foundation retains the right to pursue "separate charitable projects, including in science and education." No public dollar figure found for the Foundation's own operating endowment distinct from its equity stake.

Read for Bucket: this is the closest large-scale precedent for "a nonprofit mission wrapper that owns equity in a commercial engine," but it also shows the direction of travel over a decade was from nonprofit control toward for-profit control, the opposite of what a mission-locked structure should do. It is a cautionary structural template more than an aspirational one.

## Cluster 7: Illicit Shadow-Library Contrast

**Sci-Hub.** Funded entirely by user donations; founder Alexandra Elbakyan runs it personally to minimize compromise. Legal record: Elsevier won a **$15M default judgment** (2017); American Chemical Society won a **$4.8M default judgment** (2017) plus a permanent injunction against ISPs/registrars; designated a "notorious market" by the US Trade Representative since 2018; court-ordered ISP blocking in Sweden, Russia, France, Belgium, India (Delhi High Court ban, August 2025), UK. Usage scale: ~400K requests/day (2019), 1 billion+ cumulative downloads by mid-2020, 88.3 million files in its collection by mid-2022.

Read for Bucket: Sci-Hub proves free-to-read-at-massive-scale is achievable outside any funder relationship at all, but only by forfeiting legality entirely, accumulating tens of millions in unpaid judgments, and operating under continuous international blocking action. It is the do-not-follow end state for "redistribute paywalled content without licensing," directly relevant to any Bucket feature that touches copyrighted source material rather than freely-licensed derivative work.

# Implications for Bucket

**Which funding-model mix has worked at $1M, $10M, and $50M scale.**

At the **$1M scale**, every surviving example in this set is single-source: one family (CK-12/Khosla), one founder's prize money plus a university host (PhET/Wieman), one federal grant plus a university host (LibreTexts/DoE), or one benefactor's declared commitment (Signal/Acton's reported $50M pledge funding the first several years). None of the $1M-stage orgs had diversified revenue yet; diversification is something orgs grow into, not something they start with. The clean implication: Bucket's Phase 0 should expect, not resist, a single-benefactor or single-grant start.

At the **$10M scale**, the surviving pattern splits into two tracks. Track one is diversified philanthropy across dozens to hundreds of institutional funders plus small individual donors (Center for Open Science at $10.2M in 2024, Our World in Data building from 6 to 20 staff on a mix of Gates/Wellcome/individual small-dollar, ASSISTments crossing $6.7M on research grants). Track two is earned membership revenue replacing grants (ORCID crossed $6-7M running on 95.7% program-service/membership revenue by 2025, after a decade of grant dependency). Crossref sits above this band on membership fees alone. The clean implication: $10M is the scale at which "still 100% donor-dependent" starts to look like a design choice rather than a stage, because ORCID and Crossref prove a member-fee model can carry an org through this band without grants.

At the **$50M scale**, every example is either (a) mass small-dollar philanthropy at Wikimedia's scale ($150-200M+, built over 20 years, never dependent on any single funder), (b) a nonprofit-plus-taxable-subsidiary pairing where commercial revenue funds the mission (Mozilla, Ai2/Allen Institute doubling to $205.8M in 2024 on concentrated foundation-estate money), or (c) heavy government-contract revenue (Digital Promise spiking to $89.3M in 2021, largely COVID-era federal ed-tech money, then retreating). No org in this set reached $50M on citation fees, membership dues, or micropayments alone. The clean implication: nothing in this evidence base supports Bucket funding itself past $50M on citation-fee revenue by itself; every $50M-scale analog either took 15-20 years of mass-donor accumulation, owns a commercial subsidiary, or runs on government contracts.

**Has any nonprofit made author micropayments or citation fees a real revenue line, and what happened.**

Not among the analogs researched here, at meaningful scale, using Bucket's exact mechanism. The closest analog is ResearchHub, which pays researchers in a crypto token (RSC) for contributions and has distributed **$1.5M+ lifetime**, a real but small number next to any of the philanthropy-scale orgs above. It is founder-subsidized (Brian Armstrong), not self-sustaining off its own fee volume. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy runs the closest *non-crypto* structural cousin: libraries, not individual readers or citers, pay a membership fee to keep the content free, which is the same "someone other than the reader pays" logic Bucket's citation-fee model depends on, but SEP's payer is institutional (libraries), not a distributed set of AI agents and citing authors as Bucket's feed402 model assumes. No organization in this set has proven a citation-fee or micropayment line can carry a nonprofit past low-single-digit millions in annual revenue.

**Has crypto funding helped or harmed comparable nonprofits' reputations.**

The strongest signal is negative and explicit: Wikimedia's own community voted to **stop accepting cryptocurrency donations entirely on May 1, 2022**, despite crypto being under 0.08% of donations, meaning the decision cost almost nothing financially and was made purely on reputational/values grounds (environmental impact and volatility cited). Our World in Data separately **rejected a $7.5M grant from FTX's Future Fund in 2022** after due diligence, months before FTX's collapse vindicated the caution. Both of the most-trusted, most-philanthropically-dependent orgs in this entire dataset made a deliberate choice to distance themselves from crypto funding, not to embrace it. Set against that, the crypto-native orgs (ResearchHub, Gitcoin, Optimism Collective, Signal's MobileCoin-era history) operate in a largely separate reputational and funder universe from Gates/Hewlett/Arcadia/Simons/Sloan-style institutional philanthropy, with little evidence of crossover trust. For Bucket, which is explicitly building on x402/Base crypto rails, this is the single most load-bearing finding in the whole research: the funders who sustain Bucket's closest analogs have shown a revealed preference **against** association with crypto mechanics, not merely neutrality.

**Is Bucket's paid-to-cite model compatible with the funders that sustain its analogs (Hewlett, Gates, Arcadia, Simons, Sloan).**

Partially, and only if the crypto rail is kept invisible to the funder relationship. Every one of those five funders appears in this research funding OER or scholarly-infrastructure nonprofits (Hewlett and Gates seeded OpenStax and MIT OCW; Arcadia gave OpenAlex $7.5M and Wikimedia's endowment $8.5M combined; Simons funds arXiv). None of the fetched evidence shows any of them funding a crypto-denominated payment mechanism directly. Arcadia's $7.5M OpenAlex grant is the most relevant precedent: it funded free, open scholarly infrastructure with **no** token or blockchain component. The realistic read: these funders will likely fund Bucket's knowledge-graph, editorial, and free-access mission the same way they fund OpenAlex or OpenStax, as a grant to a 501(c)(3) doing open-access work, largely indifferent to (or wary of) the fact that a citation-payment rail underneath uses x402/Base. Bucket should expect to pitch the nonprofit mission and the crypto payment rail as separable stories to this funder class, the way Wikimedia keeps its endowment donor-advised-fund structure separate from its banner-campaign small-dollar base.

**Realistic first-year budget and staffing for a credible Phase 0.**

Cross-referencing the $1M-scale analogs (PhET's 16-person team on NSF plus one founder gift, arXiv's ~$826K/year budget for years running the entire physics/math preprint commons, ORCID's first fundable years starting near $150K-$500K and not clearing $1M until roughly year four), a credible Phase 0 for Bucket sits in the **$500K-$1.5M range for year one**, funding a small team (4-8 people: a technical lead for the knowledge graph and x402 integration, 1-2 content/taxonomy editors, a part-time grants/development lead, and founder time) plus infrastructure costs (Base gas/settlement, hosting, the feed402 reference implementation). This is deliberately below the $2-4M level at which OpenStax-scale foundation grants typically arrive, and below the level at which a program officer at Hewlett or Arcadia would expect a track record before writing a check; year one should be funded by the kind of single-benefactor or small-grant-cluster pattern seen at CK-12, PhET, and early ORCID, not by a diversified multi-funder base, because that diversification does not arrive until an org has 2-4 years of demonstrated output (per ORCID's decade-long, and Center for Open Science's multi-year, buildup patterns).

**The three most transferable playbooks.**

1. **ORCID's grant-to-membership transition.** A scholarly-infrastructure nonprofit ran negative net assets for seven straight years (2012-2018) while shifting from 70%+ grant-dependent revenue to 95%+ program-service/membership revenue, and came out the other side solvent and growing. This is the single closest financial-engineering precedent for Bucket's own "start on philanthropy, graduate to citation-fee revenue" arc, complete with the multi-year negative-equity runway a founder should plan for and disclose to funders up front, not discover midway.

2. **The university-host-subsidy pattern (OpenStax/Rice, LibreTexts/UC Davis, PhET/CU Boulder, MIT OCW, SEP/Stanford).** Five separate OER/reference projects survive by living inside a university's balance sheet rather than filing independent 990s. Bucket has no equivalent host institution (it is legally independent, founder-owned pending nonprofit status per the brief), which removes this subsidy option, but the pattern argues for actively seeking a university or library-consortium partner as a quasi-host for specific functions (data hosting, editorial review, legal cover), the way SEP uses library consortia as its funding and legitimacy anchor, rather than trying to build every function inside a single standalone entity from day one.

3. **Wikimedia's two-tier structure separating mass small-dollar operating funds from a small-donor-list permanent endowment, held in a distinct legal vehicle (donor-advised fund at Tides).** This is the clearest model for how to take large gifts from crypto-adjacent or platform-company donors (Bucket's plausible funder base given its Base/x402 foundations) without letting those donors' provenance attach to the operating mission's reputation, and for how to keep an endowment's permanent-capital logic separate from a program budget's year-to-year grant logic. Given the finding above that Bucket's most-relevant institutional funders (Wikimedia, Our World in Data) have actively distanced themselves from crypto association, structurally separating "who funds the endowment" from "who funds the free-to-read mission" the way Wikimedia does is the most directly actionable governance move available.

**What the record does not support.** Nothing in this evidence base supports a claim that a citation-fee or micropayment mechanism has been proven at nonprofit-sustaining scale anywhere in the analog set; the largest confirmed figure for a directly comparable mechanism is ResearchHub's $1.5M lifetime distributed. Nothing supports treating crypto funders as reputationally neutral to the Hewlett/Gates/Arcadia/Simons/Sloan funder class; the two most relevant analogs in this set moved explicitly away from crypto. And nothing in the university-hosted OER cluster (five separate orgs) supports building free content infrastructure as a fully standalone entity without some form of institutional host subsidy, since none of the five successful examples did that.
