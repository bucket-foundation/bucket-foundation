## Implications for Bucket

**Paid-to-cite and the open-access movement.** The Budapest Open Access Initiative (2002) and Plan S both define open access as free of charge and free of most reuse restrictions. Peter Suber's own framing separates "gratis" OA (free to read) from "libre" OA (free to reuse, typically via CC-BY). Citing a work is a form of reuse. A model where citing triggers a micropayment to the author is compatible with gratis OA but breaks from the libre-OA, CC-BY default that Plan S funders and most university libraries now expect from anything they fund. OA veterans (Suber, Brembs, the cOAlition S/SPARC constituency) have spent two decades removing exactly this kind of toll at the point of reuse, on the grounds that even a small fee excludes the poorest institutions and readers and creates a new rent layer that tends to get captured by whoever operates the payment rail, in Bucket's case Story Protocol, x402, and Bucket itself, rather than by legacy publishers. Expect skepticism or direct pushback from the mainstream institutional OA world unless Bucket draws an explicit line: free to cite for scholarly, noncommercial reuse; fees reserved for a narrower category such as commercial reuse or AI-training ingestion. DeSci Labs, ResearchHub, and Story Protocol sit in a smaller, more crypto-literate wing of open science that is already experimenting with paid citation and would likely be receptive; they are not representative of the broader OA community Bucket also needs.

**Prior micropayment-for-content efforts.** None of the reader-facing micropayment systems in the prompt's list reached durable, mainstream scale, and the pattern of failure is consistent. Flattr (2010-2022) relied on voluntary tipping and hit a collective-action problem: most users never funded a wallet. Brave BAT achieved real browser-install scale but never became a working pay-per-article system; token volatility undermined it as a stable unit of account. Coil/Web Monetization (2018-2023) needed simultaneous critical mass on both the paying and publishing sides and depended on a single funder (Ripple); Coil shut down in 2023 though the W3C spec persists with reduced momentum. Scroll (2019-2022) found a small niche before Twitter acquired and shut it down. Blendle (2014-) discovered that a per-article buy decision imposes a mental transaction cost readers dislike, and pivoted away from true micropayments toward subscription bundling around 2019-2020. Medium's Partner Program runs an opaque, pool-based pay-per-read system writers widely describe as unpredictable and low-paying. Substack is the closest thing to a winner in this list, and it succeeds by avoiding micropayments entirely in favor of recurring subscriptions and direct reader relationships. Mirror.xyz and Paragraph found real but small usage confined to crypto-native writer audiences and both contracted sharply after the 2022 downturn. Farcaster tipping is live and functioning today but at a scale of low hundreds of thousands of crypto-native users, a rounding error against mainstream platforms.

The consistent diagnosis, going back to Clay Shirky's 2000 critique of micropayments, is that human readers incur a real cognitive cost every time they are asked to make a priced, deliberate purchase decision, and that cost, not the price itself, is what has sunk most of these systems. Bucket's design differs in one respect worth taking seriously: the payment trigger in its model is an AI agent citing or quoting a work, not a human reader deciding to pay. That removes Shirky's core objection. It also means Bucket's real adoption risk is not "will students pay to read" (they never do; the model is free to read) but "will AI companies, agent builders, and eventually institutions route citation spend through x402 and register IP through Story Protocol," which remains unproven outside crypto-native developer circles.

**Do Story Protocol and x402 have credible adoption.** x402 shows real, growing usage: as of August 2026 the network reports 75.41M transactions and $24.24M in volume across roughly 94,060 buyers and 22,000 sellers, governed under the Linux Foundation with Alchemy, AWS, Cloudflare, Stripe, and Vercel as listed partners, a credible signal it is moving from a single-company experiment toward a neutral standard. The average transaction size, near $0.32, is consistent with agent-to-API micropayments rather than consumer content purchases, which supports the read that x402's early traction is machine-initiated, not human-reader-initiated. Story Protocol has a live mainnet, launched February 2025, and real funding from established crypto VCs (a16z crypto, Polychain Capital, and others; totals reported in the tens to low hundreds of millions of dollars depending on round, later-round figures are **unverified** against a fetched primary source in this research pass). Neither protocol shows confirmed adoption yet by a mainstream academic publisher, university press, research funder, or citation-infrastructure incumbent such as Crossref, arXiv, or OpenAlex. Both are real, working, and growing within crypto-native and AI-agent developer circles; neither has crossed into the scholarly-publishing or K-12-education market Bucket is actually built for. That gap is the central unproven assumption in Bucket's plan.

**Five people to contact first, with a specific ask.**

1. **Jason Priem and Heather Piwowar (OurResearch/OpenAlex).** Ask whether OpenAlex would ingest or mirror Bucket-graph citation relationships as open CC0 records, so Bucket becomes discoverable through the bibliographic layer researchers already use instead of building a second, parallel index.

2. **Ed Pentz or current Crossref leadership** (current CEO title is **unverified** in this pass; contact via crossref.org). Ask whether Bucket-graph citable objects, axioms and accepted student productions, can register through Crossref DOIs or a compatible persistent identifier, so citation counts and fee triggers piggyback on infrastructure that already exists rather than requiring a Bucket-only citation graph.

3. **Philipp Koellinger (DeSci Labs).** Ask for a direct, candid postmortem on what has and hasn't worked in DeSci Labs' IP-NFT and data-monetization model, before Bucket repeats the same mistakes on a K-12 audience far less crypto-literate than DeSci's existing user base.

4. **Erik Reppel or the Coinbase CDP x402 team.** Ask whether Bucket's projected fee structure, fractions of a cent per citation, agent-triggered, matches the transaction pattern x402 is actually seeing, and request early access or design review on subscription, refund, and dispute-handling features before production infrastructure commits to the rail.

5. **Liv Marte Nordhaug (Digital Public Goods Alliance).** Ask what Bucket would need to document or change to qualify for DPG Registry listing, since a nonprofit, MIT/CC0, education-focused knowledge graph is close to the DPG Standard's profile, and registry listing is a credible, non-crypto-native discovery and legitimacy channel Bucket currently lacks.

**A sixth name worth adding outside the top five, before any public launch language:** Peter Suber. Not for a partnership ask, but to pressure-test, from the sharpest available source, whether Bucket's "free-to-read, paid-to-cite" framing can honestly claim alignment with the open-access movement, or whether it should be described instead as a distinct, adjacent model that borrows OA's free-to-read half without claiming OA's libre-reuse half.
