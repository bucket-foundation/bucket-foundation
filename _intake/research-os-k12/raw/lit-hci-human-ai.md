## Verification notes

Pirolli and Card's 2005 sensemaking paper is real and widely cited, but it is a conference-proceedings paper from a venue, the International Conference on Intelligence Analysis, that does not carry a DOI in Crossref or OpenAlex, and no arXiv copy exists. Cite it through a library or PARC technical-report copy, not a bare identifier.

Metacademy was a web tool, not a peer-reviewed publication; treat it as prior art in a product-landscape section, not as a literature finding.

"Marco," as named in the brief, does not match any paper found. If this refers to a specific internal tool or an author's working title, get the correct name or a link before citing it; nothing here should stand in for it.

## Design principles for the find, quote, check, organize workspace

**Quote-with-provenance cards.** Every quote a student pulls into a claim should carry a position-sensitive link into its source, the page or paragraph, not just the document, following ScholarPhi and Semantic Reader. The card should show the surrounding sentence for context, following Scim's finding that dense-sentence highlighting preserves comprehension, and it should carry the source's own citation history if the student has seen it before, following CiteSee.

**Claim-evidence-warrant scaffolds.** The organize step should force a claim into three parts: the claim sentence, the quote that supports it, and a rationale linking the two, the SciFact schema. ALCE and CiteME both show that citation presence alone does not mean support; the rationale field is what makes support checkable rather than assumed.

**Disagreement surfacing.** When two sources conflict on a claim, show both excerpts side by side rather than picking one, following Synergi and NewsSense. This is the single interface pattern in this review with direct evidence of improving synthesis quality over a linear reading interface, and it doubles as a natural entry point for teaching students that sources disagree and evidence has to be weighed, not looked up.

**Show me the source affordances.** Any AI-suggested source, definition, or citation needs an expand-in-place control that pulls the actual document text, following Qlarify's incremental-expansion pattern, rather than a link that leaves the workspace. A citation the system cannot resolve to a fetched document should never render as a citation; Bhattacharyya et al.'s fabrication-rate findings and the Kalai-Vempala hallucination proof both argue this has to be a hard rule, not a quality target.

**Teacher dashboards that expose process, not only scores.** Follow Lumilo: show which atoms a student is stuck on and why, not a mastery percentage. Verbert et al.'s dashboard survey found most tools skip validating that a shown metric predicts an outcome; every signal on a teacher dashboard needs that validation before it ships, and Bodily and Verbert's review of the wider dashboard literature found mixed evidence that dashboards change teacher behavior at all, so a new dashboard signal should be piloted with a load and usefulness measure, not assumed useful because it is visible.

**A hint budget instead of a binary answer gate.** CodeAid and Bastani both show that a system restricted from full answers still needs to give something when a student is stuck, a pseudocode sketch, a narrowed search term, or the system risks abandoning a struggling learner at exactly the moment scaffolding theory says support matters most. Define a graded hint ladder for the find step, not a single lookup-or-nothing toggle.

**Turn-legible authorship.** Following CoAuthor's logging granularity and the AI ghostwriter effect finding, every AI-touched sentence in a student production should stay visibly marked as AI-suggested until the student edits it, at which point the marking clears. This keeps the felt-versus-claimed authorship gap Draxler et al. documented from reaching a paid, graph-accepted production.

**Fading initiative across learner states.** Following Molenaar's hybrid-regulation framework, the system's initiative level, how much it suggests versus waits, should decrease as a student's state on a given atom moves from Access toward Production. A student at Internalization should be doing more of the search and organizing themselves than a student at Access.

## Overlap map

**Evidence extraction.** Fully shareable. The same pipeline that pulls slots, claim, method, population, effect size, from a research paper for the hypothesis engine can pull slots, definition, example, source, from a K-12 source for a Concept Atom. PaperMage-style structured extraction, confirmed in this search as a real, maintained toolkit, is a candidate shared backend for both.

**Claim linking.** Shareable machinery, different target graph. The research engine links claims across a paper corpus for novelty and placement hypotheses; the student workspace links claims to Concept Atoms in a prerequisite graph. Both are retrieval-plus-matching problems over an embedding space; the matching model and infrastructure can be one system serving two graphs.

**Belief scoring.** Shareable computation, must differ in what it is used for. Subjective-logic belief combination across multiple evidence items works the same way whether the evidence supports a research hypothesis or a student's inferred mastery of an atom. It must not be exposed to a K-12 student as a single opaque number the way it might be shown to a research team; Verbert et al.'s dashboard findings and the concept-map literature both argue for exposing the evidence behind a score, not the score alone, to a learner. Route on it; do not display it as a grade.

**Critic and judge roles.** Shareable pattern, must be scoped down for minors. In the research engine, critic and judge can challenge and reject a hypothesis with authority. In the student workspace, the critic role is limited to checking whether a quote supports a claim, following SciFact's label-plus-rationale schema, and disagreement it finds routes to a human teacher rather than to an autonomous judge resolving it. This follows Holstein and Aleven's complementarity argument directly: the AI's job is to make a discrepancy visible, the teacher's job is to decide what it means.

**Generator role.** Not shareable. The Virtual Lab and the AI Scientist both put a generator in the loop that proposes new hypotheses or experiments; giving a K-12 student a hypothesis-generating AI is functionally answer generation and breaks the core design constraint. If a generator role exists anywhere near the student workspace, it operates behind the scenes on the organization's own research pipeline, synthesizing patterns across accepted student productions, and never appears as a chat partner to a student.

**Provenance envelopes.** Fully shareable, and higher stakes on the K-12 side. Both systems need a wallet-signed, source-linked record of where a claim came from. The student side adds two things the research side does not need as much: a version a parent or teacher can inspect without technical background, following Druga et al.'s finding that families build AI literacy together informally, and an audit trail robust enough to support a citation-fee payment decision without a dispute, given the fabrication-rate evidence from the citation-verification literature.

**Sequencing.** Partial overlap. The hypothesis engine's sequence hypotheses and Research OS's frontier-backward routing are both ordering problems over a graph, but pedagogical sequencing has to fold in learner-state and scaffolding theory, Novak, Nesbit and Adesope, van de Pol et al. on scaffolding, that the research engine has no equivalent for. Reuse the graph-traversal code; do not reuse the ordering objective function as-is.

## Open research questions

1. Does a find-quote-check-organize-only workspace produce learning gains equivalent to open-ended AI tutoring, holding time on task constant. Study: RCT comparing the constrained workspace against an unrestricted chat tutor and a no-AI control, delayed unaided post-test, extending Bastani et al.'s paradigm from math problem sets to reading and synthesis tasks.

2. Does a mandatory check step before a claim enters the graph change downstream critical-thinking transfer compared to unchecked note-taking. Study: between-group comparison with a validated transfer-task battery at a delay of weeks, addressing the correlational limitation of Gerlich 2025 with a genuine manipulation.

3. Does disagreement surfacing increase engagement with primary sources compared to silent conflict resolution. Study: log-based comparison, treatment shows conflicting excerpts side by side, control shows one resolved excerpt, outcome is click-through to source and claim quality.

4. How well does the system's inferred learner state, Access through Production, predict independently scored mastery. Study: concurrent-validity study correlating system state against an external assessment, following the validation gap Verbert et al. identify in most dashboard metrics.

5. Does a process-exposing teacher dashboard change intervention patterns and student outcomes compared to a scores-only dashboard, without raising teacher cognitive load past a usable threshold. Study: classroom quasi-experiment following Holstein et al.'s co-design methodology, with a load measure alongside a usefulness measure.

6. Does the AI ghostwriter effect appear when the organize step auto-drafts a claim sentence from a quote. Study: authorship-perception survey, following Draxler et al.'s measures, comparing auto-drafted and self-drafted claim sentences in a K-12 sample.

7. What level of system initiative in the find step is appropriate at different ages. Study: developmental comparison across age bands manipulating initiative level, outcome is search efficacy and self-reported frustration.

8. Does a citation fee for accepted productions change citation sincerity compared to a badge-only incentive. Study: field experiment comparing micro-payment, badge, and no-incentive arms on citation accuracy and fabrication rate.

9. Do belief scores calibrated for the research hypothesis engine, repurposed for student routing, produce miscalibrated confidence in students. Study: calibration study comparing system belief score, self-reported confidence, and actual performance across the five learner states.

10. Does restricting the critic role to checking, with no fix supplied, change error-correction speed and retention compared to a critic that also supplies the fix. Study: 2x2 comparing check-only and check-plus-fix conditions against a timed task and a delayed retention test, extending CodeAid's classroom methodology.

11. Do children over-trust a provenance card's verified badge the way they over-trust a conversational agent's warmth. Study: think-aloud protocol with children evaluating true and fabricated provenance cards, extending Kurian's empathy-gap concern to a non-conversational UI.

12. Does exposing belief-score components and critic disagreement to teachers, rather than a final score alone, produce better-calibrated teacher trust in the system. Study: survey and interview study manipulating dashboard detail level, outcome is trust calibration against system accuracy.

13. Do children as young as eight and teens sixteen and older generate materially different interface requirements for a shared find-quote-check-organize workspace. Study: cooperative-inquiry sessions across age bands, following Druin's method, thematic comparison of requirements.

14. Does system-routed frontier-backward entry increase or decrease the ownership gap documented by Draxler et al., compared to a self-directed entry point. Study: between-group comparison on perceived ownership and learning gain.

15. Does a paid, citeable production model shift a student's identity from consumer to contributor over a school year, and does that shift predict later voluntary engagement with primary sources. Study: longitudinal survey with log-based behavioral tracking, pre and post identity scale, one-year follow-up on voluntary source use.

## What to change

**Add a hint budget.** The pure no-answer-generation rule is supported by Bastani et al.'s finding that withholding direct answers protects learning, but the same study shows a Socratic-hint mode outperformed both full generation and bare withholding. A workspace with only lookup and no scaffold for a stuck student risks the frustration-without-learning failure mode that the scaffolding literature, van de Pol et al., Nesbit and Adesope, warns against. Define a graded hint ladder, not a binary.

**Ground the five learner states before routing on them.** Access, Awareness, Understanding, Internalization, and Production do not have a direct evidence base in the literature reviewed here. The closest validated analogues, concept-map propositional accuracy and mastery-level frameworks in the learning-analytics literature, have decades of psychometric work behind them that this taxonomy does not yet have. Run the concurrent-validity study above before building automated routing decisions on top of it, or explicitly map each state to an existing validated construct.

**Pilot the teacher dashboard with a load measure, not only a usefulness survey.** Holstein's Lumilo work supports process exposure, but Bodily and Verbert's review of the wider dashboard literature found mixed evidence that dashboards change behavior at all, and a process-rich view can raise teacher cognitive load without raising effective intervention. Be ready to simplify if teachers report it as overwhelming.

**Stop citing critical-thinking decline as settled.** Gerlich 2025 is correlational, self-report, cross-sectional, and carries a published correction. The better-supported, narrower finding from Lee et al. 2025 is that self-reported effort drops with confidence in AI output and that effort shifts toward verification rather than disappearing. Design for that: make the check step effortful and visible, rather than building the product narrative around a critical-thinking crisis the strongest study available does not show.

**Never display a raw belief score to a student.** Keep belief scores probabilistic and teacher-visible with their evidence components attached. Displaying a single number to a K-12 user as if it were a grade invites the gaming and anxiety patterns documented across the dashboard literature, and it drops exactly the transparency that makes a belief score defensible in the first place.

**Match the citation-fee gate's verification rigor to the incentive it creates.** Paying for accepted productions imports a reward that the citation-verification literature, SciFact, ALCE, CiteME, the fabrication-rate studies, shows produces errors under time or reward pressure even from careful adults. The accept-or-reject decision needs the same rationale-labeled, evidence-linked judgment SciFact uses, plus an appeals mechanism, because nothing in this review suggests children resist a legible reward less than adults do; Yip et al.'s finding that co-design sessions default toward whoever controls the structure suggests the opposite is more likely.
