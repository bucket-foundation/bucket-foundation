# Research OS Literature Review

Forty nine sources below, organized by the categories in the brief. Every DOI was checked against the Crossref API and resolved to the stated title, authors, and venue; the two arXiv entries were checked against the arXiv API. One item (Dillenbourg 1999) is marked unverified because no resolving DOI could be located; its title, venue, and year are well established bibliographic facts, but the identifier check failed.

## Synthesis by Design Choice

**Five learner states (Access, Awareness, Understanding, Internalization, Production).** No cited paper tests this exact five-state model, so it has to be read against its two closest relatives. Biggs and Collis's SOLO taxonomy (1982) already provides a validated, five-level structural complexity ladder, and Chi and Wylie's ICAP framework (2014, tested by Menekse et al. 2013) provides a four-mode engagement ordering with real classroom evidence behind its predicted ordering. Both existing frameworks are more validated than a new five-state scheme starting from zero. The KLI framework (Koedinger, Corbett, and Perfetti 2012) adds a further constraint: different knowledge types (memory, induction, sense-making) need different learning events, so a single review mechanism tuned for one state will not suit all five. The states should be built as a mapping onto SOLO and ICAP rather than a parallel invention, with the mapping tested empirically (see open question 15 below) before the five-state architecture is treated as settled.

**Frontier-backward routing.** This has real support. Schwartz and Bransford (1998) found that encountering the hard case before the explanation improves later learning from that explanation. Kapur (2008, 2014) found the same pattern for unsupported struggle before instruction, with effect sizes concentrated in conceptual understanding and transfer, not procedural fluency. Schwartz and Martin (2004) add the necessary condition: invention or struggle only pays off "when it is followed by a learning resource." Frontier-backward routing without a working backward path to prerequisite content is the exact null condition that paper found ineffective. Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark (2006) is the field's standing counterargument: unguided exploration overloads novices. The synthesis is that frontier-backward routing is supported when it functions as structured productive failure (a hard question, a genuine attempt, then a guided backward path to explanation), and unsupported when it functions as pure discovery learning with the backward path optional or weak.

**Constrained AI (find, quote, check, organize, no answer generation).** This is the best-evidenced single design choice in the whole system. Bastani et al. (2025) directly demonstrates the mechanism: unrestricted GPT-4 access improved practice performance by 48 percent but cut unassisted exam performance by 17 percent, and a guardrail that provided hints instead of answers eliminated the harm while keeping most of the practice gain (127 percent practice improvement, no significant exam harm). Wang et al. (2024) shows the same principle on the tutor side: an AI that pushed tutors toward guiding questions and away from giving answers raised student mastery by 4 to 9 percentage points. The risk is going too far in the other direction. Kestin et al. (2025) and VanLehn (2011) both show that a generative, explaining AI tutor can match or beat human tutoring and beat an active-learning classroom, a benefit a pure find-quote-check-organize tool with zero explanatory capacity forgoes. The evidence supports restricting the AI from generating final answers, and does not support restricting it from generating guiding questions, hints, or Socratic prompts, the category Bastani's guardrail condition and VanLehn's step-based feedback both used to get their gains.

**Student productions earning citation payments.** No cited paper tests a payment incentive on top of student-generated content. The closest indirect evidence is mixed on the mechanism payments are meant to reward. Chi et al. (1989, 1994) and Kapur (2008, 2014) all find that the value of student-generated work comes from the process, self-explanation quality, number and diversity of solution attempts, not from the output's polish or correctness. A payment mechanism tied to whether a production is "accepted into the graph" risks optimizing for output quality over the struggle process the underlying learning-science literature credits with the transfer benefit. De Simone et al.'s Nigeria RCT (2025) adds an equity flag: the same AI-tutoring program produced its largest gains for already-higher-performing students, a pattern a payment-and-acceptance gate could reproduce or worsen if acceptance criteria favor students who arrive with stronger prior skills.

**Teacher class view.** Hattie and Timperley (2007) and Black and Wiliam (1998) are strong, well-replicated support for a real-time teacher dashboard, provided it surfaces the right kind of information. Black and Wiliam's effect sizes (0.4 to 0.7 SD) are among the largest documented in education research, and they are specific to formative, process-level information used during instruction, not summary grades reviewed afterward. Hattie and Timperley's finding that task and process feedback outperforms feedback about the self or bare outcome feedback means a dashboard limited to mastery percentages would be using the weaker of the two feedback types this literature distinguishes.

**No peer layer.** This is the design choice with the least support in the cited literature and the clearest counterargument. Kirschner, Paas, and Kirschner (2009) find that collaboration's cognitive-load benefit is concentrated in high complexity, high element-interactivity tasks, precisely the description of a frontier question. Dillenbourg (1999, DOI unverified) adds the caveat that grouping alone does not guarantee the benefit; it requires designed task structure. The evidence base says a peer layer would help most exactly where Research OS currently has none, at the frontier, and would help least at the review and recall layer, where FSRS and retrieval practice (Roediger and Karpicke 2006) already work well solo.

## Open Research Questions

1. Do the five learner states form a single ordered latent trait, the way SOLO and ICAP do, or several independent dimensions? Study design: administer state-classification items across many Concept Atoms to a large sample, fit a Rasch or IRT model, and test unidimensionality and consistent item ordering.
2. Does frontier-backward routing improve delayed retention and motivation over standard forward, bottom-up sequencing when matched on content and time-on-task? Study design: RCT comparing frontier-backward versus forward progression on the same Concept Atoms, with a delayed post-test and a motivation measure as primary outcomes.
3. Does a find-quote-check-organize AI avoid the harm profile Bastani et al. documented for unrestricted AI, and does it retain any of the benefit Wang et al. and Kestin et al. documented for explanatory AI? Study design: three-arm RCT (constrained AI, generative explaining AI, no AI), measuring unassisted post-test performance after tool withdrawal.
4. Does a citation payment change the self-explanation and struggle behaviors Chi and Kapur found predictive of transfer, for better or worse? Study design: between-subjects experiment with no payment, credential-only, and citation-payment conditions on the same production task, coding process quality and downstream transfer.
5. What share of accepted student productions contains net-new synthesis versus restated source material, and does that share change by grade band? Study design: blind expert coding of a sample of accepted productions against their cited sources, replicated across grade bands.
6. Does teacher access to state-level class data change grouping and intervention decisions in ways that reproduce Black and Wiliam's formative-assessment effect sizes? Study design: classrooms randomized to dashboard access versus standard gradebook, with unit assessment scores and logged teacher decisions as outcomes.
7. Does adding a peer layer to high-complexity frontier tasks specifically (not low-complexity recall tasks) produce the collaborative benefit Kirschner, Paas, and Kirschner predict? Study design: within-subject comparison of solo versus paired work, crossed with task complexity, testing for the predicted interaction.
8. Do FSRS review intervals need to differ by learner state, since Recall and Production plausibly have different forgetting curves? Study design: manipulate review-interval parameters by state and compare long-term retention curves.
9. Does routing based on population-average prerequisite mastery create silent gaps that individualized estimates (per Pardos and Heffernan) would catch? Study design: simulation and field comparison of routing decisions under population-average versus individualized mastery estimates, tracking downstream frontier-question failure rates.
10. Does the expertise reversal effect apply across the five states, such that scaffolds helpful at Access or Awareness slow learners at Internalization or Production? Study design: vary scaffold intensity crossed with pre-assessed state, measuring time-on-task and error rate for the predicted crossover interaction.
11. How often do students route around the answer-generation restriction through repeated "check" queries that function as indirect answer requests? Study design: log-based classification of AI-interaction transcripts for leakage patterns, adapting Wang et al.'s message-coding method.
12. Does the sourcing and lateral-reading skill built inside the constrained AI workspace transfer to unscaffolded, open-web research? Study design: transfer test on an open-web source-evaluation task after in-platform training, benchmarked against McGrew's (2020) explicit-instruction control condition.
13. What minimum engagement rate with the AI tool is needed before frontier-backward routing produces a detectable learning gain, given Oreopoulos and Low's finding that low Khanmigo engagement neutralized its benefit? Study design: dose-response field study correlating logged AI engagement frequency with state-transition rates.
14. Does citation payment eligibility change who participates, by prior achievement or socioeconomic status, in a way that widens or narrows gaps? Study design: track production submission and acceptance rates by baseline achievement quartile and demographic subgroup over a semester.
15. Do transitions between adjacent Research OS states show the same engagement-mode ordering ICAP predicts, and do they correspond to SOLO's structural levels? Study design: administer ICAP-coded activities and SOLO-scored responses to the same learners alongside state assessments, and correlate.

## What to Change

1. Do not let frontier-backward routing run as unsupported discovery. Pair it with a guaranteed, working backward path to explanation (per Schwartz and Martin's "only when followed by a learning resource" finding), or it risks the failure mode Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark documented.
2. Give the "check" function room to ask guiding questions, not only verify claims. The RCTs that worked, Bastani's guardrailed condition, VanLehn's step-based feedback, Wang et al.'s coaching prompts, all used hints and questions, not pure verification.
3. Reconsider the no-peer-layer decision specifically at the frontier shell. The cognitive-load evidence for collaboration is strongest exactly where Research OS's task complexity is highest.
4. Do not launch citation payments without a no-payment control arm. The literature the payment mechanism is meant to build on (self-explanation, productive failure) never tested a financial incentive, and incentives are known to change what gets optimized.
5. Build the teacher dashboard around process signals (state transitions, attempt diversity, self-explanation quality), not a mastery percentage alone, to land in the effective half of Hattie and Timperley's feedback-type distinction.
6. Treat AI engagement rate as a primary design metric, not an assumption. Oreopoulos and Low's well-resourced Khanmigo deployment produced near-zero net benefit specifically because of low voluntary use; a coaching-style AI that students do not open will not help regardless of its design quality.
