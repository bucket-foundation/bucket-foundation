# Market gap or graveyard

Five companies map closest to Bucket's "knowledge graph plus citation payments" thesis. Four of the five are dead or absorbed.

**Knewton (2008-2019).** Raised $157.5M across six rounds from Accel, Bessemer, FirstMark, Founders Fund, Atomico, GSV, Sofina. Built an adaptive-learning engine that personalized STEM content in real time and produced strong outcome data: at Arizona State University, course withdrawal fell from 13% to 6% and pass rates rose from 66% to 75%. Sold to Wiley in May 2019 for under $17M, a fraction of capital raised. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knewton) The lesson: outcome evidence this strong still could not carry the company past a publisher-integration business into venture-scale returns. Distribution into existing course materials, which Wiley already owned, turned out to matter more than the adaptive engine itself.

**Kno (2009-2013).** Unverified this session; no reachable Wikipedia page, and the company site is gone. Recollection, flagged low-confidence: founded by Osman Rashid (also a Chegg co-founder) and Babur Habib, raised well over $100M from investors including Kleiner Perkins, Andreessen Horowitz, GSV and Intel Capital, pivoted from a dual-screen tablet device to a digital-textbook software platform, then sold to Intel in 2013 for a price reported well under total capital raised. Check before citing. If the recollection holds, the pattern matches Knewton: heavy capital into content and device infrastructure, an exit far below invested capital.

**Boundless (2011-2017).** Raised a $1.7M seed (2011) and an $8M Series A (April 2012). Built free digital textbooks from open educational resources. Sued by Pearson, Cengage, and Macmillan's Bedford Freeman imprint in March 2012 for copying "the distinctive selection, arrangement, and presentation" of their textbooks; settled confidentially in December 2013. Acquired by Valore in April 2015; Valore was acquired by Follett in November 2016; the Boundless collection was archived into Lumen Learning by September 2017, ending independent operation. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundless_(company)) The lesson: an open-content aggregation model can draw a direct legal attack from incumbent publishers over structure and arrangement alone, and can keep dying slowly even after settling that fight. Four years passed between the settlement and the final wind-down.

**Metacademy.** Unverified this session; no reachable Wikipedia page. Recollection, low confidence: a crowdsourced, graph-structured guide to self-teaching technical subjects, built by academic contributors rather than a funded startup, that stopped active development from a lack of sustained resourcing rather than a competitive loss. If the recollection holds, the lesson differs from the venture failures above: a knowledge graph built on volunteer contribution with no revenue mechanism can wind down quietly from maintenance burden alone. That is a direct risk to any plan assuming volunteer or lightly incentivized contributors will keep a large graph current.

**Ambient Insight.** Unverified this session; no reachable Wikipedia page. Recollection, medium confidence: an edtech market-research and analyst firm, not a product company, that tracked global edtech investment data for years before its founder wound the firm down around 2018-2019. Relevant less as a company failure than as a symptom: the firm paid to track this market's money concluded there was more value in stopping than continuing.

**Is "knowledge graph across all subjects with citation payments" a gap or a graveyard?** Both, split across the two halves of the claim. The "knowledge graph across all subjects, structured by tier and learner state" half has no funded precedent at Bucket's stated scope, either successful or failed. The nearest precedents, Knewton, Carnegie Learning's MATHia, Amira, all stopped at one subject. The nearest all-subjects precedent in spirit, Boundless, an open-content aggregation project, was sued into a forced sale within four years on structure-and-arrangement grounds unrelated to the graph idea itself. That is a genuine gap: nobody funded in this list has tried to build and sustain an all-subjects mastery graph long enough to report a result either way.

The "citation triggers a payment to the author" half is not attempted anywhere in this survey, in K-12 or in the research-tool cluster closest to it in spirit. Scite, Elicit, Consensus, and Undermind all monetize the citation-surfacing layer through subscriptions; none pays the cited author. Scite, the company built most directly on citation-relationship data, was absorbed into a larger public company (Research Solutions) rather than scaling independently, a real caution against assuming citation-centric data carries an obvious independent business model, even before adding a K-12 audience or a Base/x402 payment rail nobody in this survey uses.

Read together: the graph-scope idea is unproven rather than disproven, because nobody has run the experiment at Bucket's scope. The payment-to-author mechanism has no evidence base at all in either K-12 or research tooling. The base rate for novel monetization mechanics layered onto content infrastructure in this exact sector, Knewton, Kno, Boundless, is poor enough that the payment rail should be treated as the riskiest single component of the plan, ahead of the graph.

# Implications for Bucket

**Where the design is differentiated.** The rule that AI in the student workspace may only find, quote, check and organize, never generate the answer directly, has no matching product commitment anywhere in this survey. Chegg, Course Hero, Brainly and Quizlet all started closer to "find and check" and drifted toward "generate the answer" because that behavior drove engagement, and all four now live with a free general-purpose chatbot doing the generate-the-answer job for nothing. If Bucket holds the find/quote/check/organize line at the product level and enforces it in a way students cannot route around, that line is close to the only durable moat available against a frontier lab shipping a free general tutor. Tutoring quality itself is not a race Bucket can win against OpenAI, Anthropic, Google or Microsoft.

The nonprofit, open-source, publish-everything structure is a second real advantage, not just a fundraising constraint. Every strong outcome claim in the personalized/mastery-learning category surveyed here, Knewton in the 2010s and Alpha School/2 Hour Learning in the 2020s, was built on internal data that independent researchers never verified, and both drew that exact criticism from regulators or the press. A nonprofit that publishes its graph, its learner-state transition data, and its outcome studies in the open can answer that criticism in a way a venture-backed competitor is structurally unable to, since a venture-backed competitor's outcome data is usually a sales asset it keeps closed to outside audit.

**Where the design is naive.** Nothing in the venture materials describes a teacher- or district-facing distribution motion, and every K-12 company in this survey that reached real scale reached it through teachers or districts. Brisk's "10,000+ districts," Newsela's "75% of American classrooms," and Carnegie Learning's multi-decade district-contract base are the traction numbers that actually moved in this table. The consumer-direct companies, Brainly, Quizlet, Course Hero, Chegg, are also the ones now most exposed to free-chatbot commoditization. A "free to any learner worldwide" framing, with no paired teacher or district channel, points Bucket toward the group in this survey with the worst outcomes.

The five-state learner model, Access, Awareness, Understanding, Internalization, Production, is original to Bucket and does not map onto any framework used by the surveyed companies. Amira builds on Science-of-Reading research, Carnegie Learning's MATHia builds on ACT-R cognitive-tutor theory, and Knewton built on item-response-theory proficiency estimates; each had a citable external research base under its state model before, or while, building the product. None of the companies with a proprietary state taxonomy, Knewton included, turned that taxonomy into a lasting moat. Where a moat existed at all, it came from district integration and trust, not from taxonomy design. Five original learner states with no cited external validation is a legitimate design choice, but it is the single most untested claim in the plan, and this evidence base says taxonomy originality is not where this market's winners actually won.

**What the evidence suggests should change first.** Narrow before widening: prove the graph-plus-citation-payment loop end to end in one subject and one grade band with a real classroom before claiming all-subjects scope, since the nearest all-subjects precedent, Boundless, did not survive four years past its first legal fight, and no funded company has demonstrated an all-subjects graph can be built and kept current at all, let alone monetized. Build or partner into a teacher-facing entry point instead of relying on a student-direct workspace to find its own distribution, since that is the one variable every scaled company in this survey shares and every unscaled or declining one lacks. Treat every frontier lab's free education offer, Khanmigo, Claude for Education's 50,000-seat Northeastern rollout, Gemini for Education, Copilot for Education, as the tutoring-quality floor every user will compare Bucket against for free, and put the differentiation weight entirely on the graph, the citation ledger and the find/quote/check constraint. And publish outcome data earlier and more rigorously than Knewton or Alpha School did, since unverified internal outcome claims are the most repeated weak point across the mastery-learning category in this survey, and it is the one place Bucket's nonprofit structure gives it room to do better.
