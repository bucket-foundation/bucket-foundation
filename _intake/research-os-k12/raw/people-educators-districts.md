## Implications for Bucket

**What practitioners say they need, and what they reject.** Across the teacher-influencer tier (Davis, Clark, Burns, Miller, Keeler, Curts) and the state-level guidance documents (Washington, Oregon, Ohio, North Carolina), a consistent pattern holds: practitioners want AI positioned as an assistant to a task they still own, not a replacement for the task. Washington's language is "human-centered," Oregon's is "relational trust," Peninsula School District's is "humans in the loop," Monica Burns's is "student thinking at the center." Davis's shift from AI detection to "learning detection" and her September 2026 piece on fact-checking AI outputs with an ensemble method both point at the same need: tools that help a teacher or student verify claims rather than tools that produce claims for them to grade. What gets rejected, consistently, is AI positioned as the instructor. Alpha School draws the sharpest public criticism on the list (Guardian, CNN, Scientific American all ran skeptical 2025-2026 pieces) precisely because it markets AI as the teacher. Khanmigo's own usage data, only 15 percent of students with access use it, suggests that even a well-funded, well-reviewed AI tutor does not automatically get adopted when it competes for a student's attention rather than assisting a task the student already has to do. Bucket's stated design, AI limited to find, quote, check, organize, sits closer to what practitioners ask for than to what they reject, but that alignment is a claim to test, not a result already demonstrated: no organization on this list has evaluated Bucket, and the state and district actors who most resemble Bucket's philosophy (Washington OSPI, Peninsula SD, Digital Promise's Use/Understand/Evaluate framework) have not been shown Bucket's specific product.

**What the LAUSD AllHere failure and other pilot failures teach about district procurement and trust.** The AllHere case is the strongest and most fully documented failure in this research. LAUSD signed a $6 to $6.2 million, five-year contract with a company founded in 2016, committed $4 million in district funds, launched the "Ed" chatbot on March 20, 2024, and shut it down on June 14, 2024, less than three months later, when AllHere furloughed its staff over financial trouble. A former AllHere engineer alleged the chatbot's data collection likely violated the district's own privacy rules. Nearly two years later, on February 25, 2026, the FBI served a search warrant on then-Superintendent Alberto Carvalho's home and office and separately raided LAUSD headquarters in connection with AllHere, and investigative reporting from The 74 found that AllHere had met with LAUSD leadership months before the contract was awarded. Three lessons follow directly. First, speed of procurement and depth of due diligence trade off against each other, and a district that moves fast on a flashy AI product is a district that has not stress-tested the vendor's operating runway or its data-handling practices. Second, a single point of executive sponsorship (Carvalho championed Ed publicly) creates concentrated reputational and legal risk when the product fails; a governance structure with faculty, IT, and legal sign-off spreads that risk and slows the failure down before launch, not after. Third, and most consequential for any new entrant, districts now treat a novel AI product's collapse as evidence against the category, not just against the vendor. That dynamic is visible in 2026's largest development: both LAUSD and NYC DOE, the two largest school districts in the country, independently imposed broad restrictions or moratoriums on student-facing AI in 2026 (NYT: "Los Angeles District Bans Most A.I. for Students," "New York City Bans A.I. in Elementary and Middle Schools," both reported around September 1, 2026), and NYC's ban explicitly covers pre-K through eighth grade. Houston ISD's "AI slop" parent complaints (September 9, 2026, over errors in AI-generated worksheets) show the same trust erosion happening at the classroom level in a district that is otherwise the most aggressively pro-AI-expansion district on this list. The immediate, countervailing signal is the AFT/UFT/Microsoft "National AI Safety & Privacy Standard," announced September 9, 2026, days after NYC's moratorium, reportedly with Anthropic and OpenAI involved and an AI-training academy attached, which suggests unions and major vendors see the moratorium wave as a threat to be answered with a governance standard rather than a market signal to be obeyed. For Bucket, the practical takeaway is that any pitch to a district or state now has to lead with governance, data handling, and what the AI cannot do, before it leads with what the AI can do, and it has to assume the audience has AllHere and the 2026 moratoriums already in mind.

**Whether school librarians and research-skills instruction are the most natural entry point.** The evidence supports this more directly than any other adoption channel on the list. AASL published a second edition of its National School Library Standards in 2025, is running an active editorial line on AI in Knowledge Quest ("The Case for Using AI as a Co-Librarian," "The Vizzini Problem"), and published a dedicated practitioner book on AI in school libraries in 2025. Iowa City's district-wide AI curriculum has librarians deliver the K-5 AI lessons specifically. National History Day's entire competition structure, grades 6 through 12, is built around students finding, citing, and arguing from primary sources, which is Bucket's find/quote/check/organize loop already running as an unpaid, ungraded national program. None of this proves librarians and research-skills educators will adopt Bucket specifically, and no librarian association has evaluated the product, but the instructional territory Bucket's workspace occupies is territory this community already owns, names, and is actively re-professionalizing around ("Co-Librarian," not "replaced Librarian"). That is a stronger structural match than any teacher-influencer channel, any state guidance document, or any district pilot on this list, and it comes with a built-in credibility asset: librarians are the practitioner group least likely to be accused of chasing an AI trend, which matters given how much of the 2025-2026 record is districts pulling back from AI, not leaning in.

**What the competency-based education movement says about a five-state mastery model.** Aurora Institute, now rebranded Full Scale Learning mid-2026, is the only organization in this research whose stated mission maps onto a tiered mastery model as directly as Bucket's does, and its own recent publishing (a 106-high-school landscape scan of competency-based learning, a piece reframing the "AI replaces teachers" narrative, a rural-schools AI report) shows a movement actively working through the same tension Bucket has to resolve: how much of the progression from novice to mastery can be automated or AI-assisted before the model stops being "competency-based" in the sense the movement means it. The movement's core commitment, that a learner advances on demonstrated mastery rather than seat time, is compatible with Bucket's five states (Access, Awareness, Understanding, Internalization, Production) in structure, but the movement has spent two decades building assessment and moderation systems designed around teacher judgment of mastery, and it is not yet clear from anything published this session how that community would view an AI system, even one restricted to find/quote/check/organize, contributing to a determination of which state a learner is in. The rebrand itself, happening in the same year competency-based education is being pulled in the AI direction from multiple sides, suggests the movement's leadership sees this as a live strategic question rather than a settled one, which is an opening for Bucket to engage them as a design-input partner rather than arriving with a finished model to defend.

**Five to contact first, with a specific ask.**

1. **AASL** (ala.org/aasl). Ask: a working session with the Knowledge Quest editorial team and the authors of "Leveraging AI in School Libraries" to pressure-test Bucket's find/quote/check/organize workspace against actual research-literacy instruction, before any broader pitch. This is the single closest structural match on the list and the lowest-risk first conversation, since it is a request for expert input, not a purchase or endorsement.

2. **Digital Promise** (digitalpromise.org). Ask: an application to the Responsibly Designed AI Certification program, using its Use/Understand/Evaluate framework as the evaluation rubric. A pass here produces a third-party credential Bucket can cite everywhere else on this list, including with state agencies.

3. **Full Scale Learning (Aurora Institute)** (fullscalelearning.org). Ask: a design-partner conversation specifically about how a five-state mastery model should handle AI-assisted evidence toward a mastery determination, framed as contributing to their live rebrand-era strategic question, not as a product pitch.

4. **TeachAI coalition** (teachai.org). Ask: inclusion of Bucket's citation-payment and AI-role-restriction model as a case study in the next round of its "AI Guidance for Schools Toolkit," since TeachAI's toolkit is the template multiple state guidance documents (Ohio, North Carolina) already echo, making it a multiplier rather than a single relationship.

5. **Peninsula School District** (psd401.net) or **Iowa City Community School District** (iowacityschools.org). Ask: a small pilot with one librarian or social-studies team, chosen because both districts have documented, teacher-led AI governance processes with explicit "humans in the loop" commitments, making them realistic low-risk pilot sites rather than districts likely to treat a new AI tool as the next AllHere.

Common Sense Media, AASA, and the state departments of education (Ohio, Washington, California) are reasonable second-wave targets once Bucket has at least one completed pilot and one third-party evaluation to point to; approaching them earlier risks being evaluated against the AllHere and 2026-moratorium backdrop with no evidence base to counter it.
