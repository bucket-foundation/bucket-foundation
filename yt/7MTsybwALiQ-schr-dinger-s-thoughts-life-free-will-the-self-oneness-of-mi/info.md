Summary:

On life, the laws of physics, order and disorder:

- Living matter, compared to inanimate matter: other laws of physics currently unknown

- Life: order to disorder plus order to order (order that is kept up).

- Genetic code: atoms producing order, in tune with each other and the environment

- A 'mechanism' entirely different from the 'probability mechanism' of physics.

On Determinism, Free Will, The Self and Universal Mind:

- Events in the body of a living being which correspond to the activity of its mind are deterministic or statistico-deterministic

- Quantum indeterminacy plays no biologically relevant role in the body events

- Unpleasant feeling about 'declaring oneself to be a pure mechanism'. Contradicts free will

- (i) My body functions as a pure mechanism according to the Laws of Nature.

(ii) Yet I know that I am directing its motions

The only possible inference is that I am the person who controls the 'motion of the atoms' according to the Laws of Nature

- Insight from the Upanishads. The mystics of many centuries, independently, have described the unique experience of his or her life in terms that can be condensed in the phrase: DEUS FACTUS SUM (I have become God).

- To Western ideology the thought has remained a stranger, in spite of Schopenhauer

- Consciousness is never experienced in the plural, only in the singular

- How does the idea of plurality arise at all? Consciousness finds itself intimately connected with, and dependent on, the physical state of a limited region of matter, the body

- Consciousness is a singular of which the plural is unknown; that there is only one thing and that what seems to be a plurality is merely a series of different aspects of this one thing, produced by a deception (the Indian MAJA)

- Each of us has the indisputable impression that the sum total of his own experience and memory forms a unit, quite distinct from that of any other person. What is this 'I? It is just a little bit more than a collection of single data (experiences and memories), namely the canvas upon which they are collected

- The point of view taken here levels with what Aldous Huxley has recently called The Perennial Philosophy

The Principle of Objectivation. On how science has removed Mind from its model of reality:

- The "hypothesis of the real world" around us. We exclude the Subject of Cognizance from the domain of nature that we endeavor to understand

- A moderately satisfying picture of the world has only been reached at the high price of taking ourselves out of the picture

- The astonishment at finding our world picture 'colourless, cold, mute'. Colour and sound, hot and cold are our immediate sensations; small wonder that they are lacking in a world model from which we have removed our own mental person

The Arithmetical Paradox. The Oneness of Mind:

- The reason why our sentient, percipient and thinking ego is met nowhere within our scientific world picture: because it is itself that world picture

- The arithmetical paradox: there appears to be a great multitude of these conscious egos, the world is however only one. The several domains of 'private' consciousness partly overlap. The region common to all where they all overlap is the construct of the 'real world around us'

- Two ways out of the number paradox: 1) Leibniz's monads. 2) The unification of minds or consciousness. Their multiplicity is only apparent, in truth there is only one mind. This is the doctrine of the Upanishads.

- Aldous Huxley: The Perennial Philosophy. Agreement between humans of different race, different religion, knowing nothing about each other's existence, separated by centuries and millenia and great distances

- To Western thought this doctrine has little appeal, it is unpalatable, it is dubbed fantastic, unscientific. It is so because our science - Greek science - is based on objectivation. This is precisely the point where our present way of thinking does need to be amended

Note (Cracking the Nutshell): Using a non-dogmatic, open-minded approach like Schrödinger or Huxley did can be very rewarding and help us look at reality in a different way. While I don't necessarily agree with all of Schrödinger's views, I think that the topics raised are fascinating and a great source of food for thought.
