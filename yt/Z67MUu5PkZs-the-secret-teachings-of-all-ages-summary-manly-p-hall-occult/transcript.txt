The Pythagorean theory of music and
color.
Harmony, said the philosophers, is the
foundation of beauty. A thing is
beautiful only when its parts coexist in
perfect proportion. Good acting
according to its own nature is harmony
manifest. The universe itself is a
hierarchy of good. From the least degree
in matter to the greatest in spirit, and
beauty is the visible form of that
harmony. What man calls evil is merely
the lowest measure of good, discordant
because it reflects the least harmony.
Thus, deformity, whether moral or
physical, is not an opposite of good,
but a distortion of its order. Harmony,
then is the will of the eternal good,
expressing itself through form. The
ancient priests taught that music was
the audible image of this universal
harmony. Hermes said the Egyptians
invented the liar by stretching strings
across a tortoise shell.
And through this instrument revealed how
spirit plays upon matter to create
order. Plato declared that Egyptian
hymns were of divine origin and that
music and poetry preceded all human
civilization.
The liar itself symbolized man, its
body, the flesh, its strings, the nerves
and the musician, the inddwelling
spirit. When the spirit plays purely
upon its instrument, harmony reigns.
When the strings are defiled, discord
appears. disease of body and soul alike.
It was Pythagoras who raised music to
the dignity of a science, uniting it
with mathematics and philosophy. From
observing the tones produced by strings
of differing tension, he discovered that
musical intervals correspond to exact
numerical ratios.
The octave 2:1, the fifth 3:2, the
fourth 4:3, and so on. These ratios
symbolized in the sacred tetractus 1 2 3
4 became the key to all harmonies of
sound form and motion. Number he taught
precedes harmony. It is the divine law
that shapes all worlds.
From this revelation came the doctrine
of the music of the spheres that the
stars and planets moving in proportioned
intervals through the heavens produce a
cosmic symphony inaudible to the profane
but heard by the purified soul. To the
Pythagoreans, the universe was a
monocord. Its upper end fixed in pure
spirit, its lower in dense matter. Each
planet sounded its note according to its
speed and distance. Saturn, the deepest,
the moon the highest, the space between
them forming a perfect octave. The
combined voices of the nine spheres were
said to be the song of the muses, a hymn
to divine order. Pythagoras extended his
discovery to every field. The elements
themselves, earth, water, air, and fire,
he showed to exist in harmonic ratios,
each bearing a diatonic relationship to
the others. In his diagrams, energy
descended in one pyramid, matter
ascended in another, the two meeting in
perfect equilibrium at the sphere of the
sun. Every level of existence vibrated
to a tone in this cosmic chord.
Recognizing the therapeutic power of
harmony, if Pythagoras used music as
medicine, certain modes could calm
anger, dispel melancholy, or restore
mental balance. A friian tune could
incite passion. a spondic melody could
soothe it. He prescribed morning songs
to awaken the intellect and evening
hymns to quiet the emotions.
The Pythagoreans called this musical
medicine, the purification of the soul
through sound.
The Greeks understood music as moral
architecture. Plato warned that changing
a musical mode could shake the
foundations of the state, for rhythm and
tone shaped the character of a people.
Both melodies belong to men, gentle ones
to women. And all music was to en noble,
not flatter the senses. Music was not
amusement. It was the science of
harmony, the education of the soul. From
harmony of sound, the ancients passed
naturally to harmony of color. For both
arise from vibration.
Light, said the wise, is the visible
counterpart of music. The same divine
energy perceived through another sense.
Its seven colors correspond to the seven
tones of the scale and both to the seven
planets. Red vibrates lowest like the
note do violet highest like tea. The
octave of light though incomplete to
human eyes extends infinitely beyond
visible perception merging the unseen
worlds as fine colors and tones.
To the Egyptians, Persians and Calaldans
color was sacred language. Their temples
were painted according to planetary
hues. Red for Mars, blue for Jupiter,
gold for the sun. In India, fountains
poured in seven colors. In Babylon, the
ziggurats rose in seven painted tears.
Plato called color an effluence of form
commensurate with sight, an emanation
between perceiver and perceived. A truth
echoed in the mystery schools which
taught that every light and tone issues
from the one white source dividing into
seven creative streams, the Elohim or
builders of the universe.
In the Pythagorean system, these seven,
three higher and four lower, form the
spiritual and material halves of
creation. Their synthesis is the octave,
the return of multiplicity into unity.
The seven vowels of the Greeks, the
seven heavens of the planets, the seven
rays of the light, and the seven notes
of sound were all expressions of the
same cosmic law. When these harmonized
perfectly, they become the eternal hymn
sung by all creation. The music of the
spheres made manifest in color, tone,
and form. Thus, harmony was not merely
an art, but the secret of existence.
Beauty is harmony perceived by the soul.
Music is harmony heard. Color is harmony
seen. And through them the wise discern
the order of the cosmos and the law by
which the good sustains all things in
perfect proportion.

Ceremonial magic and sorcery.
Ceremonial magic is the science of
invoking and directing unseen forces
through sacred formula, symbols, and
words of power. It's a depths clad in
consecrated robes, wands in hand, sought
to command the elemental and astral
intelligences that dwell behind nature's
veil. Though in itself a neutral art,
its corruption gave rise to the
sorceries of black magic. In ancient
Egypt, where the Atlantean magicians had
settled, the priesthood perverted the
original mysteries, replacing spiritual
enlightenment with tyranny. A scarlet
council of sorcerers ruled both temple
and throne, destroying the true keys of
wisdom and enslaving the people through
superstition.
Rituals once designed to awaken the soul
became tools of bondage. Images that had
served as symbols were worshiped as
gods. Ignorance replaced initiation.
From that corruption arose the priest
craft that still blinds humanity.
In medieval Europe, cobbalists and
magicians believing themselves heirs to
Solomon's power revived ceremonial
magic. Some pure of purpose sought
divine knowledge. Others descended into
demonism, seeking command over spirits
for personal gain. They replaced virtue
with artifice, circles, mirrors, knives,
and charms in pursuit of power over the
elements. Yet, as in all ages, those who
attempted to control spirits without
mastering themselves fell prey to the
forces they summoned. Elementals,
deceitful and imitative, often
masqueraded as the dead. Even Socrates's
Damon, though benign, abandoned him at
death, showing that no external spirit
is a true guide.
The magician's art rests on several
principles. That the visible world has
invisible counterparts, that both good
and evil spirits exist, that man may
contact them through precise ritual, and
that contracts can bind them to service.
The black magician bargains with
infernal powers for temporal gains,
forfeiting his soul in the end. To
prolong life and delay payment, he
resorts to crime, vampirism, and occult
theft of vital force. For this reason,
selfishness, the desire to bend the
divine will to personal ends, is the
essence of black magic, and it persists
today disguised as the cult of power,
manipulation, and will. The astral
light, the universal fluid called the
great magical agent, is the medium of
all operations. Elephice Levi's
Baffomet, the goat of Mendes, symbolizes
this dual power. The balance of light
and shadow, male and female, creation
and destruction. The adept of white
magic governs it through purity. The
black magician is enslaved by its lower
currents. The legends of Foust embody
this danger. Summoning spirits for
knowledge and pleasure, Faustst made a
pact with Asteroth, who sent him
familiar demons swift as thought. Though
they obeyed his commands, he ultimately
perished by their hand. A symbol of man
destroyed by the forces he misused.
Medieval manuals such as the complete
book of magic science describe these
rights. Triple circles drawn with virgin
parchment and silk threads. Crosses of
laurel and palm invocations of Zadiel or
Rabiel in the name of the Trinity. The
magician protected within his circle
prayed for divine aid and commanded the
spirit to appear. Yet each ritual warned
that if faith or purity wavered,
disaster followed. In black magic, the
pact is reversed. The demon dictates,
the magician signs with his own blood,
binding his soul through the occult law
that he who controls the blood controls
the spirit. Terms vary. Years of service
in exchange for offerings or lives. But
the end is always the same. Servitude to
the demon he once ruled. The pentagram,
emblem of man and mastery, is the key
symbol of magic. Upright, it is the star
of light, the human soul rising above
matter. Inverted, it becomes the goat's
head, the fallen star, symbol of
perversion and black art.
All inverted symbols proclaim the same
truth that evil is not an independent
force but the misuse of divine power.
Thus ceremonial magic in its pure form
sought the cooperation of celestial
intelligences for the enlightenment of
man. But corruption by ambition and
fear. It became the dark art of
domination. The pentagram upright
reveals the redeemed soul. Inverted the
soul enslaved by its own desire. The
same symbol marks both ascent and fall.
For man himself is the magician capable

The elements and their inhabitants.
Paracelis the great alchemist and
philosopher taught that the four
elements earth, air, fire, and water
each possess both a visible and
invisible aspect. a gross body and a
subtle essence. From these ethereal
principles arise the spiritual
counterparts of nature inhabited by
intelligent beings called elementals or
nature spirits. These are not imaginary
creations but living entities existing
in the unseen strata of the world. As
animals, plants and men live in physical
matter, so do elementals live in the
spiritual essences of the elements.
Paraselus classified them into four
orders. Gnomes of the earth, undyines of
water, salamanders of fire, and silves
of air. They are half spiritual beings
composed of a finer ether than man. And
though resembling him and form, they are
without the immortal spirit that links
humanity to the divine. When they die,
they dissolve back into their element,
their individuality lost. They live for
centuries, impervious to ordinary decay,
yet lacking the promise of immortality.
Ancient civilizations acknowledge these
beings openly. The Greeks called them
demons and worshiped them as
intermediaries between gods and men.
Socrates's guiding spirit may have been
of this kind, though perhaps it was his
own higher self. Pagan belief people
every aspect of nature. The seas with
