Most of us know what the hero’s&nbsp; journey, or the “monomyth”&nbsp;&nbsp;
is even if you think you don’t. It’s the idea&nbsp; that all the myths in human history can be&nbsp;&nbsp;
simplified down into one common form. The&nbsp; concept was invented and popularized by&nbsp;&nbsp;
writer and professor Joseph Campbell in his&nbsp; book HERO WITH A THOUSAND FACES from 1949.
You’ve probably seen this handy little diagram&nbsp; in an English class or a screenwriting class.&nbsp;&nbsp;
The hero starts at home; crosses the&nbsp; threshold into a larger, strange world;&nbsp;&nbsp;
faces trials, and is transformed; eventually&nbsp; returning home, a changed person. For example,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Star Wars, one of the most&nbsp; influential films of all time,&nbsp;&nbsp;
was literally written by George Lucas&nbsp; with the hero’s journey in mind.

It’s become the gold standard for how to tell&nbsp; the story of a hero. Campbell read thousands of&nbsp;&nbsp;
myths from all over the world, all different&nbsp; time periods, and argued that all of them,&nbsp;&nbsp;
that’s right all of them, can be boiled down into&nbsp; this series of steps. “Well that seems a little&nbsp;&nbsp;
reductive,” you might think. And to that I would&nbsp; say, who are you talking to, me explaining Joseph&nbsp;&nbsp;
Campbell’s theory? Or Joseph Campbell’s theory?&nbsp; Because the answer to your question is: YES.
In his book on the Monomyth,&nbsp; Campbell describes the hero as:&nbsp;&nbsp;
“the man or woman who has been able to battle&nbsp; past his personal and local historical limitations&nbsp;&nbsp;
to the generally valid, normally human forms.&nbsp; [His] visions, ideas, and inspirations come&nbsp;&nbsp;
pristine from the primary springs of human life&nbsp; and thought. [Hence] They are eloquent, not of&nbsp;&nbsp;
the present, disintegrating society and psyche,&nbsp; but of the unquenched source through which society&nbsp;&nbsp;
is reborn.” He is an “eternal man–perfected,&nbsp; unspecific, universal man–he has been reborn.”
Campbell argues that we can take&nbsp; a specific person, whether it’s an&nbsp;&nbsp;
ancient myth or a modern hero, pull them&nbsp; out of their time and place, and voila:&nbsp;&nbsp;
they are all the same. And on the one hand, you&nbsp; could see this as a way that we are all united&nbsp;&nbsp;
in our humanity, that no matter the culture&nbsp; or time there are certain truths we uphold.&nbsp;&nbsp;
BUT the “universal truths” that Campbell suggests,&nbsp; just happen to be dripping in his own biases,&nbsp;&nbsp;
fantasies, upbringing, culture, and the&nbsp; now extremely outdated white hetero male&nbsp;&nbsp;
Christian worldview of someone raised in early&nbsp; 20th century America. In other words, whoof.
In his writing about the history&nbsp; of humanity reciting stories,&nbsp;&nbsp;
Campbell uses words like “normal” and “generally&nbsp; valid” as if those are completely agreed upon&nbsp;&nbsp;
values throughout all of time (they’re not.)&nbsp; He acts as though he is above the influence of&nbsp;&nbsp;
time and place and that his opinions&nbsp; are objectively universal and essential&nbsp;&nbsp;
(they’re not.) He claims that a hero is&nbsp; “perfected, unspecific, universal,” as&nbsp;&nbsp;
if heroes have been made on an assembly line&nbsp; for thousands of years, instead of each saying&nbsp;&nbsp;
something about the time and place they originate&nbsp; from (a third parenthetical). And you know what,&nbsp;&nbsp;
sure, instead of doing the work to understand a&nbsp; different culture’s perspective it WOULD be easier&nbsp;&nbsp;
if they all just happen to fit into the template&nbsp; you made up. So you know what, you got me there.
Campbell often assures us that every myth has&nbsp; one, objective reading and point of view. But&nbsp;&nbsp;
even with a passing knowledge of Western mythology&nbsp; you know that that is ABSOLUTELY not true!!!&nbsp;&nbsp;
Odysseus, one of the most famous mythic heroes,&nbsp; was a clever hero to the Greeks. But to the Romans&nbsp;&nbsp;
he was a deceitful villain, even calling him&nbsp; “cruel Odysseus.” The Greek playwright Sophocles&nbsp;&nbsp;
portrayed Odysseus in completely different ways&nbsp; even within his own plays: a “commonsensical&nbsp;&nbsp;
realist” in Ajax, but a “scheming sophist” in&nbsp; Philoctetes. So. Which one is the objective&nbsp;&nbsp;
truth of the myth of Odysseus? Ironically, one&nbsp; of the actual consistent themes of The Odyssey&nbsp;&nbsp;
is that there IS no objective retelling&nbsp; of events. It is the storyteller who is&nbsp;&nbsp;
motivated to tell the tale in a specific way,&nbsp; for a specific reason, to a specific audience,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with a specific goal in mind. And although Joseph&nbsp; Campbell likes to see himself as the ultimate&nbsp;&nbsp;
objective voice speaking for all of humanity, at&nbsp; the end of the day he too is simply a storyteller,&nbsp;&nbsp;
motivated to tell his tale in a specific way, for&nbsp; a specific reason, with a specific goal in mind,&nbsp;&nbsp;
with all of his biases and motives in tact.&nbsp; The way he strips myths of culture and intent&nbsp;&nbsp;
mirrors the way he wishes to strip himself of&nbsp; culture and intent. A perfect man, unbeholden to&nbsp;&nbsp;
the timeline of history. A hero, he might say,&nbsp; about himself. But here’s the objective truth:&nbsp;&nbsp;
Campbell is very much beholden to his time and&nbsp; place, as are the stories we tell. And the journey&nbsp;&nbsp;
of understanding someone else’s perspective&nbsp; is much more worthwhile, one that has the real&nbsp;&nbsp;
danger of challenging our own point of view and&nbsp; assumptions about the world, but has the potential&nbsp;&nbsp;
to shape us into a better person than who we&nbsp; were before we stepped outside our front door.

To know the man’s work, we should first take a&nbsp; look at the man, Joseph Campbell himself. What&nbsp;&nbsp;
did he believe? Why did he believe it? This&nbsp; is an easier question to ask than to answer,&nbsp;&nbsp;

Campbell grew up idolizing Douglas Fairbanks,&nbsp; the silent movie star from films like Robin&nbsp;&nbsp;
Hood and Zorro. This is probably why Campbell&nbsp; considers himself a “maverick.” “I see myself&nbsp;&nbsp;
as a maverick.” If there was a machine? Campbell&nbsp; would totally rage against it. He’s against rigid&nbsp;&nbsp;
structural systems, just like the hero outsiders&nbsp; of his childhood soaps. “How in heaven’s name are&nbsp;&nbsp;
you going to find your own track if you are always&nbsp; doing what society tells you to do? I [also]&nbsp;&nbsp;
spent a year teaching in a boy’s prep school…&nbsp; those who followed their zeal, their bliss,&nbsp;&nbsp;
they have led decent, wonderful lives; those who&nbsp; did what Dad said they should do because it’s safe&nbsp;&nbsp;
found out it’s not safe. It’s disaster.” But this&nbsp; quote is in the middle of a 300 page book about&nbsp;&nbsp;
his extremely rigid Hero’s Cycle structure, one so&nbsp; innate to human nature that supposedly no one has&nbsp;&nbsp;
ever crafted tales that deviate from it (we have.)&nbsp; He often repeats societal norms from his time as&nbsp;&nbsp;
immutable truths, things that are now completely&nbsp; outdated: everything from gender roles, to gender&nbsp;&nbsp;
expression, to compulsatory heterosexuality,&nbsp; even Protestant work ethic are all set in stone.&nbsp;&nbsp;
He insists that he and the heroes he&nbsp; recognizes should rebel against these systems,&nbsp;&nbsp;
while also reassuring himself and the heroes that&nbsp; he recognizes, that the world is actually perfect&nbsp;&nbsp;
and doesn’t need to change. Here’s Campbell saying&nbsp; as much from The Power of Myth, the PBS series&nbsp;&nbsp;
that did a lot to secure his place in pop culture&nbsp; infamy: “When people say to me you know ‘Do you&nbsp;&nbsp;

the way it is!” That people like him should work&nbsp; within the systems: “I don’t think it would help&nbsp;&nbsp;
you to change the system, but it would help you&nbsp; to live in the system as a human being.” This is&nbsp;&nbsp;

His mantra, which you’ve probably heard not&nbsp; knowing it came from him, I certainly didn’t,&nbsp;&nbsp;

“Follow your bliss, I mean find where it is, and&nbsp; don’t be afraid to follow it.” “If the work that&nbsp;&nbsp;

Looking around at the “current state of things,”&nbsp;&nbsp;
