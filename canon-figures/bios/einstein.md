## 1. Life

### Ulm and Munich (1879–1894)

Albert Einstein was born on 14 March 1879 at Bahnhofstrasse 20 in Ulm, in the Kingdom of
Württemberg in the recently unified German Empire, to Hermann Einstein, a featherbed
merchant turning toward electrical engineering, and Pauline Einstein (née Koch), the
daughter of a prosperous grain dealer from Cannstatt. The family was secular Ashkenazi
Jewish, observant in only the loosest sense. A year after Albert's birth the family
moved to Munich, where Hermann and his younger brother Jakob established a small firm —
Elektrotechnische Fabrik J. Einstein & Cie. — manufacturing dynamos, arc lamps and
electrical measuring instruments. The firm built the lighting installation for the
Munich Oktoberfest of 1885 and supplied parts of the first electrical lighting for the
Munich suburb of Schwabing. Albert grew up in the rooms above the workshop. His sister
Maria — known throughout the family as Maja — was born in November 1881; the two would
remain close until her death in 1951.

Albert was a slow speaker as a child. The household servants nicknamed him *der
Depperte*, "the dopey one." He later said himself that the speech delay made him think
harder about the meanings of ordinary words than other children did, because he had to
assemble each sentence consciously before saying it. He attended a Catholic primary
school (the Petersschule) from age six, then the Luitpold-Gymnasium in Munich from age
nine. The Luitpold was a typical Wilhelmine-era classical gymnasium with rote drilling,
military deportment and a faculty culture that Einstein found suffocating. He hated it.
The decisive intellectual encounters of his Munich childhood happened outside school: a
pocket compass shown to him by his father at age four or five (which he later named as
the first mystery he found genuinely strange — that the needle pointed without anything
visibly pushing it); the *Heilige Geometrie-Büchlein*, a small Euclidean geometry
textbook he received around age twelve and worked through cover to cover in a summer;
the lectures of Max Talmud (later Max Talmey), an impoverished Polish medical student
who came to dinner once a week as part of the family's traditional charity to a poor
scholar and brought along the *Naturwissenschaftliche Volksbücher* of Aaron Bernstein,
the writings of Ludwig Büchner, and eventually Kant's *Kritik der reinen Vernunft*.
Talmud later wrote that by age thirteen Einstein had outgrown what Talmud could teach
him in mathematics.

### Pavia and Aarau (1894–1896)

Einstein's father's electrical business failed in Munich in 1894. The family relocated
to Pavia in northern Italy, where Hermann's brother Jakob hoped to find better
prospects. Albert was left behind in a Munich boarding house to finish his gymnasium
term. He was miserable. By the spring of 1895, on his own initiative, he obtained a
doctor's note attesting to nervous exhaustion, presented it to the Luitpold, was
effectively expelled (or chose to leave under pressure — accounts differ on the exact
mechanism), and rejoined his family in Pavia. He spent most of 1895 wandering the
Italian countryside, helping in his uncle's workshop, reading and writing his first
surviving scientific essay, "Über die Untersuchung des Ätherzustandes im magnetischen
Felde" ("On the Investigation of the State of the Ether in a Magnetic Field"), which he
sent to his uncle Caesar Koch. The essay shows a sixteen-year-old already trying to
think his way past Maxwell's ether on physical-intuition grounds.

In October 1895 he sat the entrance examination for the Eidgenössische Polytechnische
Schule (the Polytechnic) in Zürich at age sixteen, two years younger than the standard
entrants, and failed the general portion (literature, French, zoology, botany) while
excelling in mathematics and physics. The director Albin Herzog recommended that he
spend a year at the Aargauische Kantonsschule in Aarau preparing for the Matura.
Einstein lodged with the family of one of the Kantonsschule teachers, Jost Winteler, an
arrangement that turned out to be the most settled and happiest year of his adolescence.
He fell briefly in love with one of Winteler's daughters, Marie; his sister Maja later
married Winteler's son Paul. The Aarau curriculum used the inquiry-and-discussion
methods of Pestalozzi rather than the Prussian drill of the Luitpold. He passed the
Matura in October 1896 and matriculated at the Polytechnic the same month. Earlier that
year he had renounced his Württemberg citizenship — the formal release was granted in
January 1896 — to avoid the German military service that would have begun at age
seventeen. He remained stateless from 1896 until 1901, when he obtained Swiss
citizenship.

### ETH Zürich (1896–1900)

The Polytechnic — renamed the Eidgenössische Technische Hochschule (ETH) only in 1911,
but conventionally back-named — admitted Einstein into its mathematics and physics
teaching diploma section. Among his classmates were Marcel Grossmann (the mathematician
who would later supply Einstein with the tensor calculus needed for general relativity),
Friedrich Adler (who later assassinated the Austrian Minister-President Karl von Stürgkh
in 1916 and survived to become a leading Austrian Social Democrat), and Mileva Marić
(the only woman in the section, a Serbian from Vojvodina, who would become Einstein's
first wife). His instructors included Hermann Minkowski, who taught him geometry and
analysis, and Heinrich Friedrich Weber, who taught experimental physics. Einstein's
relationship with Weber soured quickly: Einstein cut Weber's lectures to read Maxwell,
Hertz, Helmholtz and Boltzmann on his own; Weber retaliated by withholding the
laboratory recommendation that would have led to a teaching assistantship after
graduation. The line attributed to Weber — "You are a clever boy, Einstein, a very
clever boy. But you have one great fault: you'll never let yourself be told anything" —
is well attested by Einstein's later correspondence. Minkowski's reaction was cooler at
the time but would prove more important: in 1908 Minkowski geometrized the 1905
special-relativity paper into the four-dimensional spacetime formulation that opened the
door to general relativity.

Einstein graduated in July 1900 with a passable diploma — a 4.91 out of 6 — but was the
only one of the four physics-track diploma candidates not offered an assistantship. His
relationship with Mileva Marić had become romantic by 1899. She failed her first attempt
at the diploma exam the same summer that he passed.

### Stateless interlude and the patent office (1900–1902)

Einstein spent 1900 and 1901 looking for academic work, sending letters of inquiry to
physicists across Europe and being turned down or ignored. He published his first paper
— "Folgerungen aus den Capillaritätserscheinungen", *Annalen der Physik* (4) 4, 513–523
(1901), on capillarity and intermolecular forces — and acquired Swiss citizenship in
February 1901. In late 1901 his friend Marcel Grossmann's father intervened with
Friedrich Haller, the director of the Swiss Federal Patent Office in Bern, on Einstein's
behalf. Einstein took the entrance examination in May 1902 and was hired in June as a
Technical Expert Third Class at an annual salary of 3,500 Swiss francs. He started work
on 23 June 1902.

The patent office is now the most famous institutional employer in the history of
physics. Einstein worked there for seven years, eight hours a day, six days a week. The
work — assessing applications for electromechanical inventions, many of them devices for
synchronizing clocks across distances using electrical signals — has often been
speculated to have provided some of the conceptual material for the operational
definition of simultaneity in the 1905 special-relativity paper. The biographical
historian Peter Galison has argued the case in detail in *Einstein's Clocks, Poincaré's
Maps* (Norton 2003). The case is suggestive but not conclusive; the conceptual structure
of special relativity does not strictly require Bern's clock-synchronization patents,
and Einstein had been thinking about the constancy of the speed of light since at least
the Aarau Gedankenexperiment of imagining riding alongside a light wave.

### Family — first marriage, Lieserl, sons (1902–1914)

In January 1902 Mileva Marić, then living with her parents in Novi Sad, gave birth to a
daughter named Lieserl. Einstein never met her. The existence of Lieserl was unknown to
scholars until 1986, when fifty-four letters between Einstein and Marić from the years
1897–1903 were auctioned by Hans Albert Einstein's children and acquired largely by the
Hebrew University of Jerusalem; the correspondence was published as Volume 1 of the
*Collected Papers*. In a letter of September 1903 Einstein refers to scarlet fever
afflicting Lieserl. After that letter she vanishes from the historical record. The most
plausible reconstructions — Michele Zackheim's *Einstein's Daughter* (Riverhead 1999),
Robert Schulmann's analyses for the *Collected Papers* — converge on either an early
death from scarlet fever or, less probably, surrender for adoption to a Serbian family
near the Marić home in Kać. The matter remains genuinely unresolved.

Einstein and Marić married on 6 January 1903 in a Bern civil ceremony with two friends
as witnesses; neither family attended. Their son Hans Albert was born on 14 May 1904 in
Bern. Their second son Eduard — called Tete in the family — was born on 28 July 1910 in
Zürich. Hans Albert grew up to be a hydraulic engineer and emigrated to the United
States in 1938, eventually becoming professor of hydraulic engineering at the University
of California at Berkeley; he died in 1973. Eduard was a brilliant student, began
studying medicine at the University of Zürich with the intention of becoming a
psychiatrist, and developed schizophrenia in his early twenties. He was
institutionalized in 1932 at the Burghölzli psychiatric clinic in Zürich and lived there
for most of the rest of his life, dying in 1965. Einstein, by then in Princeton, never
saw Eduard again after 1933.

The marriage to Marić deteriorated through the years in Prague (1911–1912) and Zürich
(1912–1914) and effectively ended when Einstein moved to Berlin in April 1914. Marić and
the boys returned to Zürich within months. In February 1916 Einstein presented Marić
with a written list of conditions for any continued cohabitation that even at the time
read as ungenerous. She declined; they separated formally and divorced on 14 February
1919. Part of the divorce settlement was that any future Nobel Prize money would go to
Marić and the boys, a settlement Einstein honored when the 1921 prize was awarded in
1922.

### Annus mirabilis 1905

Einstein was twenty-six and a Technical Expert Second Class at the patent office (he had
been promoted from Third Class in April 1906; the annus mirabilis itself preceded the
promotion). He had no academic affiliation, no laboratory, no graduate students. He had
a circle of friends who met in the evenings to read philosophy and physics — Maurice
Solovine, Conrad Habicht, occasionally Michele Besso — and called themselves the
*Akademie Olympia*. Across 1905 he submitted four papers and a doctoral dissertation
that together restructured physics.

The four papers, in order of submission:

- "Über einen die Erzeugung und Verwandlung des Lichtes betreffenden heuristischen
  Gesichtspunkt" — *Annalen der Physik* (4) 17, 132–148, received 18 March 1905. The
  light-quantum paper. Einstein proposed that the energy of light is distributed not
  continuously, as Maxwell's wave theory required, but in discrete packets of energy
  *hν*. The paper used this hypothesis to derive the photoelectric equation. Einstein
  himself called it (in a letter to Habicht) "very revolutionary." The 1921 Nobel Prize
  in Physics was awarded for this paper, not for relativity.
- "Eine neue Bestimmung der Moleküldimensionen" — submitted as a doctoral dissertation
  to the University of Zürich on 30 April 1905, accepted 19 July 1905, printed by
  Buchdruckerei K. J. Wyss in Bern in 1906, and published in revised form as *Annalen
  der Physik* (4) 19, 289–306 (1906). It used the viscosity of dilute sugar solutions to
  derive a value for Avogadro's number. The methodology is the basis of what is now
  called the Stokes–Einstein relation for diffusion.
- "Über die von der molekularkinetischen Theorie der Wärme geforderte Bewegung von in
  ruhenden Flüssigkeiten suspendierten Teilchen" — *Annalen der Physik* (4) 17, 549–560,
  received 11 May 1905. The Brownian motion paper. Einstein derived the mean-square
  displacement of a suspended particle as a function of time, temperature and viscosity,
  providing for the first time a quantitative empirical handle on the molecular
  hypothesis. Jean Perrin's experimental confirmation in 1908 settled the long-running
  atomism debate.
- "Zur Elektrodynamik bewegter Körper" — *Annalen der Physik* (4) 17, 891–921, received
  30 June 1905, published 26 September 1905. The special-relativity paper. From two
  postulates — the principle of relativity for all physical laws including
  electrodynamics, and the constancy of the speed of light in vacuum independent of the
  motion of the source — Einstein derived the Lorentz transformations, the relativity of
  simultaneity, length contraction, time dilation and a new composition law for
  velocities. The paper does not cite a single previous source by name; the only
  acknowledgement is a closing thanks to "my friend and colleague M. Besso."
- "Ist die Trägheit eines Körpers von seinem Energieinhalt abhängig?" — *Annalen der
  Physik* (4) 18, 639–641, received 27 September 1905. The mass–energy equivalence
  corollary. The famous form *E = mc²* does not appear in the paper in those letters;
  what Einstein wrote was that if a body emits energy *L* in the form of radiation, its
  mass diminishes by *L*/*V*² where *V* is the speed of light. The modern symbolic form
  solidified later.

The five 1905 documents were all written while he was working a six-day week reading
patent applications.

### Bern, Zürich, Prague, back to Zürich (1908–1914)

Recognition came in stages. Einstein was made a *Privatdozent* (an unpaid lecturer) at
the University of Bern in February 1908, on the strength of a *Habilitationsschrift* he
had submitted the previous June; his first lecture was attended by three people, all
friends. In May 1909 he was appointed *ausserordentlicher Professor* of theoretical
physics at the University of Zürich. He left the patent office on 15 October 1909. In
April 1911 he moved to the German University in Prague as full professor; in October
1912 he returned to Zürich as professor at the ETH (now under that name). By the spring
of 1914, on the joint invitation of Max Planck and Walther Nernst, he had accepted a
research chair at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Physics in Berlin, election to the
Prussian Academy of Sciences, and a professorship at the University of Berlin without
teaching obligations. He moved to Berlin on 6 April 1914.

Across these years two physical lines of work matured. The first was the equivalence
principle, first stated in print in the 1907 review article "Über das
Relativitätsprinzip und die aus demselben gezogenen Folgerungen", *Jahrbuch der
Radioaktivität und Elektronik* 4, 411–462 (1907) — submitted 4 December 1907, with
corrections in volume 5 in 1908. The fundamental claim is that there is no local
experiment that distinguishes a uniformly accelerated frame from a frame at rest in a
uniform gravitational field. From this he extracted, in the same review, the prediction
that clocks run slower in a stronger gravitational potential and the prediction that
light bends in a gravitational field. The 1911 paper "Über den Einfluß der Schwerkraft
auf die Ausbreitung des Lichtes", *Annalen der Physik* (4) 35, 898–908 (1911), gave a
quantitative prediction of the bending of starlight grazing the Sun: 0.83 seconds of
arc, exactly half the value the full general theory would later give. The factor-of-two
error was not corrected until late 1915.

The second line was the search for a tensorial field theory of gravity. From 1912, with
Marcel Grossmann's mathematical collaboration in the *Entwurf* paper of 1913 — Einstein
and Grossmann, "Entwurf einer verallgemeinerten Relativitätstheorie und einer Theorie
der Gravitation", *Zeitschrift für Mathematik und Physik* 62, 225–261 (1914), originally
separately printed in 1913 — Einstein worked toward equations relating the metric tensor
of spacetime to the stress-energy tensor of matter. The *Entwurf* equations were not
generally covariant; Einstein convinced himself for two years that general covariance
was incompatible with conservation laws (the so-called *Lochbetrachtung* or "hole
argument"). He realized the error in October 1915 and over the next four weeks wrote the
four November 1915 papers in the *Sitzungsberichte* of the Prussian Academy that
culminated in the Einstein field equations.

### General relativity, Berlin and the war (1914–1918)

Einstein arrived in Berlin in April 1914. By August Europe was at war. He was one of the
very few German physicists who refused to sign the "Manifesto of the Ninety-Three" in
October 1914 in defense of German war aims, and instead signed the "Manifesto to the
Europeans" drafted by Georg Friedrich Nicolai, which had only four signatories including
Einstein. Through 1915 he worked on general relativity in Berlin. The four November 1915
communications to the Prussian Academy were:

- "Zur allgemeinen Relativitätstheorie", *Sitz. Preuss. Akad. Wiss.* 1915, 778–786
  (presented 4 November 1915). First reintroduction of general covariance.
- "Zur allgemeinen Relativitätstheorie (Nachtrag)", *Sitz. Preuss. Akad. Wiss.* 1915,
  799–801 (presented 11 November 1915). Correction.
- "Erklärung der Perihelbewegung des Merkur aus der allgemeinen Relativitätstheorie",
  *Sitz. Preuss. Akad. Wiss.* 1915, 831–839 (presented 18 November 1915). The derivation
  of the anomalous 43"-per-century perihelion advance of Mercury. Einstein wrote later
  that the moment he saw his calculation match Le Verrier's observed value he had
  palpitations.
- "Die Feldgleichungen der Gravitation", *Sitz. Preuss. Akad. Wiss.* 1915, 844–847
  (presented 25 November 1915). The field equations in their final form, with the trace
  term.

The full review came as "Die Grundlage der allgemeinen Relativitätstheorie", *Annalen
der Physik* (4) 49, 769–822 (1916), submitted in March 1916 and reprinted as a
stand-alone book by J. A. Barth in Leipzig the same year. This is the canonical primary
statement of general relativity.

David Hilbert in Göttingen had been working on a variational derivation of equations of
physics for several months in parallel; he submitted "Die Grundlagen der Physik. (Erste
Mitteilung)" to the Göttingen Society of Sciences on 20 November 1915, five days before
Einstein's submission of "Die Feldgleichungen der Gravitation". For decades the priority
question generated heat. The decisive analysis came with Leo Corry, Jürgen Renn and John
Stachel, "Belated Decision in the Hilbert-Einstein Priority Dispute", *Science* 278,
1270–1273 (14 November 1997), which examined the proof sheets of Hilbert's paper and
found that the proof version submitted on 20 November did not contain the field
equations in explicit form; the explicit equations appear in the printed version after
revisions made in December 1915, after Einstein's 25 November submission. The modern
consensus, also defended at length in Renn (ed.), *The Genesis of General Relativity*
(Springer 2007, 4 vols.), is that Einstein and Hilbert arrived independently at closely
related results, but Einstein has priority on the field equations themselves and full
priority on the physical theory, while Hilbert has priority on the variational
derivation from a Lagrangian (the Einstein–Hilbert action).

The first major external prediction of general relativity — the bending of starlight
grazing the Sun, predicted at 1.75 seconds of arc — was confirmed by the British
solar-eclipse expeditions of 29 May 1919, led by Arthur Stanley Eddington at the island
of Príncipe and by Andrew Crommelin and Charles Davidson at Sobral in Brazil. The
results were announced at the joint meeting of the Royal Society and the Royal
Astronomical Society in Burlington House on 6 November 1919, and made Einstein, almost
overnight, the most famous scientist alive.

### Berlin 1917–1933: cosmology, quantum theory, fame, divorce, remarriage

In 1917 Einstein published "Kosmologische Betrachtungen zur allgemeinen
Relativitätstheorie", *Sitz. Preuss. Akad. Wiss.* 1917, 142–152, the founding paper of
relativistic cosmology. Working on the assumption — universal at the time — that the
universe is static at the largest scales, he found that his original field equations
admitted no static cosmological solution. He introduced an additional term proportional
to the metric, with coefficient Λ, the *cosmological constant*, chosen to balance
gravitational attraction at the largest scales. Five years later Aleksandr Friedmann
("Über die Krümmung des Raumes", *Z. Phys.* 10, 377–386, 1922) showed that the original
equations did admit non-static solutions — expanding or contracting universes.
Einstein's first response was a published note rejecting Friedmann's analysis on a
calculational ground; once Friedmann pointed out the error, Einstein retracted the
rejection but continued to dislike the dynamical solutions personally. After Hubble's
1929 confirmation that distant galaxies recede at speeds proportional to their distance,
Einstein dropped the cosmological constant. Gamow, in his autobiography *My World Line*
(Viking 1970), reports Einstein calling Λ his "greatest blunder"; the second-hand
attribution has been challenged but is plausible. The historical irony is well known:
the 1998 supernova observations by the Perlmutter and Schmidt–Riess teams (Nobel Prize
2011) revived a positive cosmological constant — now read as dark energy — into the
standard cosmological model.

In 1916 and 1917 Einstein also published a pair of papers — "Zur Quantentheorie der
Strahlung", *Mitteilungen der Physikalischen Gesellschaft Zürich* 16, 47–62 (1916), and
the longer "Strahlungs-Emission und -Absorption nach der Quantentheorie", *Verh. Dtsch.
Phys. Ges.* 18, 318–323 (1916), with the canonical statement in *Phys. Z.* 18, 121–128
(1917) — introducing the *A* and *B* coefficients for spontaneous and stimulated
emission and absorption. The treatment is the basis of the entire later theory of laser
action. The third process — stimulated emission — is Einstein's invention from this
paper.

Einstein's 1924 collaboration with the young Indian physicist Satyendra Nath Bose
followed Bose's June 1924 letter to Einstein with the manuscript of a derivation of
Planck's radiation law from a new statistical counting procedure. Einstein translated
Bose's paper into German, secured its publication in *Zeitschrift für Physik* 26,
178–181 (1924), and across three papers in the *Sitzungsberichte der Preussischen
Akademie der Wissenschaften* (1924, 261–267; 1925, 3–14; 1925, 18–25) extended the
procedure to a quantum gas of massive particles, predicting what is now called
Bose–Einstein condensation — the macroscopic occupation of a single quantum state at low
temperature. The phenomenon was observed only in 1995, in a dilute gas of rubidium atoms
by Eric Cornell and Carl Wieman at JILA in Boulder, and independently in sodium by
Wolfgang Ketterle at MIT. The 2001 Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for the
experimental confirmation.

Einstein had married his cousin Elsa Löwenthal (born Einstein) on 2 June 1919, four
months after his divorce from Marić. Elsa was the daughter of his mother Pauline's
sister and the daughter of his father Hermann's brother — a double first cousin. She had
two daughters from a previous marriage, Ilse and Margot, who lived with the household.
The Berlin household at Haberlandstrasse 5 was in effect Elsa's domain; Einstein
occupied an attic study and was protected from social demands by her management.

He travelled extensively through the 1920s — to the United States with Chaim Weizmann in
1921 to raise funds for the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, to Japan in late 1922
(during which trip he was informed by telegram of the 1921 Nobel Prize, deferred a year
and finally awarded in November 1922), to Palestine in 1923, to South America in 1925,
and to Caltech and the United States again in 1930–1931 and 1931–1932. The Nobel
citation — "for his services to Theoretical Physics, and especially for his discovery of
the law of the photoelectric effect" — explicitly excluded relativity, on the
recommendation of the experimentalist Allvar Gullstrand who held that relativity was not
yet experimentally established to the satisfaction of the committee.

### The Solvay debates with Bohr (1927–1930)

The 1927 Solvay Conference in Brussels, on "Electrons and Photons", was the first major
confrontation between the new quantum mechanics — Heisenberg's matrix mechanics,
Schrödinger's wave mechanics, Born's probability interpretation, the Copenhagen
interpretation under Bohr — and Einstein's instinct that the theory had to be
incomplete. Einstein's mode of argument was the *Gedankenexperiment*: he would propose a
thought experiment apparently violating an uncertainty relation, Bohr would spend the
night working out the response, and the next morning Bohr would show that the thought
experiment in fact obeyed the uncertainty relation when all interactions were properly
counted. The most famous exchange was the photon-box thought experiment of the 1930
Solvay conference, which Bohr defeated by pointing out that Einstein's own
general-relativistic gravitational time dilation — operating on the spring-balance the
box hung from — restored the uncertainty bound.

Einstein left Solvay 1930 unconvinced. The disagreement was philosophical rather than
calculational: Einstein did not dispute the predictions of quantum mechanics; he
disputed that they constituted a complete description of physical reality. The "God does
not play dice" formulation appears in a letter to Max Born of 4 December 1926: "Die
Theorie liefert viel, aber dem Geheimnis des Alten bringt sie uns kaum näher. Jedenfalls
bin ich überzeugt, daß *der* nicht würfelt." — "Quantum mechanics is certainly imposing.
But an inner voice tells me that it is not yet the real thing. The theory says a lot,
but does not really bring us any closer to the secret of the Old One. I, at any rate, am
convinced that *He* does not play dice." Bohr's own reply, often quoted as "Einstein,
stop telling God what to do", appears not to have been said in those words but is
substantively faithful to his position.

### Princeton 1933–1955

Hitler was appointed Chancellor of Germany on 30 January 1933. Einstein was on a
visiting appointment at Caltech at the time. He never returned to Germany. He resigned
from the Prussian Academy in March 1933 (which had begun denunciations of him),
surrendered his German citizenship, and accepted an appointment at the new Institute for
Advanced Study in Princeton, New Jersey, joining the Institute on 17 October 1933. He
took American citizenship on 1 October 1940 while retaining Swiss citizenship.

His Princeton work focused on the search for a unified field theory: a single geometric
structure that would yield both electromagnetism and general relativity, and ideally
also account for the existence of elementary particles as solutions of the field
equations. The search occupied the last thirty years of his life. None of the papers in
this line — extensions of general relativity using affine connections, distant
parallelism, asymmetric metric tensors — survived as canon. The mainstream of physics
moved past him into quantum field theory; Einstein remained skeptical that the program
of reducing fundamental physics to quantum probability amplitudes was the right one.

In 1935, with Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen, he published "Can Quantum-Mechanical
Description of Physical Reality Be Considered Complete?", *Physical Review* 47, 777–780
(1935). The paper proposed a thought experiment — now called the EPR paradox — in which
quantum mechanics predicted that measurements on one particle would instantly determine
the state of a distant entangled partner, and argued that since this would entail
"spooky action at a distance" (the phrase from a 1947 letter to Born), the theory must
be incomplete and there must exist hidden variables that fix the values of physical
quantities prior to measurement. John Bell's 1964 inequality and the experiments of
Stuart Freedman and John Clauser (1972), Alain Aspect, Jean Dalibard and Gérard Roger
(1982), Anton Zeilinger's group through the 1990s and 2000s, and the loophole-free tests
of Hensen et al., Giustina et al. and Shalm et al. (all 2015) showed that no local
hidden-variable theory can reproduce the predictions of quantum mechanics. Einstein's
expectation was wrong; the EPR paper itself, however, founded the modern study of
entanglement and is the most-cited foundation of contemporary quantum information
science. The 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics, to Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger, was awarded
for experimental work descending directly from EPR.

In August 1939, the Hungarian-born physicist Leo Szilárd, working with Eugene Wigner and
Edward Teller, drafted a letter to President Franklin D. Roosevelt warning of the
possibility that Nazi Germany might be working on an atomic bomb. Szilárd asked
Einstein, the most famous scientist in the world, to sign. Einstein signed the letter on
2 August 1939; it was delivered to Roosevelt by Alexander Sachs in October. The letter
is generally credited with prompting the cascade of decisions that led, eventually, to
the Manhattan Project. Einstein himself was excluded from the Manhattan Project on
security grounds — his FBI file, declassified and now 1,427 pages, considered him a
security risk on account of his pacifist and socialist sympathies. He later said, in a
1947 interview with Newsweek and again in 1954 to Linus Pauling: "Had I known that the
Germans would not succeed in producing an atomic bomb, I never would have lifted a
finger."

Einstein refused the offered presidency of Israel on 17 November 1952, after the death
of Chaim Weizmann. The offer was conveyed by Abba Eban; Einstein replied that he was
"deeply moved by the offer from our State of Israel, and at once saddened and ashamed
that I cannot accept it. All my life I have dealt with objective matters, hence I lack
both the natural aptitude and the experience to deal properly with people and to
exercise official functions."

He died on 18 April 1955 at Princeton Hospital, of internal bleeding from the rupture of
an abdominal aortic aneurysm that had been diagnosed and clipped seven years earlier. He
had refused surgery, saying, "I want to go when I want. It is tasteless to prolong life
artificially. I have done my share, it is time to go. I will do it elegantly." His body
was cremated the same afternoon at the Ewing Cemetery in Trenton; the ashes were
scattered at an undisclosed location, in accordance with his wishes. His brain, however,
was removed during the autopsy, without family permission, by the pathologist Thomas
Stoltz Harvey. Harvey kept the brain in a glass jar (in formalin and later in celloidin
sections) for forty-three years, sectioning portions and sending tissue to researchers
around the world. The principal published study, by Marian Diamond and colleagues — "On
the Brain of a Scientist: Albert Einstein", *Experimental Neurology* 88(1), 198–204
(1985) — found anomalously high glia-to-neuron ratios in samples from Brodmann area 39
of the left inferior parietal lobule. The interpretation has been criticized for the
absence of an adequate control group and for the small sample of tissue examined. Harvey
eventually returned the remaining brain tissue to Princeton Hospital in 1998.
Photographs taken at the autopsy were rediscovered in 2010 and published by Dean Falk
and colleagues in *Brain* 136 (2013), 1304–1327.

## 2. Foundation contributions, branch by branch

### 02-physics / relativity / special

The foundation: spacetime as a single geometric arena in which inertial frames are
related by Lorentz rather than Galilean transformations, with the speed of light an
invariant. The 1905 paper *Zur Elektrodynamik bewegter Körper* derives the result from
two postulates and an operational analysis of simultaneity. The conceptual move that
distinguishes Einstein's treatment from Lorentz's and Poincaré's is the operational
reinterpretation of *t*: time is what synchronized clocks measure, simultaneity is a
convention that depends on the inertial frame, and the Lorentz transformations follow
without recourse to a preferred ether frame. Minkowski's 1908 Cologne lecture "Raum und
Zeit" recast the result in four-dimensional geometric form, supplying the substrate for
general relativity. The mass–energy equivalence corollary follows in the September 1905
paper as a direct consequence of the relativistic transformation of energy.

### 02-physics / relativity / general

The foundation: gravity is not a force; it is the geometry of spacetime, locally
Minkowskian and globally curved by the stress-energy of matter, with dynamics governed
by the Einstein field equations *G*<sub>μν</sub> + Λ*g*<sub>μν</sub> =
(8π*G*/*c*⁴)*T*<sub>μν</sub>. The route from the 1907 equivalence principle to the 1915
field equations spans eight years; the mathematical machinery — Riemannian geometry, the
Ricci tensor, the Bianchi identities — was supplied by Marcel Grossmann (1913 *Entwurf*)
and refined by Levi-Civita's 1917 work on parallel transport. Predictions: perihelion
advance of Mercury (matched 1915), gravitational deflection of starlight (1.75″,
confirmed Eddington 1919), gravitational redshift (Pound and Rebka, *Phys. Rev. Lett.*
4, 337, 1960), gravitational time dilation (Hafele–Keating, *Science* 177, 166, 1972),
the Shapiro time delay (Shapiro 1964; Cassini 2003), gravitational lensing as a survey
tool (now routine), the 1974 Hulse–Taylor binary pulsar's orbital decay matching the
predicted gravitational-wave luminosity (Nobel 1993), the direct detection of
gravitational waves by LIGO on 14 September 2015 (Abbott et al., *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 116,
061102, 2016; Nobel 2017), and the Event Horizon Telescope images of M87* (2019) and Sgr
A* (2022) showing the predicted shadow of a Kerr-metric horizon.

### 02-physics / quantum-mechanics

Three foundations: (a) the light quantum of 1905, the *first* quantization in the modern
sense, anticipating Bohr's 1913 atom and the Compton 1923 confirmation that photons
carry momentum *h*/*λ*; (b) the *A* and *B* coefficients of 1916–1917, which derive
Planck's radiation law from a kinetic balance between absorption, spontaneous emission
and *stimulated* emission, the third being Einstein's introduction and the principle
behind every laser; (c) the 1935 EPR paper, which formalized the question of whether
quantum mechanics is complete and gave the field its central thought experiment. EPR is
downstream-foundational in a way the other two are not: it is the seed of the entire
field of quantum information.

### 02-physics / statistical-mechanics

Two foundations: the 1905 Brownian-motion paper, which made the molecular hypothesis
empirically testable through the diffusion coefficient *D* = *kT*/(6π*ηa*) and which
Perrin's 1908 measurements of pollen-grain trajectories used to extract Avogadro's
number — closing the nineteenth-century debate over whether atoms physically exist; and
the 1924–1925 Bose–Einstein papers, which extended Bose's photon counting to a gas of
indistinguishable massive bosons and predicted condensation at sufficiently low
temperature, the first prediction in physics of a genuinely macroscopic quantum state.

### 06-cosmology

The 1917 paper "Kosmologische Betrachtungen zur allgemeinen Relativitätstheorie" is the
founding paper of relativistic cosmology. Even though Einstein's specific static model
with finite spherical spatial geometry did not survive Hubble 1929, the paper introduced
(i) the cosmological-principle-style assumption of large-scale homogeneity and isotropy,
(ii) the cosmological constant Λ as a permissible additional term in the field
equations, and (iii) the program of treating the universe as a whole as a solution of
the field equations. Friedmann (1922) and Lemaître (1927, 1931) built directly on this
scaffold; the modern ΛCDM model has Λ as one of its six parameters.

### 01-mathematics (cross)

The use of the absolute differential calculus of Ricci-Curbastro and Levi-Civita in
Einstein–Grossmann 1913 and the *Grundlage* of 1916 is the moment tensor calculus moved
from a specialist branch of differential geometry into a working language of physics.
Einstein is not the originator of the mathematics — Riemann, Christoffel,
Ricci-Curbastro and Levi-Civita are — but the popularization through general relativity
is what made the field a standard part of every physics graduate curriculum.
Cross-branch credit is for adoption and propagation, not invention.

## 4. What Einstein got wrong

Honest catalog. Hagiography is a defect.

**The 1911 light-bending value.** In "Über den Einfluß der Schwerkraft auf die
Ausbreitung des Lichtes" (*Ann. Phys.* (4) 35, 898–908, 1911) Einstein predicted a
deflection of 0.83″ for starlight grazing the Sun, half the value the full general
theory of November 1915 predicts (1.75″). The calculation used only the time dilation in
a gravitational potential; the spatial-curvature contribution, which doubles the effect,
requires the full field equations. Erwin Finlay-Freundlich's plans for a 1914 Russian
eclipse expedition based on the 1911 prediction were thwarted when the expedition was
interned at the outbreak of the war — historical luck, since the prediction would have
been falsified.

**The cosmological constant.** Introduced in 1917 to enforce a static cosmological
solution, abandoned after Hubble 1929, called by Gamow's report Einstein's "greatest
blunder". The phrase is second-hand and possibly rhetorical embroidery. The substantive
misjudgment was in privileging a static universe; the term Λ itself is not wrong (it is
now the basis of the standard cosmological model) but Einstein's reason for including it
was.

**The hole argument and the rejection of general covariance (1913–1915).** For two years
between the *Entwurf* and the November 1915 papers Einstein convinced himself that
generally covariant field equations were physically inadmissible because of the
so-called *Lochbetrachtung* — the observation that a coordinate transformation that is
the identity outside a region but non-trivial inside it produces two distinct metrics in
the region from the same matter distribution, apparently violating determinism. The
error was conceptual: the two metrics describe the same physical spacetime under
different coordinate labels, and physical determinism should be formulated on
equivalence classes under diffeomorphism, not on metric components. John Stachel's 1980
paper "Einstein's Search for General Covariance, 1912–1915" (in Howard and Stachel eds.,
*Einstein and the History of General Relativity*, Birkhäuser, 1989) is the standard
analysis.

**Initial rejection of Friedmann's expanding-universe solution (1922).** Einstein
submitted a critical note to the *Zeitschrift für Physik* arguing that Friedmann's
expanding-universe solution contained a calculational error. Friedmann showed by letter
that the error was in Einstein's reading. Einstein retracted his note in 1923 but
continued for several years to find dynamical cosmological solutions philosophically
uncomfortable.

**Hidden variables and the EPR program.** Bell's 1964 inequality and the experimental
tests from Freedman–Clauser 1972 through the loophole-free tests of 2015 falsify any
local hidden-variable theory of the kind Einstein expected to find behind quantum
mechanics. The intuition was wrong; the EPR paper itself, however, is foundational,
because the rigorous formulation of what Einstein wanted is what made Bell's inequality
and the experimental program possible.

**The unified field theory program (1923–1955).** Three decades of work on geometric
extensions of general relativity intended to absorb electromagnetism (and eventually
matter) into a single tensorial framework. The successive approaches — Kaluza–Klein
extensions, distant parallelism, non-symmetric metric tensors, asymmetric affine
connections — produced no equation that survived as canon. The mainstream of physics
moved past Einstein into quantum field theory and the Yang–Mills gauge program he never
accepted.

**The Solvay 1927 and 1930 thought experiments.** Each specific thought experiment
Einstein constructed against the uncertainty relations — the slit-and-shutter, the
photon-box — was answered by Bohr by morning. The deeper question Einstein was after —
completeness — outlived the specific failures and is what EPR formalized.

## 5. Lineage

**Teachers and immediate influences.** Heinrich Friedrich Weber at the ETH (the
conventional and unhelpful experimentalist; the "you'll never let yourself be told
anything" tradition); Hermann Minkowski (who taught him geometry, dismissed him at the
time as a "lazy dog", and later geometrized SR into the spacetime formalism); Adolf
Hurwitz (analysis at the ETH); Heinrich Burkhardt (number theory). Outside the
classroom: Maxwell's *Treatise* (which Einstein read in self-study during the
Polytechnic years and which is the immediate source of the conceptual problem of "what
would the world look like if I rode along with a light wave"); Hertz's experimental
confirmation of Maxwell; Lorentz's electron theory and the Lorentz transformations as a
kinematic device; Henri Poincaré's *La Science et l'Hypothèse* (read in the Akademie
Olympia 1902–1904); Ernst Mach's *Die Mechanik in ihrer Entwickelung* (the critique of
Newton's absolute space, carried by Einstein for decades); David Hume's *Treatise of
Human Nature* (the source of Einstein's epistemological skepticism toward unobservable
absolutes). Spinoza, throughout life, on questions of God and necessity.

**Working collaborators.** Marcel Grossmann (the mathematical scaffolding for general
relativity, 1912–1913); Michele Besso (the lifelong sounding board, the thanks at the
close of the 1905 SR paper); Mileva Marić (wife and intellectual interlocutor during the
Bern years, with debated extent of contribution to the 1905 papers — see §7); Jakob Laub
(Bern collaborator, 1908); Adriaan Fokker (Zürich and Leiden, 1913–1914, the
Einstein–Fokker equation); Hendrik Lorentz and Paul Ehrenfest (Leiden circle, the most
important sustained scientific friendships of his middle period); Walther Mayer
(Princeton mathematical assistant from 1929 onward); Banesh Hoffmann and Leopold Infeld
(Princeton, the 1938 Einstein–Infeld–Hoffmann equations of motion); Nathan Rosen
(Princeton, EPR 1935, also the Einstein–Rosen bridge and the Einstein–Rosen wave
solution of 1937); Boris Podolsky (Princeton, EPR 1935); Peter Bergmann and Valentine
Bargmann (Princeton, unified-field collaborators).

**Direct doctoral students.** Few. The Princeton position was a research chair without
teaching obligations; the Berlin and Zürich positions had occasional supervised students
but no large school. Hans Tanner, Jakob Laub and Heinrich Zangger were early Bern-era
close collaborators rather than formal doctoral students.

**Downstream.** Bohr, Heisenberg, Born, Schrödinger, Dirac (each of whom read Einstein
as the giant whose unfinished work they were continuing); Wheeler and Feynman in the
next generation (Feynman, who never met Einstein in scientific argument, treated the
relativity papers as the model of what theoretical physics is); every relativist after
1916; every quantum-information theorist after Bell; the entire Bose–Einstein-condensate
experimental community after 1995. The downstream is, in the narrowest fair sense, all
of twentieth- and twenty-first-century physics.

## 6. Controversies — fully detailed

**Hilbert priority on the GR field equations (November 1915).** Settled, with Einstein
the priority-holder on the field equations and the physical theory, after Corry, Renn
and Stachel (*Science* 278, 1270–1273, 1997) showed that the 20 November Hilbert proof
sheets did not contain the explicit equations and that the printed version was revised
after Einstein's 25 November communication. The variational derivation from a Lagrangian
(the Einstein–Hilbert action) is canonically Hilbert's. See Renn (ed.), *The Genesis of
General Relativity* (Springer 2007, 4 vols.), for the complete documentary record. The
dispute is now historical, not live.

**Mileva Marić's contribution to the 1905 papers.** The claim that Marić was a hidden
co-author of the annus-mirabilis papers was popularized by Senta Troemel-Ploetz, "Mileva
Einstein-Marić: The Woman Who Did Einstein's Mathematics", *Women's Studies
International Forum* 13(5), 415–432 (1990), and broadened in popular books. The
documentary record — examined in detail by John Stachel in the introduction to the
*Collected Papers* Vol. 1 and in *Einstein's Miraculous Year* (Princeton 1998) — shows
extensive intellectual companionship and discussion in the surviving Einstein–Marić
letters of 1897–1903 but no surviving evidence of authorship contribution to the 1905
papers themselves. The papers are signed by Einstein alone; manuscript drafts (where
they survive) are in his hand; no contemporary witness, including Marić's family and
biographers, claimed otherwise during her lifetime. The current scholarly consensus is
that Marić was a thoroughly engaged interlocutor but not a co-author. The claim is not
closed in popular discussion, but it is closed in the historical literature.

**Poincaré priority on special relativity.** Henri Poincaré's "Sur la dynamique de
l'électron", *Comptes Rendus de l'Académie des Sciences* 140, 1504–1508 (5 June 1905),
preceded Einstein's 30 June submission of "Zur Elektrodynamik bewegter Körper" by
twenty-five days. Poincaré had also stated a "principle of relativity" in his St. Louis
lecture of 1904 and had formulated the Lorentz group structure earlier. The standard
analysis (Olivier Darrigol, *Relativity Principles and Theories from Galileo to
Einstein*, Oxford 2022; Galison, *Einstein's Clocks, Poincaré's Maps*, Norton 2003)
finds that Poincaré had most of the mathematical structure but retained an ether and
treated the Lorentz transformations as a calculational device, while Einstein's
contribution was the operational reinterpretation of simultaneity and the elimination of
the ether as a physical entity. The two arrived independently — Einstein had not seen
Poincaré's June 1905 paper — and both deserve credit, with the conceptual reformulation
Einstein's. See also Arthur I. Miller, *Albert Einstein's Special Theory of Relativity*,
Springer 1981.

**The brain.** Removed by Thomas Stoltz Harvey at the autopsy on 18 April 1955 without
the family's permission. Harvey kept tissue, sectioned portions, sent samples to a small
number of researchers over the next several decades, and lost his Princeton Hospital
position over the matter. The principal published study, Marian Diamond et al., "On the
Brain of a Scientist: Albert Einstein", *Experimental Neurology* 88(1), 198–204 (1985),
reported a higher-than-control glia-to-neuron ratio in samples from area 39 of the left
inferior parietal lobule. The interpretation has been criticized for the inadequate
matched-control sample and for the small number of fields examined; Terence Hines,
"Neuromythology of Einstein's Brain", *Brain and Cognition* 88, 21–25 (2014), is the
dissenting review. Sandra Witelson, Debra Kigar and Thomas Harvey, "The exceptional
brain of Albert Einstein", *Lancet* 353, 2149–2153 (1999), reported anomalies in the
parietal-lobe lateral fissure. Dean Falk, Frederick Lepore and Adrianne Noe, "The
cerebral cortex of Albert Einstein: a description and preliminary analysis of
unpublished photographs", *Brain* 136, 1304–1327 (2013), examined the rediscovered
autopsy photographs. The biological interpretation of any of these findings, given the
absence of life-history controls and the n=1 sample, is best treated as suggestive
rather than established.

**Lieserl.** The illegitimate daughter of Einstein and Marić, born January 1902 in Novi
Sad. Existence revealed only in 1986 with the auctioned correspondence. Last surviving
reference: a letter from Einstein to Marić of 19 September 1903 mentioning her scarlet
fever. The two leading reconstructions — Michele Zackheim, *Einstein's Daughter: The
Search for Lieserl* (Riverhead 1999), and the analyses of the *Collected Papers*
editorial staff — converge on either an early death from scarlet-fever complications or
an adoption by a Marić-family connection in Kać. No documentary trace survives after
1903. Genuinely unresolved.

**The 1939 Szilárd letter.** Drafted by Leo Szilárd with input from Eugene Wigner and
Edward Teller, signed by Einstein on 2 August 1939, delivered by Alexander Sachs to
Roosevelt in October 1939. The letter is conventionally credited with prompting the
Advisory Committee on Uranium and the line of decisions leading to the Manhattan
Project. Einstein later regretted the signature: "Had I known that the Germans would not
succeed in producing an atomic bomb, I never would have lifted a finger." The remark
appears in his 1947 *Newsweek* interview ("The Real Cause of Hiroshima") and was
repeated to Linus Pauling in November 1954.

**Refusal of the Israeli presidency (1952).** The offer was conveyed by the Israeli
ambassador Abba Eban after the death of Chaim Weizmann. Einstein declined in a written
reply on 17 November 1952, citing a lifelong inability to deal with people in the way
official functions demand. The full text of the reply is in the Albert Einstein Archives
at Hebrew University of Jerusalem and is reprinted in Helen Dukas and Banesh Hoffmann
eds., *Albert Einstein: The Human Side* (Princeton 1979).

## 7. Politics, religion, public life

**Pacifism.** Einstein refused to sign the October 1914 Manifesto of the Ninety-Three
German intellectuals defending the German invasion of Belgium and signed instead the
Manifesto to the Europeans (Nicolai, Förster, Buek and Einstein) calling for European
reconciliation. He remained publicly pacifist through the 1920s, joining the War
Resisters' International, advocating draft refusal, and corresponding with Romain
Rolland and Rabindranath Tagore. He shifted from absolute to conditional pacifism in
1933, after Hitler's accession, recognizing that armed resistance to Nazi Germany was
unavoidable. After 1945 he advocated for international control of atomic weapons — the
Russell–Einstein Manifesto, drafted by Bertrand Russell and signed by Einstein four days
before his death, is the founding document of the Pugwash movement.

**Zionism.** Einstein supported the cultural and educational program of Zionism — the
Hebrew University of Jerusalem above all, which he helped found by traveling with
Weizmann to the United States in 1921 — while consistently rejecting militant
nationalism. He opposed partition in 1948 and called for binational arrangements with
the Arab population. The 1948 letter to *The New York Times* — Einstein, Hannah Arendt,
Sidney Hook and twenty-five other signatories, "New Palestine Party" (4 December 1948) —
denounced Menachem Begin's Herut party as "closely akin in its organization, methods,
political philosophy and social appeal to the Nazi and Fascist parties." He declined the
Israeli presidency in 1952.

**Socialism.** "Why Socialism?", *Monthly Review* 1(1), 9–15 (May 1949), is his most
concentrated political-economic statement: an argument that the predatory phase of
capitalism is the source of the major social pathologies of the period and that a
planned socialist economy with strong protection of individual rights is the
alternative. He distinguished his position from Soviet authoritarianism throughout.

**Civil rights.** A member of the NAACP from the 1940s. Friend of Paul Robeson and W. E.
B. Du Bois; offered to testify on Du Bois's behalf when the latter was indicted in 1951.
Delivered the 1946 Lincoln University commencement address ("the worst disease is the
treatment of the Negro... There is, however, a somber point in the social outlook of
Americans"). His correspondence with Marian Anderson, the contralto whose 1939
Constitution Hall booking was canceled by the Daughters of the American Revolution, is
preserved at Hebrew University.

**FBI file.** 1,427 pages, opened in May 1932 (during his Caltech visit) at the urging
of patriotic-association complaints, kept active through his death and beyond. The file
collected his pacifist, socialist and civil-rights associations and recommended against
his clearance for the Manhattan Project. Declassified under FOIA in 1983; the full file
is online at the FBI's Records Vault. Fred Jerome, *The Einstein File* (St. Martin's
2002), is the standard analysis. See also the cross-link in
`bucket-canon/08-deep-history/_intake/us-gov-declassified-deep-dive-2026-05-01.md`.

**Religion.** Not a personal-god theist; a Spinozist. The 1929 cable to Rabbi Herbert S.
Goldstein in New York: "I believe in Spinoza's God who reveals himself in the orderly
harmony of what exists, not in a God who concerns himself with fates and actions of
human beings." The 1930 essay "Religion and Science" (*New York Times Magazine*, 9
November 1930, then collected in *Ideas and Opinions*, Crown 1954) distinguishes the
religious feeling of awe at the lawfulness of nature from the personal-god religions and
from the morality-based religions, placing Einstein's own attitude in the first
category. The 1954 letter to Eric Gutkind, sold at auction in 2008 and again in 2018
(the second time for $2.9 million), is sharper in private: "The word God is for me
nothing more than the expression and product of human weaknesses, the Bible a collection
of honourable, but still primitive legends which are nevertheless pretty childish."

## 8. Archives

- **Albert Einstein Archives, Hebrew University of Jerusalem.** The master archive.
  Einstein bequeathed his papers to Hebrew University in his 1950 will; the archive is
  the home institution for the *Collected Papers* project's primary documentary work.
  Approximately 80,000 documents. Online catalog at alberteinstein.info.
- **Einstein Papers Project, California Institute of Technology, Pasadena.** The
  editorial home of the *Collected Papers* edition, founded in 1986; relocated from
  Boston University to Caltech in 2000. Director and General Editor: Diana L.
  Kormos-Buchwald. Working in partnership with Hebrew University and Princeton
  University Press.
- **Princeton University, Institute for Advanced Study Archives.** Einstein's
  Princeton-era institutional records, correspondence with Abraham Flexner, Oswald
  Veblen, John von Neumann and others.
- **Eidgenössische Technische Hochschule Zürich (ETH).** Student records 1896–1900, the
  *Akten der Schulleitung*; correspondence with Marcel Grossmann, Hermann Minkowski,
  Adolf Hurwitz; the Einstein–Besso correspondence in the ETH-Bibliothek
  Hochschularchiv.
- **Einstein House, Kramgasse 49, Bern.** The flat where Einstein lived during the annus
  mirabilis, now a museum.
- **Schwadron Autograph Collection, National Library of Israel, Jerusalem.** Holdings of
  Einstein letters acquired through Abraham Schwadron's autograph-collecting work.
- **Federal Bureau of Investigation, FBI Records Vault.** The full 1,427-page Einstein
  file, declassified 1983, online.
- **Boston University, Howard Gotlieb Archival Research Center.** Original Einstein
  Papers Project office holdings (transferred to Caltech in 2000 but with significant
  residual material).
- **Niels Bohr Archive, Copenhagen.** The Einstein–Bohr correspondence on quantum
  mechanics, 1920–1955.

## 10. Disputed and unresolved

- **The fate of Lieserl.** Last documentary trace September 1903. Most plausible
  reconstructions converge on early death from scarlet fever or adoption into a
  Marić-family connection near Kać. No surviving record after 1903.
- **The exact extent of Hilbert–Einstein priority on the GR field equations.** The
  Corry–Renn–Stachel 1997 *Science* analysis is the modern consensus; the proof-sheet
  evidence is partial (the cut portions of the December 1915 proofs are not all
  preserved) and a residual margin of historical uncertainty remains, although the
  substantive priority question is settled.
- **Interpretation of the Diamond, Witelson and Falk anatomical findings on the brain.**
  No biological inference about Einstein's cognition from anatomical anomalies in his
  brain tissue has the statistical support a serious neuroscientific claim requires
  (n=1, no proper matched controls, fractional sectioning).
- **The "I do not believe God plays dice" rhetorical line.** Documented in the 4
  December 1926 letter to Born; the various paraphrases attributed to later occasions
  are not all separately documented and may be conflations.
- **Bohr's "stop telling God what to do" rejoinder.** Substantively faithful to Bohr's
  position but not documented in his exact words at any specific Solvay session.
- **Authorship of the Manifesto to the Europeans (October 1914).** Conventionally
  attributed to Georg Friedrich Nicolai with Einstein's collaboration on the text. The
  relative contributions of the two are not fully documented.
- **The deathbed remarks (April 1955).** Reportedly in German; the only nurse on duty
  did not speak German; no recorded transcript survives. Whatever Einstein said in his
  final hours is lost.
