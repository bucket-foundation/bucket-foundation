# Isaac Newton

> Long-form contributor profile. Extends, does not replace, the contact card at
> `canon-figures/02-physics.md` § Isaac Newton.

This file exists because the standard ~250-word contact card cannot hold what Newton is for the canon. He is in the polymath register (`_polymaths.md`) on the strength of three distinct foundation-tier contributions from one working programme. He is also the source of more historiographical noise than almost any other figure in the index — the alchemical and theological corpora alone exceed in word-count everything he ever published in physics — and the bio has to handle that without either suppressing the noise or letting it eat the canon attribution.

The voice is plain. Newton was a vindictive, secretive, litigious, often paranoid man who happened to be the most consequential mathematical physicist in the European tradition. The bio reads as such.

## 1. Biographical chronology

### 1642–1661 — Lincolnshire and Grantham

- **25 December 1642 (Julian) / 4 January 1643 (Gregorian).** Born at Woolsthorpe Manor, Lincolnshire, three months after his father's death. Premature; biographers (Westfall 1980, ch. 1) record that he was not expected to live the day.
- **1645.** His mother Hannah Ayscough remarries the Reverend Barnabas Smith and moves to North Witham, leaving the three-year-old Newton to be raised by his maternal grandmother. The Manuel (1968) psychobiographical reading hangs much of Newton's adult character on this separation; Westfall is more cautious. The fact of the abandonment is not disputed.
- **c.1655–1659.** King's School, Grantham. Lodged with the apothecary William Clarke. Headmaster Henry Stokes is the first person to identify Newton as exceptional and to pressure his mother to send him on to university rather than back to manage the Woolsthorpe estate.
- **1659–1660.** Pulled out of school by Hannah to run the farm. Records (Westfall, ch. 2) indicate he was uninterested and incompetent at it; Stokes intervened and got him reinstated.

### 1661–1665 — Trinity College, Cambridge

- **5 June 1661.** Admitted to Trinity College as a *sub-sizar* — the lowest paying rank, performing menial service for wealthier undergraduates in exchange for reduced fees. His mother had remarried into modest wealth and could have paid; she did not.
- **1661–1664.** Standard scholastic curriculum (Aristotle, scholastic logic). Newton's surviving notebooks (the *Trinity Notebook* and the *Quaestiones quaedam philosophicae*, Cambridge University Library MSS Add. 3996) record him abandoning the syllabus in favor of Descartes' *Géométrie* (Schooten's Latin ed., 1659–61), Wallis' *Arithmetica Infinitorum* (1656), Galileo, Boyle, More, and Hobbes. The famous marginal heading *Amicus Plato amicus Aristoteles magis amica veritas* dates from this period.
- **April 1664.** Elected scholar — financial position improved; he was now a recognized member of the college on the academic track.
- **January 1665.** BA awarded.

### 1665–1667 — The *anni mirabiles* at Woolsthorpe

The plague closes Cambridge from August 1665 to spring 1667. Newton returns to Woolsthorpe. In a 1714 memorandum (Cambridge MS Add. 3968.41) he later compresses this period into the canonical claim that he "in those days … was in the prime of my age for invention, & minded Mathematicks & Philosophy more than at any time since." The historiography (Whiteside 1967–81; Westfall ch. 5) treats this self-report as broadly accurate but compressed: the work of 1665–66 is real, but the polished published versions are years later.

What he actually does in the plague years:
1. The **generalized binomial theorem** (winter 1664–65): expansion of `(1+x)^n` for arbitrary rational `n` via interpolation of Wallis' integral tables.
2. The **method of fluxions** (1665–66): the differential and integral calculus in geometric–kinematic dress, with `ẋ` notation for time-derivatives.
3. **Decomposition of white light** by prism (summer 1666 at Woolsthorpe; the experiments are systematized later at Cambridge in 1666–69). The crucial *experimentum crucis* — two prisms in sequence to show that refraction does not change the color — is later (1666–68).
4. **Inverse-square gravitation, in outline.** The famous apple anecdote derives from Newton's own late conversation with Stukeley (1726) and Conduitt's memorandum; the underlying calculation in 1666 compares the centripetal acceleration of the Moon with the surface gravity of the Earth and finds them to "answer pretty nearly." The full *Principia*-grade synthesis is two decades away.

### 1667–1669 — Return to Cambridge, Lucasian Chair

- **October 1667.** Elected minor fellow of Trinity.
- **March 1668.** MA. Elected major fellow.
- **1669.** Circulates *De Analysi per Aequationes Numero Terminorum Infinitas* via Barrow to John Collins — the first written exposition of his calculus. Not formally published until 1711 (William Jones).
- **29 October 1669.** Succeeds Isaac Barrow as **Lucasian Professor of Mathematics**. Barrow stepped down in part to clear the chair for him. Newton holds the chair until 1701.

### 1670s — Optics, the Royal Society, the first Hooke quarrel

- **1670–72.** Lucasian lectures on optics (the *Optical Lectures*, Cambridge MSS, edited by Shapiro 1984).
- **1671–72.** Designs and builds a reflecting telescope; second instrument sent to the Royal Society in 1671.
- **11 January 1672.** Elected Fellow of the Royal Society on the strength of the telescope.
- **6 February 1672.** Sends "A New Theory about Light and Colours" to the Society (read 8 February; published *Philosophical Transactions* 80). Hooke responds with a critique that frames Newton's prismatic results as compatible with his own (Hooke's) wave-pulse theory of light. Newton experiences the criticism as personal attack; the correspondence runs through 1675 and turns hostile. Newton withdraws from publication for nearly a decade.

### 1670s–1680s — Alchemy, theology, mathematical work

The two private programmes that consume the bulk of his actual writing time are now under way:

- **Alchemy.** From c.1668 to at least the late 1690s, Newton conducts laboratory chymistry at Trinity (the furnace shed near the chapel is documented). The surviving alchemical manuscripts (Keynes Collection, King's College Cambridge; Babson/Huntington; portions in the Portsmouth Collection) total ≈1,000,000 words — more than the *Principia* and *Opticks* combined. Modern critical edition: the Chymistry of Isaac Newton project (chymistry.org). Foundational secondary work: Dobbs, *The Foundations of Newton's Alchemy* (1975) and *The Janus Faces of Genius* (1991); Newman, *Newton the Alchemist* (2018) is the current synthesis.
- **Theology.** From c.1670 onward, Newton compiles a private case for **antitrinitarianism** — the view that the orthodox doctrine of the Trinity is a fourth-century corruption of original Christianity. He becomes, in private, an Arian. This is heresy under the Test Acts and would have cost him his fellowship; he conceals it for life. The surviving theological MSS are the Yahuda Collection (National Library of Israel, Jerusalem; ≈3 million words across all theological work) and parts of Keynes. Iliffe, *Priest of Nature* (2017) is the modern synthesis.

### 1684–1687 — Halley's prompt and the *Principia*

- **August 1684.** Edmond Halley visits Cambridge and asks Newton what curve a planet would follow under an inverse-square central force. Newton answers immediately: an ellipse. He cannot produce the calculation on demand but promises to send it. He sends a nine-page tract, *De motu corporum in gyrum*, in November.
- **1684–86.** Newton expands *De motu* into a three-book treatise. Halley personally underwrites publication, manages the printing, and absorbs Hooke's renewed (and partially valid) priority claim that he had communicated the inverse-square hypothesis in 1679–80 correspondence. Newton, furious, threatens to suppress Book III ("Philosophy is such an impertinently litigious Lady"). Halley talks him down.
- **5 July 1687.** *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* published. ≈300 copies, 510 pages, Latin, geometric proofs throughout (Newton withholds the analytical/fluxional methods he actually used). Three books: I — motion in free space; II — motion in resisting media (the weakest book; the analysis of fluid resistance is largely wrong, but the chapter on wave propagation is durable); III — *De mundi systemate*, applied to the actual solar system.

### 1689–1696 — Public life, breakdown, Mint

- **1689.** Elected MP for the University of Cambridge to the Convention Parliament after the Glorious Revolution. Sits ≈one year. Recorded as having spoken once, to ask that a window be closed.
- **1693.** Severe psychological breakdown — the so-called *black year*. Letters to Pepys and Locke accuse them, in turn, of plotting against him; he later retracts. Cause unclear; mercury poisoning from alchemical work has been hypothesized (Spargo and Pounds, *Notes Rec. Roy. Soc.* 1979, found elevated mercury in hair samples) but is not consensus. Westfall (ch. 12) treats it as a cumulative collapse rather than a single etiology.
- **March 1696.** Appointed **Warden of the Mint** through the patronage of Charles Montagu (Halifax). Moves to London. Expected as a sinecure; Newton turns it into a working position, personally prosecuting counterfeiters. **William Chaloner** — a serial coiner who had publicly attacked the Mint — is convicted and hanged at Tyburn 22 March 1699 on a case Newton built.
- **1699.** Promoted to **Master of the Mint**, the senior administrative position. He holds it until death; the salary plus profits made him wealthy.

### 1700s–1727 — Royal Society, *Opticks*, Leibniz war, knighthood, late editions

- **1701.** Resigns Lucasian Chair (Whiston succeeds him; Newton later has Whiston ejected on doctrinal grounds, 1710).
- **30 November 1703.** Elected **President of the Royal Society** following Hooke's death (March 1703). Holds the presidency until his own death.
- **1704.** *Opticks* published, in English. Includes two mathematical appendices: *Tractatus de Quadratura Curvarum* (his most polished public statement of the calculus) and *Enumeratio linearum tertii ordinis*.
- **16 April 1705.** Knighted by Queen Anne at Trinity College — the first scientist so honored, though the knighthood was political (election campaign), not scientific.
- **1708–1713.** **Leibniz priority dispute** escalates. Triggered by John Keill's *Philosophical Transactions* paper (1708) accusing Leibniz of plagiarism. Leibniz appeals to the Royal Society; the Society convenes a "neutral" committee whose report, the *Commercium Epistolicum* (1713), Newton himself anonymously edits and largely writes. The Society rules in Newton's favor. Leibniz dies 1716. Modern consensus (Hall, *Philosophers at War*, 1980): independent codiscovery, Newton priority on private development (≈1665–66), Leibniz priority on publication (1684 *Acta Eruditorum*) and on the superior notation that the field actually adopts.
- **1713.** Second edition of *Principia* (Roger Cotes, ed.). Major revisions to Book II and Book III; the *General Scholium* ("hypotheses non fingo") added.
- **1717.** Mint report on the gold-to-silver ratio. Newton, asked to advise on coinage, sets the guinea at 21 shillings and undervalues silver at the Mint; silver coin flows out of England as bullion, and Britain backs into a *de facto* gold standard. The standard is formalized by statute in 1816 but the monetary fact dates from Newton's report.
- **1726.** Third edition of *Principia* (Henry Pemberton, ed.).
- **20 March 1727 (Julian) / 31 March 1727 (Gregorian).** Dies in London. Buried in Westminster Abbey 4 April. The estate is intestate (he refused last sacraments — consistent with his Arian convictions — and made no will); the alchemical and theological MSS pass to Catherine Conduitt and from there into the dispersals that produce the Keynes, Yahuda, Babson and Portsmouth collections two centuries later.

## 3. Foundation contributions, mapped to bucket-canon branches

### 02-physics / classical-mechanics — *the* canon entry

The three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation, as set out in *Principia* Books I–III. The mechanism of the synthesis is the move that nobody before him completed: starting from the three laws as axioms (Book I, *Axiomata sive Leges Motus*), Newton **derives Kepler's three empirical laws from inverse-square central-force gravitation**, then in Book III applies the result to the actual solar system — Moon, planets, satellites of Jupiter and Saturn, comets, tides, the precession of the equinoxes.

Why canon: this *is* classical mechanics until 1915. It is also the first instance, in the European tradition, of a *complete* mathematical theory of nature — axioms, derivations, predictions, observational tests. Every subsequent physical theory is positioned relative to it: as extension (Lagrange, Hamilton), as reformulation (Hamilton–Jacobi, Lagrangian field theory), or as replacement in a regime where it fails (special relativity for `v → c`, general relativity for strong fields, quantum mechanics for `S ≈ ℏ`).

Canon entry (forthcoming per the 2026-05-01 physics-canon pass-1):
`bucket-canon/02-physics/classical-mechanics/1687-newton-principia.md`

### 01-mathematics / analysis — joint canon entry with Leibniz

The calculus of fluxions: differentiation as the fluxion `ẋ` of a fluent quantity `x`, integration as the inverse operation, the fundamental theorem of calculus as the relation between them. Newton's geometric/kinematic dress is conceptually identical to Leibniz's `dx, dy, ∫`. The math is the same; the notation is not.

Bucket's editorial call: **joint primary attribution with Leibniz** under a single canon entry, with the priority dispute documented in the entry rather than adjudicated. Newton's notation lost; his foundation didn't.

Canon entry (forthcoming):
`bucket-canon/01-mathematics/analysis/calculus-fluxions-fluxions.md` *(working title; will resolve to a joint Newton–Leibniz entry on bucketing)*

### 01-mathematics / algebra — borderline canon, originator-tier

The **generalized binomial theorem** (winter 1664–65; communicated to Wallis in the 1676 *Epistola Prior* and *Epistola Posterior*): the expansion of `(1+x)^n` for arbitrary rational `n` as an infinite series. Foundational for the analytic theory of power series. Originator-tier; bucket files this in the calculus canon entry rather than as a standalone.

### 02-physics / optics (cross-link only)

*Opticks* — corpuscular theory of light, dispersion experiments, the *experimentum crucis*, theory of color, Newton's-rings interference patterns (Book II), the queries (Book III). The corpuscular theory is wrong on the wave question — Young's 1801 double-slit and Fresnel's 1818 diffraction theory vindicated Huygens against Newton — but the *experimental* program defined optics for a century, and the queries are the source-text for an enormous amount of 18th-century physics. Quantum mechanics (1905+) partially rehabilitates the corpuscular intuition without rehabilitating Newton's specific mechanical model.

Bucket's call: not a separate canon-tier entry; cross-linked from the optics sub-branch under classical waves and from the early-modern experimental-physics scaffold.

### 03-chemistry — landscape, not canon

The alchemical corpus is voluminous, technically serious (Newman 2018 documents Newton as a competent practicing chymist by the standards of the late 17th century), and never reached the originator bar that Lavoisier's 1789 *Traité Élémentaire de Chimie* set for chemistry as a science. The MSS are primary sources for the **history** of pre-modern chemistry, not foundations of chemistry. Bucket's call: file as **landscape** in 03-chemistry (with a pointer to the Chymistry of Isaac Newton edition), not canon. Cross-listed in 08-tradition / history-of-science scaffolding.

### 06-cosmology — cross-link via *Principia* Book III

Universal gravitation as a cosmological claim: the same law governs the Moon, the planets, the satellites of Jupiter, the tides, and the shape of the Earth. Predicts the oblate spheroidal Earth (confirmed by the French Academy expeditions to Lapland 1736–37 and Peru 1735–44). Recovers Kepler's third law as a corollary. Cross-link only; the canon entry lives in 02-physics.

### 08-tradition / history of religion — landscape only

The chronological work (*The Chronology of Ancient Kingdoms Amended*, 1728) and the prophetic/exegetical work (*Observations*, 1733; *Two Notable Corruptions*, 1754). The *Chronology* tries to compress the conventional ancient timeline by ≈500 years using astronomical retrocalculation of the precession of the equinoxes against classical references; modern Egyptology and Mesopotamian chronology rejected the result. The exegetical work is intellectually serious 17th-century antitrinitarian textual criticism; modern New Testament textual criticism (Metzger, Ehrman) reaches the same conclusion on 1 John 5:7 (the *Comma Johanneum*) by independent route. Bucket's call: **landscape, not canon**, included for completeness.

## 4. Intellectual lineage

### Teachers, in the strict sense

- **Henry Stokes** (King's School, Grantham) — identified him, fought to keep him in school.
- **Isaac Barrow** (Lucasian Professor 1663–69; Newton's predecessor) — the conduit through which Newton's earliest mathematical work reached Collins and the wider mathematical community. Barrow's *Lectiones Geometricae* (1670) contains a geometric anticipation of the fundamental theorem of calculus that Newton certainly read.

### Influences absorbed (textual evidence in the *Trinity Notebook* and *Quaestiones*)

- **Descartes**, *Géométrie* (Schooten ed., 1659–61) and *Principia Philosophiae* (1644). Newton is a Cartesian in his early notebooks and an anti-Cartesian by the time of the *Principia*; the trajectory matters. The *Principia*'s very title is a deliberate contrast: not Descartes' *Principia Philosophiae* but *Principia Mathematica* — i.e., principles of natural philosophy that are *mathematical*.
- **Galileo**, *Discorsi* (1638). The kinematic results on uniformly accelerated motion are absorbed into Book I.
- **Kepler**, *Astronomia Nova* (1609) and *Harmonices Mundi* (1619). The empirical laws Newton derives.
- **Wallis**, *Arithmetica Infinitorum* (1656). Direct technical input to the binomial theorem.
- **Boyle**, especially the chymical work and the corpuscular theory of matter.
- **Hooke**, especially the 1679–80 correspondence on inverse-square central force. Newton would minimize the debt for the rest of his life; Westfall (ch. 10) treats Hooke's contribution as real and the credit dispute as substantively, if not legally, on Hooke's side.
- **Henry More** (Cambridge Platonist, family connection) — for the metaphysics of absolute space and time.

### Successors who carried the programme

- **William Whiston** — Newton's hand-picked successor in the Lucasian Chair (1701); ejected 1710 for publicly avowing the Arianism Newton kept private.
- **Roger Cotes** — editor of the 1713 *Principia* second edition; his preface is the canonical 18th-century statement of Newtonian methodology. Died 1716, age 33.
- **Henry Pemberton** — editor of the 1726 third edition; *A View of Sir Isaac Newton's Philosophy* (1728).
- **Colin MacLaurin** — *A Treatise of Fluxions* (1742), the standard British presentation that kept Newtonian fluxional notation alive in the British tradition for a century after the continent had moved to Leibniz.
- **The continental analytical-mechanics programme** — Clairaut, d'Alembert, Euler, Lagrange (*Mécanique Analytique*, 1788), Laplace (*Mécanique Céleste*, 1799–1825). This programme *completes* Newton's work — closes the three-body and lunar-theory problems Newton left open, recasts the geometric proofs in analytic form, and produces the predictive astronomical machinery that runs unchallenged until Mercury's perihelion advance in the late 19th century.

## 5. Controversies and what he got wrong

### Hooke priority on inverse-square gravitation

The 1679–80 correspondence (Newton–Hooke, in Turnbull ed., *Correspondence* vol. II) shows Hooke proposing the inverse-square attraction explicitly and asking Newton to derive the resulting orbit; Hooke's *Attempt to Prove the Motion of the Earth* (1674) had earlier stated the inverse-square hypothesis as a conjecture without derivation. Newton's *Principia* 1st ed. (1687) acknowledges Hooke; the 2nd (1713) and 3rd (1726) editions demote the acknowledgement. Modern consensus (Westfall ch. 10; Cohen *Guide* 1999): Hooke had the hypothesis, Newton had the derivation, the derivation is the foundation; but Newton's behavior on the credit was vindictive.

### Leibniz priority on the calculus

Triggered by John Keill in 1708; escalated to the Royal Society 1711–13. The *Commercium Epistolicum* (1713) was anonymously edited and substantively written by Newton himself (he also wrote the anonymous review of the *Commercium* in *Philosophical Transactions*). Modern consensus (Hall, *Philosophers at War*, 1980; Bardi, *The Calculus Wars*, 2006):

- Independent codiscovery.
- Newton priority on private development (≈1665–66).
- Leibniz priority on publication (1684).
- Leibniz priority on the notation that survived.
- The dispute set British mathematics back roughly a century by isolating it from continental analysis.

### Wave theory of light

Newton's corpuscular model (*Opticks* Book II–III) was overturned by Young (1801, double slit) and Fresnel (1818, diffraction). Maxwell (1865) made light a wave in the electromagnetic field. Quantum mechanics (Einstein 1905, photoelectric effect) restored a particulate aspect — but a quantum-field-theoretic photon, not a Newtonian corpuscle. Newton's wave/particle intuition is partially vindicated; his specific mechanical model is not.

### Absolute space and time

The *Scholium* to the *Definitions* in *Principia* asserts absolute space and absolute time as metaphysical substrata. Mach's critique (*Die Mechanik in ihrer Entwicklung*, 1883) attacks the absolute-space claim using the bucket experiment; Einstein's special (1905) and general (1915) relativity demolish both absolutes. Newton's metaphysics is wrong; his mechanics is a low-velocity, weak-field, large-action approximation that remains exact in its regime.

### Alchemy and theology — historiographical posture

Roughly 50% of Newton's lifetime word-count is alchemical and theological. Reception ranges across:

- **Brewster** (*Memoirs of the Life, Writings, and Discoveries of Sir Isaac Newton*, 1855): treats the alchemy as embarrassing senile aberration and downplays the theology.
- **Keynes** (1947 lecture, *Newton, the Man*, delivered posthumously by his brother): "Newton was not the first of the age of reason. He was the last of the magicians." The Keynes auction purchase of the alchemical MSS in 1936 is the historiographical pivot.
- **Westfall** (1980): treats alchemy and theology as **integral** to Newton's natural philosophy, not embarrassing add-ons.
- **Dobbs** (1975, 1991): the alchemy is the bridge by which active principles (gravitation, cohesion, fermentation) enter Newton's natural philosophy; the *Principia*'s "active force" language is alchemical in lineage.
- **Newman** (2018): treats Newton as a competent practicing chymist who pursued the transmutation programme as a serious empirical research project, not as occult dabbling.
- **Iliffe** (2017): the theology is the deepest layer of Newton's intellectual life and the antitrinitarianism is the fixed point against which the natural philosophy is organized.

Bucket's neutral statement of the historiographical landscape: the modern scholarly consensus treats alchemy and theology as **central to Newton's working life and integral to his natural philosophy**, while still treating only the *Principia*-and-*Opticks* programme as canon-tier in the bucket sense (foundations of an active scientific discipline). The alchemy and theology are landscape, not because they were trivial to Newton, but because the disciplines they belong to either dissolved (alchemy → chemistry, where Lavoisier is the originator) or sit outside the seven-branch canon scope (Christian apocalyptic exegesis).

### Mint career

- Personally directed prosecution of counterfeiters; **William Chaloner** hanged 22 March 1699 on a case Newton built (Levenson, *Newton and the Counterfeiter*, 2009, is the popular-history treatment; the primary documents are in MINT 15 / 19 at TNA Kew).
- The 1717 silver-coinage report — Newton, asked to advise on the gold-to-silver ratio, set the guinea at 21 shillings. The implied silver price was below market; silver coin was melted and exported. Britain backed into a *de facto* gold standard, formalized 1816. Newton did not advocate the gold standard as policy; he advocated repricing the guinea, and the gold standard was the unintended monetary consequence.

### Personality

Westfall's *Never at Rest* (CUP 1980) is the standard scholarly assessment: Newton was secretive, vindictive, prone to paranoid reading of normal criticism, capable of sustained personal vendettas (Hooke, Flamsteed, Leibniz, Whiston), and almost certainly clinically depressed during 1693. He never married and the question of his sexuality is recurrent in the biographies (Manuel 1968 reads him as repressed; Westfall declines to speculate). The Flamsteed quarrel — Newton, as President of the Royal Society, forced publication of Flamsteed's *Historia Coelestis Britannica* (1712) over Flamsteed's objections, then was sued by Flamsteed and lost on appeal in 1716 — is the clearest documentary case of the institutional vindictiveness.

This is consensus. The bio reports it as such.

## 9. Editorial judgments made in this card

Two non-trivial calls the bio takes a position on rather than reporting consensus:

1. **The 1717 silver-coinage report as the *de facto* origin of the British gold standard.** This framing is standard in monetary history (Feavearyear, *The Pound Sterling*, 1931, 2nd ed. 1963; Quinn, *Gold, Silver, and the Glorious Revolution*, 1996) but is occasionally contested by historians who treat the gold standard as a 19th-century institutional creation that the 1717 report only foreshadowed. The bio takes the monetary-fact-versus-statutory-formalization distinction explicitly.
2. **Joint primary attribution of the calculus to Newton and Leibniz under one canon entry rather than two.** The Hall (1980) consensus is "independent codiscovery"; the historiographical convention in mathematics has been to write parallel histories. Bucket's editorial move — one canon entry, two contributors — is a *bucket* decision, not a historiographical one. Documented here so that the canon entry inherits the framing rather than re-litigating it.
