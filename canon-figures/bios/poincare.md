## 1. Life

### Nancy (1854–1873)

Jules Henri Poincaré was born on 29 April 1854 in Nancy, in the Meurthe department of
the Second French Empire, into a family of the Lorraine bourgeoisie that produced more
public figures in a single generation than most provincial families produce in three.
His father, Léon Poincaré, was a physician and professor of medicine at the Faculty of
Nancy. His mother, Eugénie Launois, came from a comfortable Lorraine family and ran the
household with a discipline that Poincaré's later memoirists agreed was the dominant
shaping presence of his childhood. He had a younger sister, Aline, who married the
philosopher Émile Boutroux in 1878 and through whom Henri Poincaré became uncle by
marriage to a small constellation of French academic figures.

The cousin who would later loom largest in any popular biographical sketch was Raymond
Poincaré, born 1860, who would serve as President of the French Republic from 1913 to
1920 — the year after Henri's death — and would dominate the politics of the Third
Republic during the First World War. Henri and Raymond were close in childhood and
remained on warm if intermittent terms throughout their adult lives; the two represented
the same Lorraine bourgeois milieu turning its energies in two different directions, one
into pure abstraction and the other into the legal-political machine of the Republic.
The shared name has been a source of confusion in popular accounts ever since.

The decisive event of Poincaré's childhood was the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–1871. He
was sixteen when the Prussian army occupied Nancy in August 1870, and he saw the
occupation at close range; the family did volunteer ambulance work, and Henri served as
a translator-aide to his father in the field. The annexation of Alsace and a sliver of
Lorraine to the new German Empire, ratified by the Treaty of Frankfurt in May 1871,
marked the entire generation of French intellectuals from Lorraine and shaped the
revanchist undertone that runs quietly through much of Poincaré's later public writing
on science and the nation. Nancy itself remained French, but it now sat on a hostile
border, and the Polytechnique generation that came up after 1871 understood mathematics
and engineering as instruments of national reconstruction.

Poincaré's school career was prodigious in the manner the French system specialises in
recognising. He was a slow and physically clumsy child — extreme nearsightedness that
went uncorrected for years, a lack of manual coordination that would persist into
adulthood and made him notoriously bad at simple drawing — but his memory was almost
total and his capacity for sustained abstract concentration was clear early. At the
Lycée de Nancy (now the Lycée Henri-Poincaré, renamed after his death) he won the
Concours général first prize in elementary mathematics in 1872 and the special prize in
mathematics the following year, the highest honours then available to a French
secondary student.

### École Polytechnique and Mines de Paris (1873–1879)

In October 1873 Poincaré entered the École Polytechnique in Paris, ranked first in the
entrance examination — the place that the *X* tradition treats as a kind of national
auspex. His two years at Polytechnique placed him under the lectures of Charles Hermite
in analysis and Joseph Bertrand in mechanics; he graduated in 1875, second in his class
behind François Bonnefoy. The story repeated by his classmates was that the only reason
Poincaré ranked second was that the descriptive-geometry examination required graphical
work that Poincaré could not execute neatly enough for full marks, although the
underlying solutions were perfect. Hermite, who would supervise Poincaré's doctoral
work, reportedly said of him during this period that he had a mind that did not need to
calculate.

After Polytechnique, Poincaré followed the standard *X-Mines* career path into the
École des Mines, the elite engineering school that supplied the corps of mining
engineers to the French state. He graduated as an *ingénieur des mines* in March 1879
and was posted briefly to the Vesoul region as a working mining engineer; an account
survives of him descending into the Magny mine after a firedamp explosion in late 1879
and writing the official accident report. He also continued his doctoral research in
parallel under Hermite at the Sorbonne, defending his doctoral thesis *Sur les
propriétés des fonctions définies par les équations aux différences partielles* on 1
August 1879. Gaston Darboux, who sat on the jury, later wrote that the thesis was the
work of a mathematician who already knew more than any of his examiners about the
subject he was treating, and that several of the side results, casually inserted, would
have been theses on their own.

### Caen, then Paris (1879–1912)

In December 1879 Poincaré was appointed *maître de conférences* (lecturer) in
mathematical analysis at the Faculty of Sciences in Caen. He stayed there only twenty
months. The most consequential mathematical event of his life happened during this
short Caen period: between late 1880 and early 1881 he developed the theory of what
Felix Klein would prefer to call automorphic functions and Poincaré insisted on calling
*Fuchsian* and *Kleinian* functions, after Lazarus Fuchs and Klein himself. The famous
account, recorded by Poincaré in the address "L'invention mathématique" (later
collected in *Science et méthode*, 1908), describes the moment of insight as he was
boarding an omnibus at Coutances during a geological excursion, mid-conversation, with
no apparent warning: the realization that the transformations he had been using to
define a new class of functions were identical with those of non-Euclidean geometry. It
is the most-quoted single anecdote in the literature on mathematical creativity, and
the only first-person primary source for the idea that mathematical invention works by
a sudden conscious surfacing of subconsciously prepared material.

In October 1881 Poincaré was called to the Faculty of Sciences in Paris as a
*maître de conférences* in analysis, and from that point he never left Paris again
except for short trips and one celebrated visit to the United States (the St Louis
World's Fair Congress of Arts and Science of 1904, where he gave the lecture
"L'état actuel et l'avenir de la physique mathématique" that contains the first
public statement of what would become the "principle of relativity" in his sense of
the term). At Paris he was successively chair of mathematical physics and probability
(1886), chair of mathematical astronomy and celestial mechanics (1896), and chair of
mathematical physics again (1900). He was also from 1893 a member of the Bureau des
Longitudes, the body that adjudicated French time-keeping, the meridian of Paris, the
geodetic survey, and the synchronization of telegraphic time signals across the
French colonial network. The Bureau work matters because it is where Poincaré
encountered, as a working administrator and not as a philosopher, the practical
problem of synchronizing distant clocks by light or telegraphic signal — the empirical
substrate that fed directly into "La mesure du temps" (1898) and through that into the
1904–1906 relativity papers. Peter Galison's *Einstein's Clocks, Poincaré's Maps*
(2003) is the canonical study of this connection.

He was elected to the Académie des sciences on 31 January 1887, at the age of 32,
filling the seat in the geometry section vacated by the death of Edmond Laguerre. He
served as president of the Academy in 1906. He was elected to the Académie française —
the literary academy, the *forty immortals*, the rarer and more politically prestigious
of the two — on 5 March 1908, succeeding Sully Prudhomme; the election was treated in
the press as a recognition that scientific writing in French had reached the level of
literature, and Poincaré's three popular books (*La Science et l'hypothèse*, *La Valeur
de la science*, *Science et méthode*) were the cited evidence.

He married Louise Poulain d'Andecy in April 1881. They had four children: three
daughters and one son. The household in Paris on the rue Claude-Bernard, and later in
the apartment on the rue Claude-Bernard taken over by his widow, was according to all
witnesses orderly, quiet, and built around the rhythm of Poincaré's working day, which
was famously short — four hours in the morning and two in the late afternoon, with the
intermediate and evening hours given over to family, walking, and reading.

### Death (1912)

Poincaré had suffered for several years from what was probably a benign prostatic
condition; in early July 1912 he underwent surgery from which he initially seemed to
recover. On 17 July 1912 he died suddenly of an embolism while dressing, at the age of
58. He had given his last public lecture, on the new mechanics, only three weeks
earlier; he had submitted a final paper on Fuchsian functions and number theory the
previous month. The death was unexpected enough to cause real public shock in France,
and the funeral procession through Paris was attended by representatives of every
French scientific institution. He is buried in the Poincaré family vault at the
Montparnasse cemetery, across the city from the Pantheon where his cousin Raymond would
later be buried after a state funeral.

He left behind unfinished projects in three of the fields he had worked in
simultaneously: a planned fourth volume of the *Méthodes nouvelles*, a long-expected
treatise on probability and statistical mechanics, and the next instalment of his
project of unifying topology and analysis. The eleven-volume *Œuvres de Henri
Poincaré* (Gauthier-Villars, 1916–1956), edited by a succession of his colleagues and
students, is the closest the field has come to gathering what he completed.

## 2. Branches

### 2.1. 01-mathematics

#### Automorphic and Fuchsian functions (1881–1884)

The theory developed in the omnibus at Coutances was published in a sequence of
substantial memoirs in the first volumes of Mittag-Leffler's new journal *Acta
Mathematica*: "Théorie des groupes fuchsiens" (vol. 1, 1882, pp. 1–62), "Mémoire sur
les fonctions fuchsiennes" (vol. 1, 1882, pp. 193–294), "Mémoire sur les groupes
kleinéens" (vol. 3, 1883, pp. 49–92), and "Mémoire sur les fonctions zétafuchsiennes"
(vol. 5, 1884, pp. 209–278). The mathematical move underneath the whole sequence is
the recognition that the discrete subgroups of the group of Möbius transformations
acting on the upper half-plane and on the disk give rise to a class of meromorphic
functions invariant under those subgroups, and that the resulting function theory is
the natural setting for the uniformization of algebraic curves. The Fuchsian groups
are exactly the discrete subgroups of PSL(2,ℝ) that act on the hyperbolic plane; the
Kleinian groups are the analogous subgroups of PSL(2,ℂ) acting on the Riemann sphere.

This work was carried out in intense correspondence with Felix Klein, who was thinking
in parallel along closely related lines from his Erlangen-programme starting point.
The Klein–Poincaré correspondence of 1881–1882 (collected in volume 11 of the *Œuvres*)
is a primary document in the history of the subject and shows two mathematicians
arriving at the same theory from opposite directions — Klein from the group-theoretic
classification of geometries, Poincaré from the differential equations of Fuchs. The
priority dispute was conducted entirely through their letters and never spilled into
print; the resolution was effectively that Klein got the better name (*automorphic
functions*) and Poincaré kept his (*Fuchsian*, *Kleinian*) for the special cases. The
strain of working at Klein's pace through that year contributed to Klein's nervous
breakdown in late 1882, after which Klein largely abandoned creative work for editing
and teaching.

The uniformization theorem in its full generality — that every Riemann surface is
covered by the Riemann sphere, the complex plane, or the disk — was conjectured at the
end of the Fuchsian functions sequence and was finally proved independently by Poincaré
("Sur l'uniformisation des fonctions analytiques", *Acta Mathematica* 31, 1907,
pp. 1–63) and Paul Koebe in 1907. The 1907 paper closes the project Poincaré opened in
1881, and is the foundation on which all of subsequent Riemann surface theory rests.

#### *Méthodes nouvelles de la mécanique céleste* and the discovery of chaos (1890–1899)

In 1885 King Oscar II of Sweden and Norway, advised by Mittag-Leffler, announced a
prize competition for a memoir on a question chosen from a list of four. The question
that attracted Poincaré was the one Weierstrass had drafted: to determine whether the
solar system, modelled as a system of point masses interacting by Newtonian gravitation,
is stable in the long term. Poincaré entered a memoir on a restricted form of the
problem — the three-body problem with one body of negligible mass — and was awarded the
prize in January 1889. The memoir was scheduled to appear in *Acta Mathematica*.

In December 1889, while the volume was being printed, Lars Edvard Phragmén, a young
editor at *Acta*, asked Poincaré for clarification of a step in the proof of one of the
stability theorems. Poincaré checked, found that the step was wrong, and over the
following weeks worked out that the error was not local but invalidated the central
stability result. He paid out of his own funds for the printed copies of the original
memoir to be recalled and destroyed (the cost — 3585 kronor — was greater than the
prize itself), and rewrote the memoir. The corrected version, *Sur le problème des
trois corps et les équations de la dynamique*, appeared in *Acta Mathematica* vol. 13
in November 1890 (pp. 1–270). The corrected memoir contained, in place of the original
stability theorem, the first description of what we now call homoclinic intersections —
points where the stable and unstable manifolds of a periodic orbit cross transversally,
forcing the dynamics in their neighbourhood into infinitely intricate, sensitively
dependent behaviour. Poincaré described the resulting picture in a famous passage:
"one will be struck by the complexity of this figure, which I shall not even attempt to
draw." The acknowledgement that the stability question could not be answered by the
methods then available, and that the obstacle was not technical but a real feature of
the dynamics, is the first identification of what was later called chaotic dynamics.
The June Barrow-Green monograph *Poincaré and the Three-Body Problem* (American
Mathematical Society, 1997) is the canonical study of this episode and the
corresponding archival material.

The three volumes of *Les méthodes nouvelles de la mécanique céleste* (Gauthier-Villars,
1892, 1893, 1899) extend and systematize the prize memoir into a full qualitative
theory of differential equations applied to celestial mechanics. The main innovations
are: the systematic use of *Poincaré sections* (the reduction of the flow of a
differential equation in *n* dimensions to an iterated map on a transverse hypersurface
of dimension *n*−1); the classification of periodic orbits by their stability indices;
the introduction of *integral invariants* (later recognised as forms preserved by the
Hamiltonian flow, and reorganised into the modern symplectic geometry by Élie Cartan);
the proof of the recurrence theorem (almost every orbit of a measure-preserving flow on
a finite-volume phase space returns arbitrarily close to its initial point, infinitely
often); the analysis of asymptotic, doubly asymptotic, and homoclinic solutions; and
the demonstration that perturbative series expansions for the planetary problem are
formally divergent, although they may be useful as asymptotic series. The companion
*Leçons de mécanique céleste* (3 vols., 1905–1910) is the more elementary, lecture-room
version of the same material.

The contemporary read of the *Méthodes nouvelles* was that it was the masterwork of a
French analyst applying his methods to astronomy. The retrospective read, after the
work of George David Birkhoff in the 1910s and 1920s, of Andrey Kolmogorov in 1954, of
Vladimir Arnold and Jürgen Moser in the early 1960s (the KAM theorem), and of Stephen
Smale on stable manifolds and the horseshoe in the same decade, is that Poincaré had
invented the qualitative theory of dynamical systems wholesale, and that the chaos
theory rediscovered by Edward Lorenz in 1963 and popularised by James Yorke and others
in the 1970s was the recovery of structures Poincaré had described seventy years earlier
and that had then largely fallen out of view in the West.

#### *Analysis Situs* and the founding of algebraic topology (1895–1904)

Poincaré published "Analysis Situs" in the *Journal de l'École Polytechnique* (2nd
series, vol. 1, 1895, pp. 1–121). The text, together with its five supplements,
constitutes the founding document of algebraic topology. The supplements appeared as:
"Complément à l'Analysis Situs" (*Rendiconti del Circolo Matematico di Palermo* 13,
1899, pp. 285–343); "Second complément" (*Proceedings of the London Mathematical
Society* 32, 1900, pp. 277–308); "Sur certaines surfaces algébriques; troisième
complément" (*Bulletin de la Société Mathématique de France* 30, 1902, pp. 49–70);
"Sur les cycles des surfaces algébriques; quatrième complément" (*Journal de
mathématiques pures et appliquées*, 5th series, vol. 8, 1902, pp. 169–214); and
"Cinquième complément à l'Analysis Situs" (*Rendiconti del Circolo Matematico di
Palermo* 18, 1904, pp. 45–110).

The 1895 paper introduces the notation and the basic apparatus that the field has used
ever since. It defines the concept of a manifold by parametric and implicit
representations; it introduces the simplicial decomposition of a manifold and the
incidence numbers between simplices; it defines the homology of a manifold via
boundaries of chains and proves the duality theorem for closed orientable manifolds
that has been called Poincaré duality ever since; it defines the *fundamental group*
(the group of homotopy classes of loops based at a point) and shows that it is a
topological invariant; and it computes the first nontrivial examples — lens spaces,
torus bundles, three-manifolds obtained as quotients of the three-sphere by finite
group actions. The five supplements deepen the treatment, correct errors, and treat
intersection numbers, the homology of algebraic surfaces, and the homology of the
complement of an algebraic curve in projective space.

The Fifth Supplement (1904) is where Poincaré states what would become known as the
Poincaré conjecture. He had first asked, at the end of the Second Supplement, whether a
closed three-manifold whose homology agreed with that of the three-sphere had to be
homeomorphic to the three-sphere. In the Fifth Supplement he constructs an explicit
counterexample: the *Poincaré homology sphere*, a closed orientable three-manifold
obtained as the quotient of SO(3) by the binary icosahedral group of order 120, with
the same homology as S³ but with nontrivial fundamental group (the binary icosahedral
group itself, of order 120, perfect, finite). Having ruled out the homological
characterisation, Poincaré reformulates the question: is a closed simply-connected
three-manifold necessarily homeomorphic to the three-sphere? He calls the answer
"hors de mes considérations" — outside the scope of his treatment — and leaves the
question open. The question stayed open for ninety-eight years. It was resolved in the
affirmative by Grigori Perelman in three preprints posted to the arXiv in 2002 and
2003, by way of Richard Hamilton's Ricci flow programme and Perelman's introduction of
the entropy and reduced-volume monotonicity arguments and the analysis of κ-solutions.
Perelman was offered the Fields Medal in 2006 (declined) and the Clay Millennium Prize
in 2010 (declined). The Poincaré conjecture is the only Clay Millennium Problem to
have been solved and the longest-standing single conjecture in topology to have been
resolved within its parent field.

The 1895 *Analysis Situs* did not immediately create a discipline; the supplements were
needed because the original paper contained errors that the structure of the field at
the time could not catch. Algebraic topology in its modern, rigorous form did not
crystallise until the work of Solomon Lefschetz, J. W. Alexander, Heinz Hopf, and the
introduction of axiomatic homology theory by Eilenberg and Steenrod in the 1940s. But
every concept in that later edifice is a development of something Poincaré named in
*Analysis Situs* and its supplements. The textbook tradition's habit of citing
Poincaré 1895 as "the founding paper" is, in this case, not rhetorical inflation; it is
a literal description of where the apparatus of the field was first written down.

#### *Calcul des probabilités* (1896)

The *Calcul des probabilités* (Gauthier-Villars, 1896; second edition, substantially
revised, 1912) is Poincaré's lectures on probability at the Sorbonne, written up as a
treatise. It is not foundational in the way Kolmogorov's 1933 axiomatisation is — it
predates Lebesgue and uses the older calculus of analytical probability — but it is the
text where the *method of arbitrary functions* is first systematically developed (the
argument that the probability distribution of a random outcome insensitive to the
initial probability distribution under sufficient mixing is a feature of the dynamics,
not the prior). This argument is the early formal connection between dynamical-systems
ideas and probability and is one of the routes by which Poincaré's recurrence theorem
fed into the later Boltzmann–Gibbs ergodic theory.

### 2.2. 02-physics

#### "Sur la dynamique de l'électron" (1905–1906) and the priority question

The relativity papers proper are two: a short note "Sur la dynamique de l'électron"
in the *Comptes rendus de l'Académie des sciences* of 5 June 1905 (vol. 140,
pp. 1504–1508), and a long paper of the same title in the *Rendiconti del Circolo
Matematico di Palermo* vol. 21 (1906), pp. 129–176, written in July 1905 and submitted
to Palermo on 23 July 1905. Together with the philosophical preparatory paper "La
mesure du temps" (*Revue de métaphysique et de morale* 6, 1898, pp. 1–13) and the St
Louis lecture of 1904, they constitute Poincaré's contribution to special relativity.

The Palermo paper does the following. It identifies the Lorentz transformations as a
group, names the parameter that we now call the relativistic factor, and proves that
the group has the structure (in modern terms) of SO(3,1)⁺. It writes Maxwell's
equations in a form manifestly invariant under this group. It proposes a relativistic
correction to the law of gravitation so that the gravitational interaction propagates
at the speed of light and the resulting equations of motion are Lorentz-invariant — a
proposal that turns out to be wrong in detail but right in spirit, and that is the
first published attempt at a relativistic theory of gravity. It introduces the
four-vector formalism (Poincaré writes the imaginary time coordinate *ict* and treats
it as the fourth coordinate of a four-dimensional space, the move that Hermann Minkowski
would in 1908 turn into the Minkowski-spacetime formulation). And it states what
Poincaré had already named in St Louis as the *principe de relativité* — the principle
that the equations of physics should take the same form in any reference frame moving
uniformly with respect to any other.

Einstein's "Zur Elektrodynamik bewegter Körper" was submitted to *Annalen der Physik*
on 30 June 1905 — about three weeks after Poincaré's 5 June 1905 *Comptes rendus* note,
about three weeks before Poincaré's submission of the long Palermo paper to Palermo on
23 July 1905, and about four months before Einstein's paper appeared in print
(September 1905). The two men did not correspond and almost certainly did not see each
other's work in 1905. The independent and near-simultaneous publication is genuine.

What Einstein did and Poincaré did not do is the conceptual move. Einstein took the
constancy of the speed of light in all inertial frames as a *postulate* — an empirical
fact about the world to be assumed and reasoned from — and derived the Lorentz
transformations as a consequence of that postulate together with the principle of
relativity. Poincaré reached the same transformations from the opposite direction: he
took the Lorentz transformations as the empirically required modification of the
electromagnetic theory to accommodate Michelson–Morley, and he treated the speed of
light's invariance as a derived consequence of those transformations applied to a
medium, the ether, that he never abandoned. He continued to speak of the ether as a
real physical substrate after 1905. He continued to treat Lorentz contraction as a
physical contraction of bodies moving through the ether rather than as a kinematic
consequence of the geometry of spacetime. He did not draw the inference that the
relativity of simultaneity dissolved the ether's privileged frame. He stopped at a
point that, with hindsight, looks like deliberate philosophical caution but at the time
read as a failure to take the second postulate seriously.

The honest assessment, which is the one offered in the modern historical literature
(see Galison 2003, Darrigol 2004, and the survey in Pais 1982 *Subtle Is the Lord*) is
that the mathematical formalism of special relativity was completed independently by
Lorentz, Poincaré, and Einstein between 1900 and 1906, with Poincaré contributing the
group structure, the four-vector formulation, and the gravitational-propagation
proposal; that the conceptual reformulation that consists in *taking the postulates as
the foundation and re-deriving everything*, including the relativity of simultaneity,
the kinematic interpretation of length contraction, and the dispensability of the
ether, is Einstein's; and that the conceptual move is what made everything that came
after — general relativity in particular — accessible. Priority on the formalism is
genuinely shared. Priority on the conceptual leap is Einstein's. Poincaré, who lived
seven more years after Einstein's 1905 paper, never publicly endorsed Einstein's
formulation and continued in lectures to present what he had himself developed; he
also, characteristically, never publicly disputed Einstein's priority, and the silence
on his side has been interpreted by different historians in different ways.

#### Three-body problem and celestial mechanics (1890 onward)

The 1890 prize memoir on the three-body problem is described above under
01-mathematics; its physics content is the first qualitative analysis of a real
celestial-mechanical system and the first rigorous result on the limits of
predictability of the solar system. The line that runs from this memoir through the
Birkhoff–Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser tradition into modern Solar-System dynamics terminates
in Jacques Laskar's numerical demonstrations from 1989 onward that the inner solar
system is chaotic on a Lyapunov timescale of about five million years, and that the
long-term stability of Mercury's orbit is a genuinely open question with a non-trivial
probability of escape or collision over the remaining lifetime of the Sun. This is a
direct descendant of the 1890 memoir's negative result on the original Oscar prize
question.

### 2.3. 06-cosmology

Poincaré's contribution to cosmology is indirect. He did not propose a cosmological
model. The thread is the dynamical-systems treatment of the *n*-body problem, which —
through Birkhoff in the 1910s and 1920s and through KAM theory in the 1950s and
1960s — became the substrate on which the modern theory of the long-term stability of
planetary systems and of galactic dynamics is built. Laskar's work, and the analogous
work of Wisdom and Sussman on the outer planets, are the descendants. The Poincaré
recurrence theorem is also one of the technical ingredients in the modern discussion of
recurrence in cosmological models with finite phase space (the so-called Poincaré
recurrence paradox in eternal inflation). The cross-branch placement is on these
grounds rather than on any direct cosmological work of Poincaré's.

### 2.4. 07-mind — philosophy of science

The three popular books — *La Science et l'hypothèse* (1902), *La Valeur de la science*
(1905), and *Science et méthode* (1908), all published by Flammarion in the
*Bibliothèque de philosophie scientifique* — are Poincaré's philosophical statement.
They are short, written for the educated general reader, and were among the
best-selling French books of the early twentieth century; *La Science et l'hypothèse*
sold more than 16,000 copies in its first decade, was translated into German, English,
Spanish, Hungarian, Japanese, and Russian within twelve years, and was the book
Einstein later said he and his Bern friends in the Olympia Academy read aloud to each
other "for weeks on end" in 1903. The historical importance of the trilogy is partly
intrinsic and partly transmissive: it carried the working method of a great
mathematician into the reading habits of a generation of physicists and philosophers
who would not otherwise have encountered it.

The philosophical positions are distinctive enough to have a name — *conventionalism* —
although Poincaré never used the word as a self-description and the doctrines associated
with him under that name are more nuanced than the slogan suggests. The principal
theses are: (i) the choice between Euclidean and non-Euclidean geometry is not a
question of empirical fact but of convenience; we adopt Euclidean geometry because it
is the simplest, but no experiment can refute the conjunction of a non-Euclidean
geometry with a suitably modified physics; (ii) the principle of inertia and the
conservation laws are not empirical generalizations but methodological conventions
constitutive of the practice of physics; (iii) mathematical induction is a synthetic
*a priori* faculty and cannot be reduced to logic alone (the position that brought
Poincaré into open dispute with Russell and Couturat over the logicist programme,
particularly in the years 1905–1909); (iv) the role of intuition in mathematics is
primary and the role of formal proof is secondary, in the sense that proof verifies
what intuition has discovered; (v) the unconscious mind plays a generative role in
mathematical invention (the omnibus-at-Coutances passage and the broader essay
"L'invention mathématique" in *Science et méthode* 1908).

The conventionalism thesis on geometry was partly vindicated and partly superseded by
general relativity. Poincaré argued in 1902 that no experiment could force a choice
between Euclidean geometry plus a modified physics and a non-Euclidean geometry plus
ordinary physics. General relativity — the empirical confirmation of which began with
Eddington's 1919 eclipse expedition, four years after Poincaré would have been able to
read Einstein's *Grundlage* — turned out to require a non-Euclidean geometry whose
local curvature is determined empirically by the stress-energy tensor, in a framework
where the geometry-physics distinction Poincaré had relied on does not survive in its
clean form. The conventionalist intuition that the geometry-physics distinction is not
hard remained correct; the specific conventionalist conclusion that we are free to
choose Euclidean geometry by methodological fiat did not.

The philosophy-of-science work is also where Poincaré made the methodological
observations that have most often been recovered in later twentieth-century philosophy:
the role of *underdetermination of theory by data* (taken up by Quine), the
distinction between hypotheses that are merely stipulative and hypotheses that have
empirical content (taken up by the logical positivists), and the recognition that
science is a structured human activity with social and aesthetic constraints as well
as empirical ones. Imre Lakatos's *Proofs and Refutations* (1976) cites Poincaré
explicitly as a major source for the methodological position; Karl Popper's *Logik der
Forschung* (1934) takes the conventionalism thesis as the explicit foil against which
to develop falsificationism.

## 3. Primary works (exhaustive)

The complete works of Poincaré were published as *Œuvres de Henri Poincaré* in eleven
volumes by Gauthier-Villars between 1916 and 1956, edited by a succession of his
colleagues and students. The contents of the eleven volumes:

- **Vol. 1** (1928, ed. Paul Appell, Jacques Hadamard) — Equations of mathematical
  physics; partial differential equations.
- **Vol. 2** (1916, ed. Niels Erik Nørlund, Ernest Lebon) — Functions defined by
  differential equations; algebraic curves.
- **Vol. 3** (1934, ed. Jules Drach) — Equations of mathematical physics, continued.
- **Vol. 4** (1950, ed. Georges Valiron) — Diophantine equations, theory of numbers;
  algebraic groups.
- **Vol. 5** (1950, ed. Albert Châtelet) — Arithmetic; algebra.
- **Vol. 6** (1953, ed. René Garnier, Jean Leray) — Geometry; analysis situs; the
  five supplements bound together.
- **Vol. 7** (1952, ed. Jean Kampé de Fériet) — Mass distributions; potential theory.
- **Vol. 8** (1952, ed. Pierre Sémirot) — Mechanics; celestial mechanics.
- **Vol. 9** (1954, ed. Gérard Petiau) — Mathematical physics; mechanics of fluids;
  electromagnetic theory.
- **Vol. 10** (1954, ed. Gérard Petiau) — Optics; electricity; telegraphy; capillarity.
- **Vol. 11** (1956, ed. Jules Lévy) — Memoirs and reviews; correspondence; popular
  writings; philosophy of science.

The major separate books, in the editions Poincaré himself oversaw or that have become
canonical:

- *Sur les courbes définies par les équations différentielles* (Gauthier-Villars,

- *Leçons de mécanique céleste* (3 vols., Gauthier-Villars, 1905, 1907, 1910).
- *La Science et l'hypothèse* (Flammarion, 1902; English: *Science and Hypothesis*,

## 4. Intellectual lineage

### Teachers and immediate context

- **Charles Hermite** (Polytechnique and Sorbonne) — the doctoral supervisor;
  Poincaré inherited the Hermite tradition of analytic number theory and of classical
  analysis tied to the theory of elliptic and modular functions.
- **Joseph Bertrand** (Polytechnique) — mechanics and probability; Bertrand's *Calcul
  des probabilités* (1889) is the immediate predecessor to Poincaré's 1896 lectures.
- **Gaston Darboux** (Sorbonne) — differential geometry; Darboux sat on the doctoral
  jury and remained a senior colleague throughout Poincaré's Paris career.
- **Lazarus Fuchs** (Berlin, by reading) — the linear differential equations whose
  monodromy groups Poincaré would name after Fuchs.

### Contemporaries with whom the work was conducted in dialogue

- **Felix Klein** (Erlangen, Leipzig, Göttingen) — parallel work on automorphic
  functions; the 1881–1882 correspondence is foundational.
- **Magnus Gösta Mittag-Leffler** (Stockholm) — editor of *Acta Mathematica*; the
  Oscar II prize competition was Mittag-Leffler's project; the entire dynamical-systems
  programme reached print through *Acta*.
- **Hendrik Antoon Lorentz** (Leiden) — the Lorentz transformations are named for
  Lorentz; Poincaré's 1905–1906 papers explicitly acknowledge Lorentz as the source of
  the formalism Poincaré was completing into a group.
- **David Hilbert** (Göttingen) — the two were friendly competitors; Hilbert's 1900
  Paris address was given at the Second International Congress of Mathematicians, where
  Poincaré chaired a major section.
- **Ernst Mach** (Vienna) — Mach's *Mechanik in ihrer Entwicklung* (1883) was a major
  influence on Poincaré's philosophy of science and is acknowledged in *La Science et
  l'hypothèse*.
- **Jacques Hadamard** — the next generation in French analysis; corresponded with
  Poincaré on geodesics on surfaces of negative curvature, the work Hadamard would
  continue after 1898.

### Downstream

- **George David Birkhoff** (Harvard) — the direct American successor; Birkhoff's
  proof of Poincaré's last geometric theorem (1913) was treated as the founding act of
  twentieth-century American dynamical systems.
- **Solomon Lefschetz, J. W. Alexander, Heinz Hopf** — the mathematicians who turned
  *Analysis Situs* into rigorous algebraic topology in the 1920s and 1930s.
- **Andrey Kolmogorov, Vladimir Arnold, Jürgen Moser** — the KAM theorem (1954, 1962,
  1963) on the persistence of quasi-periodic motions in nearly integrable Hamiltonian
  systems; the affirmative answer to the version of the stability question Poincaré
  had been forced to leave negative in 1890.
- **Stephen Smale** — the horseshoe and stable-manifold theory, the modern form of the
  homoclinic-tangle picture from the 1890 memoir.
- **Edward Lorenz, James Yorke, Mitchell Feigenbaum** — the rediscovery of chaos in
  the 1960s and 1970s, recognised within a decade as the Western recovery of
  Poincaré's qualitative dynamics.
- **Jacques Laskar** — long-term integration of the Solar System, the modern descendant
  of the *Méthodes nouvelles*.
- **Grigori Perelman** — the proof of the Poincaré conjecture (arXiv preprints 2002,
  2003).
- **Albert Einstein** — special relativity, with the priority complications discussed
  above; Einstein read the philosophy-of-science books in Bern in 1903–1904 and cited
  Poincaré in several of his later reviews.
- **Hermann Minkowski** — the 1908 Cologne lecture making the four-vector picture
  into spacetime geometry is a direct development of the formalism in the Palermo
  paper of 1906.

## 5. What Poincaré got wrong, contested, or left unresolved

A short list, kept honest:

1. **Special relativity, the conceptual leap.** As discussed above. He had the
   formalism early and in considerable detail; he did not take the empirical
   constancy of *c* in all inertial frames as a postulate from which the kinematics
   could be re-derived; he kept the ether as a real substrate. Einstein's move is
   genuinely Einstein's. Pretending Poincaré made it is bad history; pretending
   Poincaré contributed only marginally is also bad history.
2. **The 1890 memoir error.** The originally submitted prize memoir contained an
   error in the proof of the central stability theorem; the error was caught by
   Phragmén during proofing; Poincaré rewrote at his own expense. The error itself,
   in retrospect, pointed Poincaré at the homoclinic structure that he then described
   in the corrected memoir. The episode is the origin of chaos theory.
3. **Conventionalism in geometry.** The thesis that the choice between geometries is
   methodological rather than empirical was partly vindicated (geometry and physics
   are not separable) and partly superseded (general relativity makes the geometry
   itself an empirical structure determined by the stress-energy tensor).
   Reichenbach's *Philosophie der Raum-Zeit-Lehre* (1928) is the classic statement of
   the post-relativistic position on this question.
4. **Logicism.** Poincaré's rejection of the Frege–Russell programme as
   circular — his argument that mathematical induction cannot be reduced to logic
   without presupposing it — anticipated some of the later difficulties (Gödel
   incompleteness in 1931 vindicated part of the spirit) but was conducted on
   grounds that the logicist tradition itself never accepted as decisive. The dispute
   is unresolved on the terms in which it was conducted.
5. **The relativistic theory of gravitation.** The 1906 Palermo proposal — that
   gravitation propagates at the speed of light and admits a Lorentz-invariant
   formulation — was the right intuition with the wrong details. General relativity
   in 1915 took the question into a different formal apparatus (Riemannian geometry of
   spacetime). Poincaré's specific equations are not used.

## 6. Archives and primary-source repositories

- **Archives Henri Poincaré – Philosophie et Recherches sur les Sciences et les
  Technologies** (UMR 7117 CNRS, Université de Lorraine, Nancy). The institutional
  centre for Poincaré studies; holds the family papers, the correspondence (about 2000
  letters in or out, a substantial fraction unpublished), the manuscripts of the
  popular books, and the editorial files for the *Œuvres*. Online edition of the
  correspondence at henripoincarepapers.univ-lorraine.fr.
- **Académie des sciences, Paris.** Poincaré's dossier as a member; the manuscripts of
  his presentations to the Academy.
- **Institut Henri Poincaré, Paris** (founded 1928). Not a primary archive but the
  institutional descendant; holds documentation on the reception and continuation of
  the work.
- **Mittag-Leffler Institute, Djursholm** (Sweden). The Poincaré–Mittag-Leffler
  correspondence of the 1880s and 1890s, including the *Acta Mathematica* editorial
  files for the prize memoir affair.
- **Niedersächsische Staats- und Universitätsbibliothek, Göttingen.** The
  Klein–Poincaré correspondence on Fuchsian and automorphic functions.
- **Eidgenössische Technische Hochschule, Zürich.** Letters from Poincaré in the
  Hurwitz and Minkowski papers.

## 7. Bibliography (secondary)

The literature is enormous; the following are the items the field treats as canonical
or as load-bearing for a particular question.

- Bell, Eric Temple. *Men of Mathematics*. New York: Simon & Schuster, 1937. The
  chapter on Poincaré is dated and mythologising in the way the whole book is, but it
  is the canonical popular assessment of the period and is still the source most
  general readers have first encountered.
- Hadamard, Jacques. *L'œuvre mathématique de Poincaré*. *Acta Mathematica* 38
  (1921), pp. 203–287. The first major scientific obituary, by Poincaré's most
  distinguished French successor.
- Pais, Abraham. *Subtle Is the Lord: The Science and the Life of Albert Einstein*.
  Oxford University Press, 1982. Chapters 6–7 are the standard physicist's account of
  the Poincaré–Lorentz–Einstein priority question.
- Barrow-Green, June. *Poincaré and the Three-Body Problem*. American Mathematical
  Society and London Mathematical Society, 1997 (History of Mathematics, vol. 11).
  The canonical study of the prize memoir affair, the error, and the rewriting.
- Galison, Peter. *Einstein's Clocks, Poincaré's Maps: Empires of Time*. Norton, 2003.
  The book that placed the technical-administrative work at the Bureau des Longitudes
  on synchronized telegraphic time at the centre of the relativity story.
- Darrigol, Olivier. "The Mystery of the Einstein–Poincaré Connection." *Isis* 95,
  no. 4 (2004), pp. 614–626. The most careful single article on the priority
  question.
- Verhulst, Ferdinand. *Henri Poincaré: Impatient Genius*. Springer, 2012. A short
  scientific biography on the centenary of the death; useful as a single-volume
  technical introduction.
- Gray, Jeremy. *Henri Poincaré: A Scientific Biography*. Princeton University Press,
  2013. The canonical modern scholarly biography; the book to read first if you read
  only one. Comprehensive on the mathematics, careful on the philosophy, balanced on
  the priority disputes.
- Nabonnand, Philippe (ed.). *La correspondance entre Henri Poincaré et Gösta
  Mittag-Leffler*. Birkhäuser, 1999.
- Walter, Scott. *Henri Poincaré: A Biography Through the Daily Papers*. World
  Scientific, 2018. The reception side: how French and international newspapers
  covered Poincaré in life.
- The journal *Philosophia Scientiæ* (Nancy, 1996– ), special issues on Poincaré:
  the running specialist literature.

## 9. Polymath qualifier

Polymath, four-branch (01-mathematics primary; 02-physics, 06-cosmology, 07-mind
cross). The cross-branch contributions are not biographical breadth but work the
relevant fields actually cite: special-relativity formalism in physics, the
qualitative theory of the *n*-body problem in cosmology, the conventionalist
philosophy-of-science trilogy in the philosophy of mind and science. The case is the
cleanest nineteenth-century example after Helmholtz of a single integrated working
practice — analytic mathematics applied wherever it could be applied — producing
foundation-tier output across more than one canon branch. He sits in the polymath
register alongside Helmholtz, Gauss, Riemann, Newton, Einstein, von Neumann, and
Aristotle.

The qualifier is conservative. Two further branches could be argued (04-information,
on the basis of his probability work and its connection to the early information-
theoretic treatment of measurement; 03-chemistry by way of the kinetic-theory and
statistical-mechanics readings of the recurrence theorem) but in neither case is the
contribution foundation-tier in the way the four listed branches are. The four-branch
register is the one the canon team should treat as load-bearing.
