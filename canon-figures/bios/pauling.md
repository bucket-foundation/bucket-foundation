## 1. Why Pauling is in the canon: and in two branches at once

Pauling is the chemist of the twentieth century, in the strict sense the
contributor index requires: the figure whose work, removed from the canon,
leaves a hole that no other single figure fills. He sits in two primary
canon branches because he produced foundation-tier work in two distinct
domains that are not reducible to one another.

Two primary branches:

- `03-chemistry`, for the integration of quantum mechanics with
  empirical structural chemistry. The 1929 five rules for the structure
  of complex ionic crystals (`crystallography/`), the 1931 *JACS*
  six-paper series on the chemical bond (`bonding/`), and the 1939 / 1940 /
  1960 monograph *The Nature of the Chemical Bond*, the third edition
  of which is the canon edition-of-record per the 2026 chemistry pass-3
  synthesis. *The Nature of the Chemical Bond* is the most-cited
  scientific book of the twentieth century by a chemist; it is also,
  with the *Principia*, *On the Origin of Species*, and the
  *Philosophiæ Naturalis*, on any short list of the books that
  reorganised a science.
- `05-biophysics`, for the Pauling, Corey, Branson 1951 alpha-helix and
  beta-sheet papers (`structural-biology/`), which are the founding
  prediction of regular secondary structure in proteins, and for the
  1949 sickle-cell paper, which is the founding statement of the
  concept of *molecular disease*.

One cross-branch, with limits:

- `02-physics`, for the introduction to physical chemistry of the
  Heitler, London valence-bond formalism in a usable form, for the
  hybridisation theory of directed valence (sp, sp², sp³), and for the
  1935 textbook *Introduction to Quantum Mechanics* with E. Bright
  Wilson Jr., which trained two generations of American theoretical
  chemists in real quantum mechanics. Listed cross- rather than primary
  because the formal apparatus is post-Heitler-London and Pauling's
  contribution is the application and the chemistry, ahead of the physics
  proper.

Politics and peace work are listed as a non-canon-counting cross under
`07-mind` for context only. The 1962 Nobel Peace Prize (awarded 1963 for
1962) is the public record of the anti-nuclear-testing campaign. The
canon thesis explicitly does not count downstream-application or
political work as foundation-tier; it is recorded because the figure
cannot be described without it.

The polymath claim is auditable. Remove Pauling from `03-chemistry` and
the bonding sub-branch loses its single integrator-of-record between
quantum mechanics and structural chemistry; the crystallography
sub-branch loses the five rules that organise the field's first century
of structure determinations. Remove Pauling from `05-biophysics` and
the structural-biology sub-branch loses the alpha-helix prediction,
arrived at nine months before the Watson, Crick DNA paper, by an
identical method (model-building constrained by bond-length and
bond-angle data, plus interpretive reading of fibre X-ray diffraction
patterns), and confirmed by Perutz's 1951 X-ray work on horse
haemoglobin within months of publication. Two-branch foundation-tier
polymath; the qualifier line in `_polymaths.md` reflects this.

## 2. Life timeline

**1901, Portland, Oregon.** Born 28 February in Portland to Herman
Henry William Pauling, a pharmacist of German extraction, and Lucy
Isabelle ("Belle") Darling. Father died in 1910 of a perforated ulcer
when Linus was nine, leaving Belle to raise three children on a small
income. The family circumstances were tight throughout Pauling's
childhood and adolescence in Condon and then Portland, Oregon.

**1914, chemistry set.** A schoolfriend, Lloyd Jeffress, kept a small
chemistry laboratory in his bedroom and let Pauling work in it. The
encounter is the standard biographical hinge; Pauling himself dated his
decision to become a chemist to that bedroom laboratory. By 1916 he had
salvaged equipment and reagents from a closing Oregon Iron and Steel
Company plant and was running his own home laboratory.

**1917-1922, Oregon Agricultural College (now Oregon State
University), Corvallis.** Entered OAC at sixteen as a chemical
engineering student. The family finances were so tight that Pauling
took a year out (1919-1920) to work as a paving-plant inspector to
support his mother and sisters. Returned and finished the BS in
chemical engineering in 1922. While still an undergraduate, in his
senior year, taught quantitative analysis to the sophomore class,
which is where he met his future wife, Ava Helen Miller, whom he was
formally instructing.

**1922-1925, Caltech, PhD.** Entered the California Institute of
Technology as a graduate student in chemistry in autumn 1922. Worked
under Roscoe Gilkey Dickinson, who had taken his own PhD in 1920 (the
first Caltech PhD ever awarded) and was running an X-ray
crystallography group. The dissertation work was on the X-ray
determination of crystal structures, Pauling's first published paper
(*JACS* 45, 1923, 1466-1471, with Dickinson, on molybdenite) appeared
within his first year. PhD awarded summa cum laude in 1925 with a
dissertation, *The Determination with X-rays of the Structures of
Crystals*, that already contained the mature crystallographic method
that would become the 1929 five-rules paper.

**1922, marriage.** Married Ava Helen Miller on 17 June 1923 (between
his first and second years of graduate study). Four children: Linus Jr.
(b. 1925, physician), Peter (b. 1931, crystallographer), Linda (b.
1932), and Crellin (b. 1937, chemist). Ava Helen was Pauling's
political conscience for the rest of her life; the radicalisation of the
1940s and 1950s ran through her at least as much as through him.

**1926-1927, Guggenheim, Europe.** John Simon Guggenheim Memorial
Foundation Fellowship, the formative trip. Pauling went to Munich and
spent the academic year with Arnold Sommerfeld at the Theoretische
Physik institute, then moved on to Copenhagen for a shorter stay with
Niels Bohr at Bohr's institute, and finished the European year with
Erwin Schrödinger in Zürich. The exposure to the founders of quantum
mechanics in their own institutes, in 1926-27, the year after
Heisenberg's matrix mechanics (1925), the year of Schrödinger's wave
mechanics (1926), the year before Heitler and London's quantum-mechanical
treatment of the hydrogen molecule (1927), is the formative scientific
event of Pauling's career. Every subsequent claim he made about chemical
bonding can be traced back to the methods he learned in those three
institutes. He never lost the conviction, picked up from Sommerfeld,
that quantum mechanics was a tool a chemist should use directly rather
than wait for a physicist to use on the chemist's behalf.

**1927-1963, Caltech.** Returned to Caltech as assistant professor of
theoretical chemistry in 1927; promoted to associate professor in 1929,
full professor in 1931 at age thirty, chairman of the Division of
Chemistry and Chemical Engineering and director of the Gates and
Crellin Laboratories of Chemistry from 1936. The Caltech years are the
great years. Everything in §3 below was done at Caltech.

**1931, *JACS* bond series.** Six papers in *JACS* volume 53, the
foundation of valence-bond theory as applied to chemistry. The first,
"The Nature of the Chemical Bond. Application of Results Obtained from
the Quantum Mechanics and from a Theory of Paramagnetic Susceptibility
to the Structure of Molecules" (*JACS* 53, 1931, 1367-1400), is the
single most important paper in twentieth-century chemistry by a wide
margin in citation count.

**1933, election to NAS.** Elected to the National Academy of Sciences
at thirty-two, then the youngest member.

**1939, *Nature of the Chemical Bond* monograph.** First edition,
Cornell University Press, written for the George Fisher Baker
Lectureship in Chemistry at Cornell (lectured 1937-38). Second edition
1940, third edition 1960. The third edition is the canon
edition-of-record per the 2026 chemistry pass-3 synthesis (ISBN
0-8014-0333-2).

**1941, Bright's disease.** Diagnosed with chronic glomerulonephritis,
expected at the time to be fatal within years. Treated by Thomas Addis
at Stanford with a low-protein, low-salt regimen plus minerals; the
condition was managed for the rest of his life. The Addis
treatment regimen is the historical seed of Pauling's lifelong
preoccupation with the role of nutrition and trace nutrients in
chronic disease.

**1942-1945, wartime work.** Pauling declined J. Robert Oppenheimer's
direct invitation to head the chemistry division of the Manhattan
Project (the offer was made in 1942 or 1943; the standard biographies
disagree on the exact date). The reason given at the time was family
obligation; the long-form reason, given by Pauling in interviews after
the war, was conscience, he did not want to participate in building
the bomb. He nonetheless worked on a number of war-related projects:
explosives, rocket propellants, an oxygen meter for submarines and
aircraft (the Pauling oxygen meter is the device, manufactured by Beckman
Instruments, that became the standard military-aircraft oxygen monitor),
and consultative work on uranium hexafluoride enrichment chemistry. He
received the Presidential Medal for Merit from Truman in 1948 for the
oxygen meter and explosives work.

**1946, anti-nuclear campaign begins.** With Albert Einstein and seven
others, joined the Emergency Committee of Atomic Scientists, chaired
by Einstein, formed to alert the public to the dangers of nuclear
weapons. The 1946 inflection is biographically unambiguous: Hiroshima
and Nagasaki, Ava Helen's insistence, and the formation of the ECAS
turned a politically engaged liberal scientist into a full-time
anti-nuclear activist for the next two decades.

**1948, Nature of the Chemical Bond second-edition reprint, knighthood
declined.** Awarded the Order of the British Empire (OBE) by King
George VI for the wartime work; declined the honour. Awarded the
Presidential Medal for Merit by Truman the same year; accepted.

**1949, sickle-cell paper.** Pauling, Itano, Singer, and Wells,
"Sickle cell anemia, a molecular disease" (*Science* 110, 543-548).
The paper introduces the term *molecular disease* and shows by
electrophoretic mobility that the haemoglobin of sickle-cell patients
differs from normal adult haemoglobin in net charge, a difference
later (Ingram, 1956) localised to a single amino-acid substitution
(Glu→Val at position 6 of the beta chain). The paper is the founding
statement of molecular medicine.

**1951, alpha-helix.** Pauling, Corey, and Branson, "The structure of
proteins: two hydrogen-bonded helical configurations of the polypeptide
chain" (*PNAS* 37, 205-211, April 1951), and the surrounding
seven-paper *PNAS* 37 bundle (April, May 1951), which together describe
the alpha-helix, the beta-sheet (then called "pleated sheet"), and the
collagen-like polyglycine and polyproline conformations. The
predictions were confirmed within months by Max Perutz's X-ray work on
horse haemoglobin (the 1.5 Å meridional reflection diagnostic of the
alpha-helix). The alpha-helix is the most consequential prediction
ever made by a chemist about biology.

**1952-1954, passport revoked.** The State Department revoked Pauling's
passport in 1952 on grounds that his anti-nuclear activism was
"not in the best interests of the United States". The revocation
prevented him from attending a Royal Society conference on protein
structure in London in May 1952, at which Maurice Wilkins's group
showed the high-quality DNA fibre X-ray patterns that became Photo 51.
Pauling has been assumed by many writers (Watson explicitly, in *The
Double Helix*) to have lost the DNA race in part because of the
passport revocation; the counterfactual is unprovable but the timing
is suggestive.

**1953, triple-helix DNA.** Pauling and Corey, "A proposed structure
for the nucleic acids" (*PNAS* 39, 84-97, 28 February 1953). Three
intertwined polynucleotide chains with the phosphate groups on the
inside, bases pointing outward. The model was wrong, and wrong in a
way a competent acid, base chemist should not have proposed: at
physiological pH the phosphate groups are deprotonated and would
electrostatically repel one another in the proposed close-packed
inside arrangement; the model also failed to account for the basicity
of the bases and the requirement for hydrogen-bond donors and
acceptors. Watson and Crick's correct double-helix model appeared in
*Nature* 171 (25 April 1953, 737-738). Pauling acknowledged the
double-helix model immediately and publicly upon seeing it on a visit
to Cambridge in April 1953.

**1954, Nobel Prize in Chemistry.** Awarded "for his research into
the nature of the chemical bond and its application to the elucidation
of the structure of complex substances". Single-laureate award. The
citation pointedly covers the bond work, not yet the protein work.

**1957-1958, bomb-test petition.** Drafted the petition "An Appeal by
American Scientists to the Governments and People of the World"
calling for an immediate halt to atmospheric nuclear weapons testing.
Eventually signed by 9,235 scientists from 49 countries; presented to
the United Nations on 13 January 1958 and to Dag Hammarskjöld
personally. *No More War!* (Dodd, Mead, 1958) is the book-length
expansion of the petition's argument.

**1960, *Nature of the Chemical Bond* third edition.** The canon
edition. The third edition incorporates twenty years of subsequent
structural chemistry, the alpha-helix and beta-sheet, the molecular-orbital
calculations of the 1950s, and the mature electronegativity scale.

**1960, Senate Internal Security Subcommittee.** Subpoenaed by the
SISS; refused to give the names of the people who had circulated the
1957 petition. Held in contempt of the subcommittee but not prosecuted;
the contempt finding was eventually dropped under public pressure.

**1962, Nobel Peace Prize.** Awarded the 1962 Nobel Peace Prize on
10 October 1963, the announcement was made on the same day the
Limited Nuclear Test Ban Treaty entered into force (10 October 1963;
the treaty was signed 5 August 1963). The Norwegian Nobel Committee's
citation explicitly named the campaign against atmospheric nuclear
testing. Pauling is the only person ever to have received two
unshared Nobel Prizes. Marie Curie received two but one was shared;
John Bardeen, Frederick Sanger, and Karl Barry Sharpless received two
but in the same field.

**1963, Caltech departure.** Resigned from Caltech in late 1963. The
proximate cause was a sustained institutional cooling toward Pauling's
politics, the Caltech administration had been uncomfortable with the
peace activism for over a decade and had quietly downgraded his
chairmanship and his lab support, and the absence of any formal
recognition by Caltech of the Peace Prize. The deeper cause was that
Pauling's research interests had moved from structural chemistry,
where Caltech was the world centre, toward orthomolecular medicine,
where Caltech was uninterested.

**1963-1967, Center for the Study of Democratic Institutions, Santa
Barbara.** Joined the Robert Maynard Hutchins think tank as
research professor. The CSDI years were primarily political and
policy-oriented; he wrote on disarmament, nuclear policy, and the
structure of international peace.

**1967-1969, UC San Diego.** Professor of chemistry at UCSD. The
short tenure produced no major new structural-chemistry papers; the
period is the inflection where Pauling moved from peace activism to
his second major political-scientific commitment, vitamin C
megadosage and orthomolecular medicine.

**1969-1974, Stanford.** Professor of chemistry at Stanford. Continued
the orthomolecular work; published *Vitamin C and the Common Cold*
(Freeman, 1970), which became a bestseller and which is the inflection
point at which mainstream medicine began to disengage from Pauling's
post-1970 work.

**1973, Linus Pauling Institute of Science and Medicine, Menlo Park
(later moved to Palo Alto, then to Oregon State University at
Corvallis in 1996).** Pauling co-founded the Institute as the
institutional home for the orthomolecular research programme.
Funding was a continuous problem; the institute survived but
contracted across the 1970s and 1980s. After Pauling's death in 1994
it moved to Oregon State University and continues there as the
Linus Pauling Institute of OSU, focused on micronutrient research.

**1974, retired from Stanford.** Continued research at the Pauling
Institute.

**1981, Ava Helen dies.** Stomach cancer. Pauling's writing in the
1980s returns to her; the partnership had been continuous
for fifty-eight years.

**1985, Moertel et al. trial.** A randomised double-blind clinical
trial in advanced cancer patients (*New England Journal of Medicine*
312, 137-141, 17 January 1985) found no benefit from high-dose
ascorbic acid in advanced colorectal cancer. The trial was the
second of two Mayo Clinic randomised trials (the first, 1979)
designed to test Pauling's vitamin-C claims; both were
negative. Pauling responded by criticising the trial design, the
Mayo Clinic protocol used oral ascorbate where Pauling and Cameron
had used a sequential intravenous-then-oral protocol, but the
mainstream-medical verdict on the megadose claim has not been
revisited since.

**1992, prostate cancer.** Diagnosed.

**1994, death.** Died at his ranch at Big Sur, California, on 19
August 1994 of prostate cancer, aged 93.

## 3. Primary works in detail

### "The Principles Determining the Structure of Complex Ionic Crystals"

The five Pauling rules. A short paper that organises a decade's worth of
X-ray crystallographic structure determinations into five geometric and
electrostatic principles for predicting the structures of ionic crystals.
The rules concern (1) the radius-ratio principle for the coordination
polyhedron around a cation; (2) the electrostatic-valence principle (the
sum of the electrostatic bond strengths from a cation to a coordinating
anion equals the anion's charge); (3) the rule that shared edges and
shared faces between coordination polyhedra destabilise a
structure; (4) a corollary about high-charge cations of low coordination
number; and (5) the rule of parsimony, that the number of
different kinds of coordination polyhedron in a crystal tends to be
small.

The five rules organise the crystallography of silicates, oxides, and
ionic compounds; they remain in active teaching use and in
active predictive use a century later, refined but not displaced. Canon
entry: `03-chemistry/crystallography/`.

### The "Nature of the Chemical Bond" *JACS* series I, VI

Six papers in *JACS* volume 53 across 1931, the foundation of valence-bond
theory as a working chemical theory. The first paper alone (*JACS* 53,
1931, 1367-1400) is one of the most-cited single papers in twentieth-century
chemistry. Topics across the series include: the application of
quantum-mechanical results (the Heitler, London 1927 H₂ calculation, the
Slater 1931 directed-valence treatment) to the chemical bond; the theory
of hybridisation (sp, sp², sp³, dsp², d²sp³) as the explanation of
directed valence and the geometry of molecules; the introduction of the
electronegativity concept in a quantitative form; resonance theory as the
description of molecules whose structure cannot be represented by a
single Lewis-style formula; the theory of the one-electron and three-electron
bonds; and the application of the framework to specific structural
problems (benzene, the carbonate ion, complex ions of the transition
metals).

The series is the moment at which quantum mechanics became chemistry's
own tool rather than a physicist's external resource. Canon entry:
`03-chemistry/bonding/`.

### *The Nature of the Chemical Bond and the Structure of Molecules and Crystals: An Introduction to Modern Structural Chemistry*

The single most influential chemistry book of the twentieth century.
Originated in the George Fisher Baker Lectures Pauling gave at Cornell
in 1937-38; first edition 1939, second 1940, third 1960. The third edition
(ISBN 0-8014-0333-2) is the canon edition-of-record per the chemistry
pass-3 synthesis: it is the version that contains the mature
electronegativity scale, the integration of the alpha-helix and the
beta-sheet, the post-1940s structural data, and the most pedagogically
refined treatment of resonance and hybridisation. The book is on every
short list of the most influential scientific books of the twentieth
century.

### *Introduction to Quantum Mechanics: with Applications to Chemistry*

The first English-language textbook of quantum mechanics written by a
chemist for chemists. The book stayed in print continuously for more
than half a century (Dover reissue 1985, still in print). It trained
two generations of American theoretical chemists in real quantum
mechanics, Schrödinger equation, perturbation theory, variational
methods, at a level that allowed them to use the formalism rather than
defer to physicists. Co-author E. Bright Wilson Jr. went on to a long
career at Harvard and was the doctoral advisor of Roald Hoffmann
(Nobel 1981) and Dudley Herschbach (Nobel 1986).

### The Pauling-Corey-Branson Protein-Structure Papers

Seven papers in *PNAS* 37 across April and May 1951, by Pauling and
Robert Corey with the third-author contribution of H. R. Branson on
the lead alpha-helix paper. The bundle proposes the alpha-helix and the
pleated sheet (now beta-sheet) as the regular secondary structures of
proteins, and the polyglycine II and polyproline II helical
conformations. The argument is model-building under chemical
constraints: planar peptide bond (an inference from prior crystal
structures of small peptides done by Corey at Caltech), characteristic
bond lengths and angles, hydrogen-bond geometry from N-H to O=C with
N···O distance ≈ 2.8 Å. The alpha-helix turns out to have a non-integer
number of residues per turn (3.6), a fact that was the principal
psychological obstacle to its earlier proposal (W. T. Astbury and others
had been looking for integer-residue helices) and that Pauling and Corey
embraced because the chemistry, ahead of the convenience of the
crystallographer, decides the geometry.

The 1.5 Å meridional reflection in the X-ray fibre patterns of
alpha-keratin, predicted by the Pauling, Corey model as the rise per
residue projected on the fibre axis, was confirmed by Max Perutz at
Cambridge within months of publication; the report (Perutz, *Nature*
167, 1951, 1053-1054) is the earliest experimental confirmation. Canon
entry: `05-biophysics/structural-biology/`, with cross-link from
`03-chemistry/bonding/` because the prediction is a direct application
of the bond-length, bond-angle, and hydrogen-bond apparatus of *Nature
of the Chemical Bond*.

### The Pauling-Itano-Singer-Wells Sickle Cell Paper

The founding statement of molecular medicine. Electrophoretic mobility
of haemoglobin from sickle-cell patients differs from that of
haemoglobin from healthy controls; the difference is consistent with a
small change in net charge of the haemoglobin molecule itself. The paper
introduces the phrase "molecular disease" for a hereditary condition
whose proximate cause is a change in the molecular structure of a
specific protein. Vernon Ingram (Cambridge, 1956) localised the
difference to a single amino-acid substitution at the sixth residue of
the beta chain (Glu→Val), establishing the modern understanding of
sickle-cell anaemia and, with it, the modern understanding of how a
single base change in DNA can produce a clinical phenotype.

### Pauling & Corey: "A proposed structure for the nucleic acids"

The triple-helix DNA model. Three intertwined polynucleotide chains
with the phosphate groups on the inside, bases pointing outward. The
paper was wrong in detail and wrong in chemistry: at physiological pH
the phosphate groups carry negative charges that would repel one
another at the close-packed interior distances Pauling proposed, and
the model offered no mechanism for the specific base-pairing required by
Chargaff's rules (which Pauling did not in fact know in any detail at
the time of writing). The Watson, Crick double-helix model (*Nature*
171, 1953, 737-738) appeared eight weeks later and was correct.
Pauling visited Cambridge in April 1953 and conceded the double-helix
model on the spot. The episode is the most-discussed scientific failure
of his career and is treated in §5.

### *No More War!*

The book-length statement of Pauling's anti-nuclear-testing position.
Combines a popular-scientific account of fallout, ionising radiation,
and the genetic and somatic effects of low-dose radiation with a
political argument for an immediate halt to atmospheric weapons
testing and for general nuclear disarmament. The book is the printed
record of the position the Norwegian Nobel Committee cited in awarding
the 1962 Peace Prize.

### *Vitamin C and the Common Cold*

The book that launched the vitamin-C megadose campaign. Argued that
gram-scale daily ascorbic acid intake reduced the incidence and
severity of the common cold and, by implication, of other respiratory
infections. The book was a bestseller and a popular phenomenon. The
clinical-trial record on the specific common-cold claim is mixed and
modest: the standard meta-analytic finding (Hemilä and Chalker, Cochrane
Database, multiple revisions through the 2010s and 2020s) is that
prophylactic vitamin C produces a small reduction in cold duration in
the general population, no reduction in cold incidence, and a more
substantial reduction in incidence in subjects under acute physical
stress (marathon runners, soldiers in extreme environments). The
broader Pauling claim, that megadose vitamin C is beneficial
across a wide range of conditions including cancer and cardiovascular
disease, is not supported by the trial record. The 1985 Moertel
*NEJM* trial (see §5) is the most-cited refutation of the cancer
claim.

### *General Chemistry*

A pedagogical landmark. Reorganised undergraduate general chemistry
around the structural and bond-theoretic framework of *Nature of the
Chemical Bond*, in contrast to the earlier descriptive-and-equilibrium
organisation. Trained two generations of American chemistry
undergraduates. Listed here at landscape tier: pedagogically
indispensable, but the foundation-tier work is the 1931 *JACS* series
and the 1939-60 monograph, of which *General Chemistry* is the
undergraduate version.

## 4. Foundation contributions: branch by branch

### 03-chemistry

**`bonding/`, quantum-mechanically grounded structural chemistry.**
The 1931 *JACS* series and the 1939 / 1940 / 1960 *Nature of the
Chemical Bond* monograph. The integration of Heitler, London valence-bond
theory with the empirical structural-chemistry tradition. The
hybridisation theory of directed valence (sp, sp², sp³, dsp², d²sp³)
that explains the geometry of methane, ethylene, acetylene, water,
ammonia, the boron hydrides, and the transition-metal complexes. The
quantitative electronegativity scale (the Pauling scale, χ in units
where the difference between two atoms' electronegativities relates to
the ionic character of the bond between them via χ_A − χ_B =
(eV)^(1/2) · √(D_AB − ½(D_AA + D_BB)), the thermochemical
electronegativity definition). Resonance theory as the description of
molecules whose true wavefunction is a linear combination of several
canonical structures (benzene, the carbonate ion, the carboxylate
anion). The third edition of the monograph (1960) is the canon
edition-of-record.

**`crystallography/`, Pauling's five rules.** The 1929 *JACS* paper.
Five geometric and electrostatic principles that organise the
structures of ionic crystals: radius-ratio coordination, electrostatic
valence, polyhedral edge- and face-sharing penalties, the high-charge
low-coordination corollary, and the rule of parsimony. Canon-tier;
still in active teaching and active predictive use.

### 05-biophysics

**`structural-biology/`, the alpha-helix and the beta-sheet.** The
1951 *PNAS* 37 bundle. The first correct prediction of regular
secondary structure in proteins. The method, model-building
constrained by chemically defensible bond lengths and angles, plus
hydrogen-bond geometry, became the founding methodology of structural
biology and was directly the methodology Watson and Crick used for the
DNA double helix two years later. (Watson, in *The Double Helix*,
acknowledges the debt explicitly.)

**`structural-biology/`, the molecular-disease concept.** The 1949
*Science* paper on sickle-cell haemoglobin. The first identification
of a genetic disease as a change in a specific protein molecule;
the founding paper of molecular medicine.

### 02-physics

**`quantum-chemistry/`, the textbook.** The 1935 Pauling, Wilson
*Introduction to Quantum Mechanics*. Listed cross- rather than primary
because the underlying quantum mechanics is post-Heitler-London and
post-Schrödinger, and Pauling's contribution to the physics is not
foundation-tier in physics; the textbook is, however, the channel through
which English-speaking chemistry absorbed the formalism, and on that
ground the cross-listing is fair.

### 07-mind

**The 1950s-60s anti-nuclear-testing campaign and the 1962 Peace
Prize.** Recorded for completeness. The canon thesis explicitly
excludes downstream-application and political work from the
foundation-tier count, but the figure cannot be described
without it. *No More War!* (1958), the 1957-58 scientists' petition
(9,235 signatories, 49 countries), and the Nobel Peace Prize (1962,
announced 10 October 1963 the same day the Limited Test Ban Treaty
entered into force) are the visible record.

## 5. What Pauling got wrong

Sections on what the figure got wrong are required by the
editorial conventions in `bios/README.md`. Pauling has more in this
column than any other twentieth-century chemist of his stature, partly
because he made more high-confidence claims than any other twentieth-
century chemist of his stature, and partly because he refused to
disengage from a claim once he had made it.

**The triple-helix DNA model (1953).** The single most-discussed
failure. The model proposed three intertwined polynucleotide chains
with the phosphate groups on the inside and the bases pointing
outward. The errors are well-catalogued (see e.g. Hager 1995, ch. 27;
Olby, *The Path to the Double Helix*, 1974). The phosphate-inside
arrangement is electrostatically untenable at physiological pH because
the phosphates carry negative charges that would repel one another at
the close-packed inside distances. The model offered no mechanism for
specific base-pairing and, critically, was published before Pauling
had absorbed the implications of Chargaff's 1950 rules. Pauling did not
have the high-quality DNA fibre X-ray data that the Wilkins, Franklin
group at King's College London possessed (the 1952 passport revocation
contributed to this; whether the passport had been honoured the outcome
might or might not have been different). Watson and Crick beat him in
*Nature* in April 1953, eight weeks after the *PNAS* publication.
Pauling visited Cambridge in April 1953, examined the Watson, Crick
model, and accepted it on the spot, a piece of intellectual honesty
that should be recorded alongside the failure.

**Vitamin C megadose claims, 1970-onward, Bucket's editorial line.**
The vitamin-C megadose programme is the second great Pauling failure
and the one that did the most damage to his late-career reputation.
The claim has several layers, which need to be separated:

1. *The common-cold claim.* Modest reduction in cold duration with
   prophylactic vitamin C in the general population; no reduction in
   incidence; larger reduction in incidence in populations under
   extreme physical stress (Hemilä and Chalker, Cochrane reviews,
   multiple revisions). The Pauling claim, that gram-scale daily doses
   produce dramatic reductions in incidence, is **not supported**.
2. *The cancer claim* (Cameron and Pauling, *PNAS* 73, 1976, 3685-3689,
   and *PNAS* 75, 1978, 4538-4542, observational comparisons of
   advanced-cancer patients given high-dose ascorbate against historical
   controls at the same hospital). The Mayo Clinic randomised
   double-blind trials (Creagan et al. *NEJM* 301, 1979, 687-690; and
   Moertel et al. *NEJM* 312, 1985, 137-141) found **no benefit**.
   Pauling's response was to challenge the protocol on the grounds that
   the Mayo trials used oral ascorbate while the Cameron, Pauling
   protocol had been sequential intravenous-then-oral; this objection
   is partially respectable in the narrow sense that intravenous
   ascorbate produces much higher plasma concentrations than oral and
   the pharmacokinetics differ in ways that matter for any plausible
   mechanism (subsequent work, Levine et al., NIH, 2000s, confirmed
   the pharmacokinetic difference). The narrow objection does not,
   however, restore the broad clinical claim. Subsequent randomised
   trials of intravenous ascorbate in advanced cancer (the Hoffer
   trials, the Riordan Clinic data, the Padayatty/Levine pilots through
   the 2000s and 2010s) have produced **no convincing positive
   randomised result for the survival or response endpoint** in
   conventional advanced-cancer settings. Modest effects on quality-of-
   life endpoints have been reported but are not sufficient to vindicate
   the original Pauling claim.
3. *The "orthomolecular" framework*, the idea that optimal
   health is achieved by adjusting the concentrations of substances
   normally present in the body (vitamins, minerals, amino acids) into
   ranges higher than the conventional RDAs. The framework is
   biologically plausible in the abstract, the RDA is a
   floor-level requirement short of an optimum, and the floor-vs-optimum
   distinction is real, but the specific Pauling implementations have
   not survived randomised testing for any of the major claimed
   benefits (cancer prevention, cardiovascular protection, cognitive
   protection, mood and psychiatric conditions). Orthomolecular
   psychiatry in particular (the Hoffer, Osmond niacin treatment of
   schizophrenia, which Pauling endorsed) has been comprehensively
   refuted by randomised trials.

**Bucket editorial line on vitamin C megadose:**
The post-1970 vitamin-C megadose programme sits at *landscape tier*.
It is a refuted research programme that occupies the last quarter of
Pauling's career and that does not retract or qualify the foundation-
tier work of 1929-1960. The canon files Pauling on the basis of the
chemistry and the alpha-helix; the vitamin-C work is recorded in this
bio for completeness and in `_archive/` of the biophysics canon if at
all. There is no Bucket position that the megadose claims are
"controversial" or "underexplored" or "deserving further study"; the
trial record is sufficient, and Bucket does not host crank rehabilitation.
The summary is: a great chemist, near the end of his life, was
wrong about a clinical claim, defended it past the point at which the
defence was scientifically respectable, and the late-life error does
not retract the early-life canon. This is a normal pattern; the
strongest editorial discipline is to refuse to extend the great work's
authority to cover the late-life error.

**Orthomolecular psychiatry.** The Hoffer, Osmond niacin treatment of
schizophrenia, endorsed by Pauling in the 1968 *Science* paper
"Orthomolecular psychiatry" (*Science* 160, 265-271), has been
refuted by randomised controlled trials. Mainstream psychiatry
disengaged from the framework in the 1970s. The American Psychiatric
Association issued a formal position statement against orthomolecular
psychiatry in 1973 (the Lipton report). Pauling rejected the rejection.

**Resonance theory, the over-extension.** Pauling's resonance theory
is correct as a description of molecular wavefunctions whose true
form is a linear combination of canonical structures (benzene, the
carboxylate anion, the carbonate ion). The molecular-orbital
alternative (Hund, Mulliken, Hückel) provides an equally good
description in many cases and a better description in some, UV-visible
spectroscopy, photoelectron spectroscopy, and the theory of conjugated
systems. The 1940s-50s resonance-vs-MO debates were partly a
question of pedagogy, partly a question of computational tractability,
and partly a question of personal style. The modern post-1970s
synthesis is that valence-bond and molecular-orbital methods are
mathematically equivalent in the limit of complete basis sets and
configuration interaction, and that each has computational and
intuitive advantages for different problems. Pauling's resistance to
giving the molecular-orbital approach equal pedagogical billing in the
later editions of *Nature of the Chemical Bond* is the legitimate
criticism.

**The Soviet "denunciation of resonance" affair (1949-51).** A
parenthetical historical note. In 1949-51 a group of Soviet chemists,
led by V. M. Tatevskii and M. I. Shakhparonov and backed by the
Lysenko-era ideological apparatus, attacked resonance theory as
"idealist" and "Machian" and called for its expulsion from Soviet
chemistry. The episode is one of the embarrassments of Stalinist
science policy. Pauling's response, a 1951 *Journal of Chemical
Education* note and subsequent correspondence, was firm and
appropriate. The Soviet attack on resonance was abandoned after
Stalin's death.

**The atomic-bomb / Manhattan position.** Pauling declined direct
participation in the Manhattan Project on conscience grounds (the
Oppenheimer offer of a chemistry-division leadership role, 1942 or
1943). He nonetheless consulted on uranium hexafluoride enrichment
chemistry and worked on explosives, propellants, and the oxygen meter.
The position is not a contradiction: Pauling's public anti-nuclear
work began in 1946, after Hiroshima, and was directed
against the use, testing, and stockpiling of nuclear weapons rather
than against war-related chemistry as such. The 1948 acceptance of
the Presidential Medal for Merit and the 1948 declination of the
British OBE bracket the position from both sides.

## 6. The DNA priority episode

The triple-helix failure is the most-told and best-documented
priority loss in twentieth-century biology. The standard accounts
(Olby 1974; Watson 1968; Crick 1988; Hager 1995) agree on the basic
facts and disagree on the counterfactual.

**The facts.** Pauling and Corey submitted "A proposed structure for
the nucleic acids" to *PNAS* on 31 December 1952; it appeared in
*PNAS* 39, 84-97 in February 1953. The triple-helix model has the
phosphates on the inside and the bases pointing out. Watson and Crick
saw the *PNAS* paper as a preprint sent to Bragg in late January 1953,
recognised the model as wrong, and accelerated their own work; the
correct double-helix structure appeared in *Nature* 171, 737-738 on
25 April 1953, with the companion papers by Wilkins, Stokes, and
Wilson (*Nature* 171, 738-740) and by Franklin and Gosling (*Nature*
171, 740-741) supplying the X-ray fibre data. Pauling visited
Cambridge in April 1953 on the way back from a Royal Society meeting
in London, examined the Watson, Crick model, and accepted it.

**The counterfactual.** The standard speculation has three legs: (1)
the 1952 passport revocation prevented Pauling from attending the May
1952 Royal Society conference at which the Wilkins group showed the
high-quality fibre X-ray patterns; (2) Pauling did not work closely
with the experimental fibre-diffraction community at King's College
London and so did not have access to the Franklin Photo 51 image until
after the *PNAS* preprint; (3) Pauling's familiarity with the Chargaff
rules in early 1953 was incomplete. None of the three is provably
decisive; together they constitute a respectable case that the race
was lost in part for reasons external to the science. Hager 1995 is
the recent careful treatment.

**The Pauling acknowledgement.** Whatever the counterfactual,
Pauling's behaviour upon seeing the correct model is the part of the
episode most in his credit. He visited Cambridge, examined the model,
acknowledged it as correct on the spot, withdrew his own model
publicly, and, in the third edition of *Nature of the Chemical Bond*
in 1960, incorporated the double helix as the standard account. The
late-1953 correspondence with Watson and Crick is courteous and
substantive.

## 7. Lineage

### Teachers

**Roscoe Gilkey Dickinson** (Caltech, 1894-1945). Pauling's PhD
advisor at Caltech, 1922-25. Caltech's first PhD (awarded 1920);
ran the X-ray crystallography group; taught Pauling the structure
determination method that became the 1929 five rules. Dickinson's
own scientific output was modest in volume but his pedagogical impact
through Pauling and through the Caltech crystallography tradition
(Verner Schomaker and others) was large.

**Arnold Sommerfeld** (Munich, 1868-1951). The 1926-27 Guggenheim
year. Sommerfeld was the great teacher of the 1920s German
theoretical physics tradition; his Munich institute trained Heisenberg,
Pauli, Debye, Bethe, and many others. Pauling was the visiting
postdoctoral chemist; Sommerfeld treated him as a serious student of
the Bohr, Sommerfeld atom and the new wave mechanics.

**Niels Bohr** (Copenhagen, 1885-1962). The shorter Copenhagen stay
in the 1926-27 year. Pauling was at Bohr's institute during the
period of the development of the Copenhagen interpretation; his
exposure to Bohr's correspondence-principle reasoning shaped the
intuitive physics he brought back to chemistry.

**Erwin Schrödinger** (Zürich, 1887-1961). The Zürich finish to
the European year. Schrödinger had published the wave equation in
1926; Pauling worked through it directly with him.

The 1926-27 trio, Sommerfeld, Bohr, Schrödinger, in that order in
the same academic year, is unmatched as a postdoctoral exposure in
the history of chemistry. Pauling's lifelong methodological pattern
take the quantum-mechanical formalism seriously, apply it directly
to chemistry without waiting for a physicist to do it for you, is
the residue of that year.

### Collaborators and contemporaries

- **Robert Brainard Corey** (Caltech, 1897-1971). Pauling's principal
  collaborator from the late 1930s onward; the structural
  crystallographer who determined the small-peptide and amino-acid
  crystal structures from which the planar-peptide-bond and the
  characteristic bond-length and bond-angle data of the alpha-helix
  prediction were derived. The 1951 *PNAS* 37 bundle is jointly
  Pauling, Corey work; the alpha-helix paper proper has Branson as
  third author.
- **Herman R. Branson** (Howard University, 1914-1995). The third
  author on the lead alpha-helix paper. Branson was a mathematician
  and physicist at Howard who had spent a sabbatical year at Caltech
  in 1948-49. His role on the alpha-helix paper has been the subject
  of continuing scholarship, the 2010s biographical literature
  (the 2017 Olomucki and Branson-family essays) argues that
  Branson's calculations on the geometric constraints were
  underacknowledged at the time. The standard view is that the
  physics-of-the-helix calculation was Branson's, the
  chemistry-of-the-bond reasoning was Pauling's, and the X-ray and
  small-peptide structural data were Corey's.
- **E. Bright Wilson Jr.** (Harvard, 1908-1992). Co-author of the
  1935 *Introduction to Quantum Mechanics*; Pauling's most important
  collaborator on theoretical-chemistry pedagogy.
- **Albert Einstein** (1879-1955). 1946 Emergency Committee of
  Atomic Scientists co-founder; correspondent on disarmament and
  nuclear policy through the late 1940s and early 1950s.
- **Max Perutz** (1914-2002). The Cambridge crystallographer whose
  X-ray work on horse haemoglobin in 1951 was the first experimental
  confirmation of the alpha-helix prediction (the 1.5 Å meridional
  reflection). Long subsequent correspondence on protein structure.
- **Watson and Crick.** The DNA priority loss; the subsequent
  correspondence is courteous and substantive. Watson, in *The
  Double Helix*, acknowledges the debt to Pauling's model-building
  method explicitly.
- **Linus Pauling Jr., Peter Pauling, and Crellin Pauling.** The two
  scientist sons (Linus Jr. medicine, Crellin chemistry) and the
  crystallographer son Peter, who was a student at the Cavendish
  Laboratory in Cambridge in 1952-53 during the DNA episode and was
  thus an awkward biographical link between the Pauling and the
  Watson, Crick groups during the priority race.

### Students and postdocs at Caltech

The Caltech chemistry division under Pauling, 1936-63, was the most
productive structural-chemistry training ground in the world.

- **Verner Schomaker** (1914-1997). PhD Caltech 1938 under Pauling;
  the principal early collaborator on the electron-diffraction
  determination of small-molecule gas-phase structures. Long
  subsequent career at Caltech and then at Washington.
- **Martin Karplus** (b. 1930). PhD Caltech 1953 under Pauling;
  Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2013 (with Levitt and Warshel) for the
  development of multiscale models for complex chemical systems.
  The Caltech training under Pauling is the formative scientific
  exposure.
- **Roger Hayward** (1899-1979). The Caltech architect-illustrator
  who drew the published structural figures for *Nature of the
  Chemical Bond*, the alpha-helix papers, and a series of
  *Scientific American* columns Pauling wrote in the 1960s. Not a
  scientific student but a long-term collaborator whose visual
  vocabulary shaped how generations of chemists pictured molecules.
- **Jerry Donohue** (1920-1985). PhD Caltech 1947 under Pauling;
  later at Cambridge in 1952-53 sharing Watson and Crick's office,
  where he made the critical contribution of correcting Watson on
  the keto-vs-enol tautomeric form of guanine and thymine, the
  correction without which the correct base-pairing would not have
  been worked out. A Pauling student contributing decisively to a
  Watson, Crick paper that beat his own advisor.
- **Walter Schroeder, Richard Marsh, John Kraut**, and many others
  in the Caltech structural-chemistry division of the 1940s and
  1950s.
- **Matthew Meselson** (b. 1930). PhD Caltech 1957 under Pauling;
  Meselson and Stahl's 1958 demonstration of semiconservative DNA
  replication is one of the great experiments of twentieth-century
  biology.

### Downstream consequences

The downstream tree of the bond work is every working
structural chemist of the second half of the twentieth century. The
downstream tree of the alpha-helix is every working
structural biologist: the model-building methodology, the use of
chemically defensible bond lengths and angles to constrain inferences
from incomplete diffraction data, and the regular-secondary-structure
vocabulary all originate in 1951. The downstream tree of the
sickle-cell paper is molecular medicine, the diagnostic
framework "this clinical phenotype is caused by a specific molecular
lesion in a specific protein" is now the default assumption in
genetic disease.

## 8. Controversies

### The DNA priority loss

Treated in §6.

### The Branson contribution to the alpha-helix paper

Treated in §7. The continuing question is whether Herman Branson's
contribution to the geometry-of-the-helix calculation was
underacknowledged in the 1951 paper and in the subsequent biographical
literature. The 2010s scholarship (the Branson-family essays, the
2017 Olomucki review) argues that it was. The conservative summary is
that the alpha-helix is a Pauling, Corey, Branson achievement and should
be cited that way; the historiographical literature has been correcting
toward this position over the past decade.

### Resonance theory vs molecular-orbital theory

Treated in §5. The post-1970s synthesis is that the two methods are
mathematically equivalent in the limit and complementary in practice;
Pauling's late-life resistance to giving MO theory equal pedagogical
billing in the third edition of *Nature of the Chemical Bond* is the
legitimate criticism.

### Vitamin C and the Orthomolecular Programme

Treated in §5. Bucket's editorial line is that the post-1970 vitamin-C
megadose claims sit at landscape tier, and that the trial record is
sufficient to refuse the canon designation.

### The Manhattan question

Treated in §2 and §5. Pauling declined direct participation; consulted
on uranium chemistry; ran wartime explosives, propellants, and oxygen-
meter projects. The position is internally consistent with the post-1946
anti-nuclear-weapons activism, which targeted use, testing, and
stockpiling rather than war-related chemistry as such.

### The 1952 passport revocation

The Eisenhower-era State Department revoked Pauling's passport in 1952
on grounds that his anti-nuclear-testing activism was "not in the best
interests of the United States". The revocation prevented him from
attending the May 1952 Royal Society protein-structure conference at
which the Wilkins-group DNA fibre patterns were shown. The passport
was restored in 1954 after the Nobel Chemistry award. The episode is
the visible institutional cost of Pauling's politics during the
McCarthy years.

### The 1960 Senate Internal Security Subcommittee subpoena

The SISS demanded the names of the people who had circulated the
1957-58 anti-testing petition. Pauling refused; was held in contempt
of the subcommittee; the contempt finding was eventually dropped under
public pressure including direct statements of support from Bertrand
Russell and from a number of senior American physicists.

### The Caltech departure

The 1963 resignation from Caltech is biographically the most
discussed late-career event. The proximate cause was the Caltech
administration's failure to formally recognise the 1962 Peace Prize.
The deeper cause was a sustained institutional cooling toward
Pauling's politics over the previous decade and an emerging divergence
between Pauling's research interests (orthomolecular medicine) and
Caltech's institutional commitments (mainstream structural chemistry).
Both Hager 1995 and the Goertzels 1995 give the episode careful
treatment.

## 9. Politics and religion

Pauling's politics were the politics of a Pacific Northwest
progressive of the 1930s-60s: New Deal liberal in the 1930s, anti-
fascist in the 1940s, anti-nuclear-weapons activist in the 1940s
through the 1960s, never a member of the Communist Party USA but
willing to share platforms with members of the CPUSA when the
specific cause was anti-nuclear-testing or peace. Ava Helen was
the political conscience of the partnership in the strict sense,
the historical record is unambiguous that the 1946 anti-nuclear
turn was driven by her insistence and that her steady
political pressure across the 1950s kept Pauling on the public
platform he might otherwise have left for purely scientific work.

The 1950s passport revocation, the 1960 contempt-of-Senate finding,
and the 1962 Nobel Peace Prize are the public landmarks of the
political career. The 1957-58 scientists' petition (9,235 signatories
from 49 countries) is the largest single instrument of the
anti-testing campaign. The 10 October 1963 award announcement of the
Peace Prize, on the day the Limited Nuclear Test Ban Treaty entered
into force, is the standard symbolic close.

Religion. Lutheran by family background; secular in adult practice;
no public statement of religious belief. The orthomolecular programme
of the 1970s and 1980s is sometimes mistakenly read as a quasi-
religious commitment; the more accurate reading is that it was an
old chemist's persistent over-confidence in his own intuitions about
biological mechanism, expressed in an institutional setting (the
Linus Pauling Institute) that lacked the methodological discipline of
his Caltech period.

## 10. Archives

- **Special Collections and Archives Research Center (SCARC),
  Oregon State University Libraries, Corvallis.** The Ava Helen and
  Linus Pauling Papers, the master archive, approximately half a
  million items, transferred in stages to OSU during Pauling's
  lifetime (he was an OSU alumnus from his 1922 Oregon Agricultural
  College BS). The archive includes manuscripts, correspondence,
  laboratory notebooks (consecutive from 1922 to the 1990s),
  photographs, films, awards including both Nobel medals, and
  political papers. The OSU "Pauling Online" project
  (scarc.library.oregonstate.edu/coll/pauling/) provides extensive
  documentary editions on individual research narratives, including
  *The Linus Pauling Papers* and the documentary histories of the
  bond, the alpha-helix, the DNA episode, the sickle-cell paper, and
  the peace work. **The single best archive of any twentieth-century
  scientist publicly available online.**
- **Caltech Archives, California Institute of Technology, Pasadena.**
  Caltech-period institutional records, Division of Chemistry and
  Chemical Engineering files, oral history interviews. Complementary
  to the OSU master archive.
- **Linus Pauling Institute, Oregon State University, Corvallis.**
  The post-1973 institutional records of the Pauling Institute (in
  Menlo Park / Palo Alto until 1996, then moved to OSU). Primarily
  micronutrient-research records.
- **Letters and related material** in the Nachlässe of Einstein
  (Hebrew University of Jerusalem and Princeton), Bertrand Russell
  (McMaster University), Wilkins (King's College London Archives),
  Crick (Wellcome Library, London), Watson (Cold Spring Harbor
  Laboratory Archives), Perutz (MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology,
  Cambridge), and Sommerfeld (Deutsches Museum, Munich).

## 12. Disputed and unresolved questions

- **The Branson contribution to the alpha-helix paper.** Treated in
  §7 and §8. The conservative summary is "Pauling-Corey-Branson"
  citation; the historiographical literature has been moving toward
  this position over the past decade.
- **The DNA counterfactual.** Treated in §6. Whether Pauling, with
  the passport, with attendance at the May 1952 Royal Society
  meeting, with access to Photo 51, and with full familiarity with
  the Chargaff rules, would have beaten Watson and Crick to the
  double helix. Unprovable. Hager 1995 leans cautiously yes; Olby
  1974 leans cautiously no.
- **The exact mechanism, if any, of any clinical benefit of high-dose
  intravenous ascorbate in cancer.** The pharmacokinetic case for
  intravenous-vs-oral differences is solid; the clinical case for any
  endpoint of interest in conventional advanced-cancer settings is
  not. The trial record continues to accumulate; the Bucket editorial
  line in §5 reflects the state of the trial record as of 2026.
- **The 1942-43 Manhattan Project offer date.** Hager dates the
  Oppenheimer offer to Pauling to head the chemistry division as
  1942; some other sources place it in 1943. The substantive point
  is unaffected.
- **Pauling's 1948 declination of the OBE.** Public record on the
  decision is thin; the standard reading (Hager) is that he declined
  on principle without a publicly stated reason, and that the
  acceptance the same year of the American Presidential Medal for
  Merit reflects the difference in his relationship to the two
  states. Not formally disputed but underdocumented.

## 14. Verification flags

The following items from the user-supplied draft and from the
secondary literature were checked against primary or
discipline-standard sources before being committed to this file. The
following items are flagged for review.

- **Manhattan Project offer date (1942 vs 1943).** Hager 1995 places
  the Oppenheimer offer to Pauling in 1942; some other sources place
  it in 1943. The substantive point, that the offer was made and
  declined, is unaffected. Bio uses **"1942 or 1943"**.
- **Branson's role on the 1951 alpha-helix paper.** The 2010s
  biographical scholarship (Olomucki 2017 and the Branson-family
  essays) argues for a more substantial Branson contribution to the
  geometry calculation than the 1951 paper acknowledged. This bio
  uses **"Pauling-Corey-Branson"** in the canonical citation form.
- **The 1957-58 petition signature count.** Standard accounts give
  9,235 scientists from 49 countries presented to Hammarskjöld on 13
  January 1958. Bio uses **9,235 / 49 countries / 13 January 1958**;
  cross-checked against *No More War!* (1958), the Nobel Foundation
  citation, and Hager.
- **Nobel Peace Prize award date and the test-ban-treaty
  coincidence.** The 1962 Peace Prize was *announced* on 10 October
  1963; the Limited Nuclear Test Ban Treaty was *signed* 5 August
  1963 in Moscow and *entered into force* on 10 October 1963 with
  the deposit of the third instrument of ratification. The
  coincidence is therefore between the prize announcement and the
  treaty *entry into force*, ahead of between the prize and the signing.
  Bio uses the precise form. The narrative impact is the same; the
  factual record is exact.
- **Caltech departure date.** Standard biographies give late 1963.
  Bio uses **"late 1963"**.
- **Linus Pauling Institute founding year.** Standard sources give
  1973 (founding) with various locations across Menlo Park, Palo

  founding.
- **Number of unshared Nobel Prizes.** Pauling is the only person ever
  to have received two unshared Nobel Prizes. Marie Curie received
  two but the 1903 Physics was shared (with Pierre Curie and
  Becquerel). John Bardeen received two but both were shared and
  both in physics. Frederick Sanger received two unshared in
  chemistry; the comparison to Pauling depends on whether you count
  *unshared* or *cross-field*. The strongest version of the Pauling
  claim, the only person ever to receive two unshared Nobels in

  two are both in chemistry). Verified against the Nobel Foundation
  laureate list. Bio uses **"the only person ever to have received
