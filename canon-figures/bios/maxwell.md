## 1. Why Maxwell is in the canon

Maxwell is the most consequential physicist between Newton and Einstein, and the
case for that claim is short. He produced the first complete mathematical
field theory of any branch of physics — the unified theory of electricity,
magnetism, and light, set out in *A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic
Field* (1865) and codified in *A Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism*
(1873). He produced, independently of Boltzmann and a few years before him,
the first probabilistic-statistical theory of any physical system — the
Maxwell distribution of molecular velocities (1860) and its full dynamical
derivation (1867). He gave the first definitive mathematical analysis of
Saturn's rings (Adams Prize 1856; published 1859), proving that the rings
cannot be solid or fluid and must be a swarm of small bodies. He produced
the first colour photograph (1861) as the experimental capstone of his
work on trichromatic colour vision. He founded the Cavendish Laboratory at
Cambridge in 1871 and made it, within a generation, the most productive
physics laboratory in the world.

The canon entry sits in `02-physics` for the obvious reason: every one of
the foundation-tier contributions named above is a physics contribution.
The cross-branch listings are conservative.

- `02-physics` (primary). Three sub-branches receive foundation-tier
  contributions: `electromagnetism/` (the field equations and the
  prediction that light is an electromagnetic wave), `statistical-mechanics/`
  (the Maxwell and Maxwell–Boltzmann distributions, Maxwell's demon), and
  `classical-mechanics/` (Saturn's rings, the dynamical method generally).
- `09-art` (cross). The colour-vision work — the trichromatic theory worked
  out experimentally by Maxwell in 1855–61 and demonstrated in the 1861
  Royal Institution lecture with the first colour photograph — is the
  empirical foundation under the Young–Helmholtz theory of colour vision,
  and is therefore canon-tier in the colour and form sub-branch of
  `09-art`. The branch is listed as cross- rather than primary because
  Maxwell's principal contribution there is empirical confirmation, not
  the doctrine itself.
- `03-chemistry` (cross). The kinetic theory of gases is the bridge by
  which physical chemistry inherited statistical-mechanical reasoning;
  the cross-link runs through `03-chemistry/thermodynamics/` and
  `03-chemistry/spectroscopy/`. Maxwell did not himself work in chemistry,
  but the downstream effect on chemical thermodynamics (via Boltzmann and
  Gibbs) is large enough to register a cross-branch entry.

Polymath-rule audit (per `_polymaths.md`): if you remove Maxwell from
`02-physics`, the electromagnetism sub-branch loses its single founding
text and the statistical-mechanics sub-branch loses one of two
independent originator-tier contributions. Maxwell qualifies for the
polymath register on a weaker basis than Helmholtz — his cross-branch
contributions are real but not foundation-tier in the strict sense — and
is included there in the *partial polymath* category, with the qualifier
"physics primary; colour vision (art) and statistical-mechanical
underpinnings of physical chemistry (chemistry) as cross-branches".

## 2. Life timeline

**1831 — Edinburgh.** Born 13 June at 14 India Street, Edinburgh, the
only surviving child of John Clerk Maxwell, an advocate of the Scottish
Bar from the Clerk family of Penicuik, and Frances Cay, of an old
Northumbrian family. The double surname *Clerk Maxwell* came from the
Maxwell estates at Middlebie in Galloway, inherited through John Clerk's
mother and held on the condition that the surname be maintained. The
family moved soon after James's birth to the small estate at Glenlair,
near Corsock in the Galloway hills, which John Clerk had built up in
the 1820s; this remained Maxwell's home of the heart for his whole life
and the place to which he returned during his retirement years
(1865–71) to write the *Treatise*.

**1839 — mother's death.** Frances Cay Maxwell died in December 1839
of abdominal cancer — by the family record, of the same disease and at
the same age (forty-eight) at which James himself would die forty
years later. He was eight. His father became the principal parent and
the closest companion of his early intellectual life; the surviving
correspondence between father and son, preserved in the Cambridge
Maxwell papers and quoted at length in Campbell and Garnett's 1882
*Life*, is one of the most affecting sets of family letters in
nineteenth-century British science.

**1841–47 — Edinburgh Academy.** Enrolled at the Edinburgh Academy in
November 1841, aged ten. The early years at the Academy were difficult
(the Galloway accent and home-tutored manners earned him the nickname
"Dafty"); the difficulty did not last. By thirteen he was winning
mathematical prizes; by fourteen he had submitted his first paper to
the Royal Society of Edinburgh, *On the Description of Oval Curves and
Those Having a Plurality of Foci* (read on his behalf by James Forbes
to the RSE on 6 April 1846 because the author was considered too young
to read it himself; published in *Proceedings of the Royal Society of
Edinburgh* 2, 1845–46, 89–91). The paper generalises the gardener's
construction for the ellipse to curves with arbitrary numbers of
foci; it is a piece of pure geometry, but the precocity it shows is
unmistakable.

**1847–50 — University of Edinburgh.** Matriculated at sixteen.
Studied natural philosophy with **James David Forbes** (1809–1868),
mathematics with Philip Kelland, and logic and metaphysics with
William Hamilton (the Scottish philosopher, not the Irish
mathematician). Forbes was the decisive influence: a serious
experimentalist with first-rate equipment, an interest in colour and
in glaciology, and the willingness to give an undergraduate the run
of his laboratory. The colour-vision work that culminated in the
1861 photograph began under Forbes's direction in these years.
Maxwell published two further papers as an Edinburgh undergraduate:
*On the Theory of Rolling Curves* (*Trans. RSE* 16, 1849, 519–540)
and *On the Equilibrium of Elastic Solids* (*Trans. RSE* 20, 1853,
87–120, communicated by Kelland and read in 1850), the latter
containing what became known as the Maxwell stress tensor for
photoelasticity.

**1850–54 — Cambridge, Trinity (and Peterhouse first).** Matriculated
at Peterhouse in October 1850 but migrated almost immediately to
Trinity, where the chances of a fellowship were better and the
mathematical company stronger. Read for the mathematical tripos under
**William Hopkins** (1793–1866), the Cambridge private tutor known as
the *wrangler-maker* — Hopkins's pupils included Stokes, Kelvin
(Thomson), Tait, Routh, Cayley, and Maxwell, an extraordinary
concentration of nineteenth-century mathematical talent. Sat the
tripos in January 1854 and graduated **second wrangler** to Edward
John Routh, who was first; Maxwell and Routh were declared joint
winners of the Smith's Prize, which was decided on a separate and
more demanding examination and was the better measure of original
mathematical power. Stokes, who set the Smith's Prize papers,
wrote that the two candidates had performed equally; the Smith's
Prize examination of 1854 contained, on Stokes's set, the question
"if a closed curve be drawn embracing a magnet, the integral of the
component magnetic force along the curve is equal to four times pi
times the strength of all the magnetic poles enclosed by it" — the
first appearance in print of what would become Stokes's theorem and
one of the mathematical tools Maxwell would use in his own
electromagnetic work.

Elected fellow of Trinity in October 1855.

**1855–56 — *On Faraday's Lines of Force*.** Read in two parts to the
Cambridge Philosophical Society on 10 December 1855 and 11 February
1856; published in *Transactions of the Cambridge Philosophical
Society* vol. 10, 1855–56, 27–83. The paper takes Faraday's
*Experimental Researches in Electricity* (1839–55) and constructs a
mathematical translation of Faraday's lines-of-force imagery into a
formal field theory, using the analogy of an incompressible fluid
flowing through a porous medium. It is not yet the field theory of
1865 — there is no displacement current, no derivation of light as an
electromagnetic wave — but it is the first systematic mathematical
treatment of Faraday's physical ideas. Maxwell sent a copy to Faraday,
who replied in March 1857 with the often-quoted line: "I was at first
almost frightened when I saw such mathematical force made to bear
upon the subject, and then wondered to see that the subject stood
it so well." The friendship between the two — Faraday forty years
older, the great experimentalist of the Royal Institution; Maxwell
the young Cambridge fellow — was the most important intellectual
relationship of Maxwell's life.

**1856 — Aberdeen.** Took the chair of natural philosophy at
Marischal College, Aberdeen, in November 1856, aged twenty-five. The
appointment came partly because Forbes had moved to St Andrews and
recommended him, partly because John Clerk Maxwell's death (3 April
1856) had made the family situation in Edinburgh different — Maxwell
inherited Glenlair and the obligations attached to it. He was the
youngest professor in any Scottish university at the time of his
appointment.

**1856 — Adams Prize set.** The Adams Prize at Cambridge for the year
1857 (decided in March 1857) had been set in 1855 on the topic of the
motion of Saturn's rings — specifically, whether the rings could be
shown to be solid, fluid, or composed of disconnected masses, and
whether the mathematical analysis of their stability could decide
between these alternatives. The problem was Challis's; the topic had
been pressed by Kelvin and others. Maxwell entered, won (his was the
only essay submitted that the examiners considered acceptable), and
the essay was published as *On the Stability of the Motion of
Saturn's Rings* (Cambridge: Macmillan, 1859). The essay shows that
neither a solid ring nor a fluid ring is dynamically stable: a solid
ring would be torn apart by tidal forces unless its mass distribution
were finely tuned in a way no plausible physical process would
produce, and a fluid ring would break up into droplets which, by their
mutual gravitational attraction, would clump and produce instabilities
of a different kind. The conclusion — that the rings must be
composed of "an indefinite number of unconnected particles" — was
established mathematically by Maxwell in 1856 and confirmed
spectroscopically by James Keeler at Lick Observatory in 1895
(differential rotation of inner and outer rings), and observationally
by the Voyager flybys (1980–81) and Cassini (2004–17). The ring
analysis was Maxwell's first major piece of original physical work
and is foundation-tier in `02-physics/classical-mechanics/`.

**1858 — marriage.** Married Katherine Mary Dewar, daughter of the
Principal of Marischal College, on 2 June 1858 at Aberdeen. The
marriage was childless. Katherine collaborated actively with Maxwell
on the colour experiments (she sat for several of the colour-matching
trials, since reliable trichromatic matches required a small panel of
observers with normal colour vision); she was unwell for much of
their married life, and Maxwell's care for her in the late 1860s and
through the Cambridge years is one of the persistent themes of the
private correspondence. Campbell and Garnett's 1882 *Life* records
that Maxwell once said, in a letter to a cousin, that he and his wife
"read theology together every evening" — a remark that sounds
sentimental and was meant literally, and is the most direct private
record of his religious life.

**1860 — Marischal merger and the move to King's.** The 1858 Scottish
Universities Commission decided to merge Marischal College and King's
College, Aberdeen, into a single University of Aberdeen, effective
1860. The merger eliminated one of the two natural philosophy chairs;
the King's incumbent, David Thomson, was retained, and Maxwell was
made redundant. He applied unsuccessfully for the Edinburgh chair
left vacant by Forbes's move to St Andrews (the chair went to Tait —
Maxwell's friend, but a more established figure with the political
backing of the Edinburgh establishment); he then took the chair of
natural philosophy at King's College London, vacated by Thomas
Minchin Goodeve, in July 1860.

**1860 — Maxwell distribution.** *Illustrations of the Dynamical
Theory of Gases*, *Philosophical Magazine* 4th series, vol. 19, 1860,
19–32 (Part I: "On the Motions and Collisions of Perfectly Elastic
Spheres") and vol. 20, 1860, 21–37 (Part II: "On the Process of
Diffusion of Two or More Kinds of Moving Particles Among One
Another", and Part III: "On the Collision of Perfectly Elastic Bodies
of Any Form"). The first probabilistic-statistical derivation of any
physical distribution: Maxwell shows that the velocities of molecules
in a gas at thermal equilibrium are distributed according to what is
now called the Maxwell distribution, derived from the assumption that
the three orthogonal components of velocity are independent and
identically distributed and from the requirement of rotational
symmetry. The argument is short, elegant, and was the
methodological turning-point in the kinetic theory of gases. The
later, fuller dynamical derivation came in 1867 (see below).

**1861 — first colour photograph.** Royal Institution lecture
delivered 17 May 1861, *On the Theory of Three Primary Colours*. The
lecture demonstrated trichromatic colour reproduction by projecting
three black-and-white photographic transparencies of a tartan ribbon
through red, green, and blue filters in superposition on a screen.
The plates had been taken by Thomas Sutton (the inventor of the
single-lens reflex camera) under Maxwell's direction. The result is
recognised as the first colour photograph; it is also the experimental
demonstration of the Young–Helmholtz trichromatic theory of colour
vision, established by Maxwell's own quantitative colour-matching
experiments using the *colour box* and the *colour wheel* he had
designed at Edinburgh and developed further at Aberdeen. The original
plates survive at the Cavendish Laboratory and the National Museums
Scotland.

**1861–62 — *On Physical Lines of Force*.** Four parts in
*Philosophical Magazine* 4th series, vol. 21 (Parts I and II, March
and April 1861) and vol. 23 (Parts III and IV, January and February
1862). The four parts give the celebrated mechanical model of the
electromagnetic field — vortex tubes for magnetic field lines, with
small idle wheels (later called "particles of electricity") between
them mediating electric current — and use it to derive the
electromagnetic equations including, in Part III, the **displacement
current**. Part III also contains Maxwell's first calculation of the
speed of propagation of an electromagnetic disturbance from purely
electromagnetic measurements (the ratio of the electromagnetic to the
electrostatic unit of charge, measured by Wilhelm Weber and Rudolf
Kohlrausch in 1857), and the famous statement: "we can scarcely avoid
the inference that *light consists in the transverse undulations of
the same medium which is the cause of electric and magnetic
phenomena*" (vol. 23, p. 22, italics in original). The mechanical
model was always understood by Maxwell himself as a model — a
heuristic device, an analogy — and not as a literal description of
the ether. The 1865 paper drops the model and derives the same
equations directly from the dynamics of the field.

**1865 — *A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field*.** Read
to the Royal Society on 8 December 1864; published in *Philosophical
Transactions of the Royal Society* 155, 1865, 459–512. The pure
field-theoretic derivation: Maxwell drops the mechanical model of
1861–62 and derives the electromagnetic equations directly from the
energy of the field, treated as a dynamical system with its own
state variables. The paper contains twenty equations in twenty
variables in the original notation; Heaviside and Hertz independently
in the 1880s reduced these to the four vector equations now taught as
*Maxwell's equations*. The paper also contains, as a deduction from
the equations, the prediction that a disturbance in the field
propagates at a speed equal to the ratio of the electromagnetic and
electrostatic units of charge, that this ratio is numerically equal
within experimental error to the measured speed of light, and that
*light is therefore an electromagnetic wave*. The prediction was
confirmed experimentally by Heinrich Hertz in 1887–88.

**1865 — retirement to Glenlair.** Maxwell resigned the King's
College London chair in spring 1865 and returned to Glenlair, where
he lived until 1871. The reasons were multiple: Katherine's poor
health, his preference for the country, his obligations to the
estate, and the desire for sustained writing time. The *Treatise*
was written almost entirely during these years, along with the 1867
gas-theory paper and *Theory of Heat*. The retirement was not
idleness — it was the most productive concentrated writing period
of his life.

**1867 — *On the Dynamical Theory of Gases*.** *Philosophical
Transactions of the Royal Society* 157, 1867, 49–88; communicated by
Stokes. The full dynamical derivation of the velocity distribution,
using the method of transfer equations (the "Maxwell equation of
transfer") and treating intermolecular forces as inverse-fifth-power
repulsions for tractability. Boltzmann's 1872 *Weitere Studien über
das Wärmegleichgewicht unter Gasmolekülen* (*Wiener Berichte* 66,
275–370) generalises the result, removing the inverse-fifth-power
restriction and proving the H-theorem; the joint product is the
**Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution**. The 1867 paper is the
foundation-tier text for `02-physics/statistical-mechanics/`
alongside Boltzmann 1872.

**1871 — Cavendish Laboratory.** The seventh Duke of Devonshire,
William Cavendish (himself a former second wrangler and Smith's
prizeman, 1829), endowed a new chair of experimental physics at
Cambridge in 1870 and offered to fund the construction of a
laboratory to go with it. The chair was offered first to Kelvin, who
declined to leave Glasgow, then to Helmholtz, who declined to leave
Berlin, and then to Maxwell, who accepted in March 1871 on the
condition that he could continue to spend the long vacations at
Glenlair. He took up the chair in October 1871 and spent the rest
of his life designing the laboratory, equipping it, and editing the
unpublished electrical papers of Henry Cavendish (the Duke's
ancestor, whose 1771–81 manuscripts on electrical capacity, the
inverse-square law for electrostatic force, and the conduction of
electrolytes Maxwell published as *The Electrical Researches of the
Honourable Henry Cavendish*, Cambridge University Press, 1879). The
laboratory opened in 1874. Within fifteen years of Maxwell's death,
under Rayleigh and then J. J. Thomson, the Cavendish would discover
the electron (1897), the isotope (1913), and the structure of the
atom (1911); within fifty years it would be the laboratory in which
the neutron was discovered (Chadwick, 1932) and the atom split
(Cockcroft and Walton, 1932).

**1871 — *Theory of Heat*.** London: Longmans, Green; multiple later
editions, the most-cited being the 4th of 1875 with revisions
incorporating the *Treatise*'s mature thermodynamic position. The
book is a textbook in the strict sense — written for the working
engineer or the advanced student, not the specialist physicist — but
it contains, in the chapter on the second law, the celebrated
thought experiment of *Maxwell's demon*: a being able to sort fast
and slow molecules between two compartments without doing work,
apparently violating the second law. Maxwell's purpose was to
demonstrate that the second law is *statistical*, not absolute, and
that its certainty derives from the practical impossibility of any
real device performing the demon's discrimination — not from a
mechanical impossibility in principle. The thought experiment has
been generative for a century and a half: Szilard's 1929 *Über die
Entropieverminderung in einem thermodynamischen System bei Eingriffen
intelligenter Wesen* (*Zeitschrift für Physik* 53, 840–856) showed
that the demon's measurement itself produces entropy at least equal
to the entropy reduction it permits, recovering the second law;
Landauer 1961 and Bennett 1973–82 connected the analysis to the
thermodynamics of computation and to the energetic cost of erasing
information; and the demon remains the most-cited entry point into
the relation between thermodynamics and information theory.

**1873 — *A Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism*.** Oxford:
Clarendon Press, 2 vols. The systematic exposition of the field
theory, in the form Maxwell wanted to leave it. The work is in four
parts: electrostatics (vol. 1), electrokinematics (vol. 1),
magnetism (vol. 2), and electromagnetism (vol. 2), with the field
equations, the displacement current, and the electromagnetic theory
of light all presented in their developed form. The original
twenty-equations notation is retained; the modern four-vector form
is the work of Heaviside (*Electromagnetic Theory*, 3 vols.,
1893–1912) and Hertz, both posthumous to Maxwell. The second
edition (Niven, 1881) corrects several errors that Maxwell had
identified in his own copy before his death; the third edition
(J. J. Thomson, 1891) is the canon edition-of-record per the physics
pass-1 entry — Thomson's notes carry the Cavendish-school
interpretation forward.

**1879 — death.** Diagnosed in spring 1879 with abdominal cancer of
the same kind that had killed his mother. He continued working
through the summer at Glenlair, then returned to Cambridge in October
to die under the care of his physician, George Edward Paget. Died
5 November 1879 at Cambridge, aged forty-eight — the same age, to
the year, at which Frances Cay Maxwell had died. Buried at Parton
Kirk, Galloway, beside his parents. Lord Rayleigh (John William
Strutt) succeeded him in the Cavendish chair in 1879; J. J. Thomson
succeeded Rayleigh in 1884.

**1890 — *Scientific Papers*.** Edited by W. D. Niven; Cambridge
University Press, 2 vols. The standard reference edition of the
collected papers; reprinted by Dover in two volumes (2003) and still
in print. The two volumes contain everything except the *Treatise*,
*Theory of Heat*, and the *Electrical Researches of Henry
Cavendish*.

## 3. Primary works in detail

### *On Faraday's Lines of Force* (1855–56)

The first paper of the electromagnetic series. Maxwell's project is
to translate Faraday's qualitative concept of *lines of force* —
introduced in *Experimental Researches* over twenty years from 1839
— into a quantitative mathematical theory, without committing to
any specific mechanical model of the underlying medium. The vehicle
is the analogy with the steady flow of an incompressible fluid
through a resistive medium: the field lines correspond to streamlines,
the field intensity to fluid velocity, the source distribution to
source and sink density. The paper introduces what would later be
called the *vector potential* in primitive form and uses Stokes's
theorem (which Stokes had set on the Smith's Prize examination Maxwell
sat in 1854) to relate line integrals of the field to surface
integrals of its curl. There is no displacement current, no
prediction of electromagnetic waves; the paper is a methodological
clearing of the ground.

The historical significance is twofold. First, it establishes that
Faraday's physical intuitions — which the Continental tradition,
following Ampère and Weber, had treated as picturesque but
mathematically unserious — could be given the same mathematical
rigour as the action-at-a-distance theories. Second, it sets the
research programme that Maxwell would carry through to the *Treatise*:
the field as the fundamental physical object, mechanical models as
heuristic devices to be discarded once the equations are clear.

### *On the Stability of the Motion of Saturn's Rings* (1859)

The Adams Prize essay. The argument is as follows. A solid ring would
have to satisfy a set of dynamical conditions for stable rotation
that no plausible mass distribution would produce; Laplace's earlier
treatment had assumed the rings were rigid and had concluded
(incorrectly, by an analysis Maxwell shows to be incomplete) that
they were stable for an asymmetric mass distribution. Maxwell extends
the analysis to general perturbations and shows that the asymmetric
mass distribution Laplace required would be itself unstable to small
displacements. A fluid ring is examined next; Maxwell shows by a
Fourier analysis of the perturbations that any fluid ring of the
relevant dimensions would break up into a string of droplets which
would themselves be gravitationally unstable to clumping. The
remaining possibility — a swarm of unconnected particles — is shown
to be the only dynamically admissible configuration, and Maxwell
shows that such a swarm is stable to first order if its surface
density is below a certain bound.

The essay was published as a Macmillan pamphlet in 1859 (62 pages)
and is in the *Scientific Papers* vol. 1 at pp. 288–376. The result
was definitive: every subsequent observation of the rings — the
spectroscopic detection of differential rotation by Keeler in 1895,
the resolution of the rings into discrete particles by the Voyager
flybys in 1980–81, the meticulous mapping of the ring structure by
Cassini between 2004 and 2017 — has confirmed Maxwell's conclusion.
Airy reportedly called the essay one of the most remarkable
applications of mathematics to physics ever made.

The paper is foundation-tier in `02-physics/classical-mechanics/`
and is the founding text of the modern dynamics of self-gravitating
particle systems, of which the analysis of planetary rings, accretion
disks, and the spiral structure of galaxies are descendants.

### *Illustrations of the Dynamical Theory of Gases* (1860)

The Maxwell distribution. Two papers in the *Philosophical Magazine*
of 1860 (Part I in vol. 19, Parts II and III in vol. 20). Part I
contains the derivation. The argument is short enough to summarise
in a paragraph: assume the three Cartesian components of molecular
velocity are independent and identically distributed; assume that
the distribution depends only on the speed (rotational symmetry);
the only function with both properties is the Gaussian, and
normalisation plus the kinetic-energy interpretation of temperature
fixes the constants. The argument is *not rigorous* in the modern
sense — the independence assumption is unjustified, and Boltzmann's
1872 derivation supplies the missing dynamical content via the
H-theorem — but the result is correct, and the method of
*statistical-distribution-as-physical-prediction* was without
precedent in physics.

The other achievements of the 1860 papers are less famous and worth
naming. Maxwell predicts that the viscosity of a gas is independent
of its density (a result so counter-intuitive that he and Katherine
verified it experimentally in their London attic in 1865, with a
torsional pendulum oscillating in air at varied pressures, published
as *On the Viscosity or Internal Friction of Air and Other Gases*,
*Phil. Trans.* 156, 1866, 249–268). He gives the first kinetic
account of diffusion. He computes a value for the mean free path of
a gas molecule.

### *On Physical Lines of Force* (1861–62)

The mechanical-model paper. Four parts. The model: space is filled
with rotating vortex tubes whose axes are along magnetic field lines
and whose angular velocity is proportional to the magnetic field
strength; between adjacent vortex tubes are small spherical
"particles of electricity" acting as idle wheels, allowing the
vortices to rotate in the same sense without slipping at their
contacts. Electric current is the translation of the idle-wheel
particles; magnetic induction is the rotation of the vortex tubes.
By giving the vortex medium an elastic restoring property, Maxwell
adds — in Part III — the displacement current: a transverse
disturbance in the elastic medium, propagating at a speed determined
by the ratio of the electrostatic to the electromagnetic unit of
charge.

The decisive computation, in Part III: Maxwell takes the ratio of
units measured by Weber and Kohlrausch in 1857 (3.1 × 10^10 cm/s)
and notes that it agrees, within experimental error, with Fizeau's
1849 measurement of the speed of light (3.15 × 10^10 cm/s). The
inference — that light is an electromagnetic wave — is one of the
two or three most important inferences in the history of nineteenth-
century physics. The agreement was, at the time of Maxwell's writing
in 1861, a coincidence among three numbers that were all
independently measured and that nobody had any prior reason to
expect to be related. The 1865 paper would derive the relation from
the equations themselves; the 1861 paper announces it and traces it
to the equations via the model.

It is essential to the historical record that Maxwell did not
believe in the literal reality of his vortex-and-idle-wheel
machinery. His own language in Part I is explicit: the model is "a
mode of conception" and "a temporary instrument of research"; the
equations it produces are what survive when the scaffolding is taken
down. The 1865 *Dynamical Theory* takes the scaffolding down. The
common popular description of Maxwell as having believed in a
literal mechanical ether is therefore wrong; he believed in *some*
medium for the propagation of electromagnetic disturbances (he was
a nineteenth-century natural philosopher and the field-without-a-
medium was hard for him to conceive), but the specific vortex
machinery of 1861 was a model in the modern sense, used to derive
equations and discarded once the equations were in hand.

### *A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field* (1865)

The pure field-theoretic derivation. The mechanical model is gone.
The field is treated as a dynamical system with its own state
variables (the components of the electric and magnetic field at every
point of space), its own kinetic and potential energy (the Lagrangian
of the field), and its own equations of motion (derived by applying
Lagrange's equations to the field Lagrangian). The result is a system
of twenty equations in twenty variables — vector and scalar potentials,
field components, current density, charge density, and the constitutive
relations — that contain everything Maxwell wanted to say about
electromagnetism, including the electromagnetic theory of light.

The reduction to the four vector equations now taught in every
undergraduate physics class is the work of Heaviside (in *The
Electrician* through the 1880s, collected in *Electrical Papers*,
2 vols., 1892, and developed further in *Electromagnetic Theory*,
3 vols., 1893–1912) and of Hertz (whose 1890 paper *Über die
Grundgleichungen der Elektrodynamik für ruhende Körper*, *Annalen
der Physik* 40, 577–624, gives the modern symmetric form). Both are
posthumous to Maxwell. The four-equation form is canonically
associated with Maxwell, and properly so — the physics is his — but
the notation is Heaviside's and the symmetric arrangement is
Hertz's.

The paper is the founding text of `02-physics/electromagnetism/`. The
prediction that light is an electromagnetic wave was confirmed by
Hertz in 1887–88 with the laboratory generation and detection of
free electromagnetic waves. The downstream tree from this paper is
literally the entire history of electrodynamics: Lorentz's electron
theory, Einstein's special relativity (which arose from the
incompatibility of Maxwell's equations with Galilean kinematics),
quantum electrodynamics, and the entire electromagnetic-communication
economy.

### *On the Dynamical Theory of Gases* (1867)

The full derivation of the Maxwell distribution and the Maxwell
equations of transfer for the kinetic theory. The 1860 derivation
had relied on the unjustified assumption of independent velocity
components; the 1867 paper derives the distribution from the
collision dynamics of gas molecules, treating them as point particles
interacting via inverse-fifth-power repulsive forces (a choice made
for mathematical tractability — the inverse-fifth-power case happens
to be analytically solvable for the relevant collision integrals).
The paper introduces the *equation of transfer* for an arbitrary
function of molecular velocity — what is now called the Maxwell
moment equation — and uses it to derive the macroscopic equations of
gas dynamics (the Navier–Stokes equations) from the microscopic
collision dynamics. This is the first derivation in the history of
physics of macroscopic thermodynamic and hydrodynamic equations from
microscopic mechanics.

Boltzmann's 1872 *Weitere Studien* generalises Maxwell's transfer
equation to the *Boltzmann transport equation* and proves the
H-theorem, which gives the statistical-mechanical foundation of the
second law of thermodynamics. The combined Maxwell–Boltzmann
distribution and the Boltzmann transport equation are the founding
apparatus of `02-physics/statistical-mechanics/`.

### *Theory of Heat* (1871)

The textbook. Written for engineers and advanced students, not for
specialist physicists. The chapters cover thermometry, calorimetry,
the laws of thermodynamics, the kinetic theory of gases, and the
electromagnetic theory of heat. The book went through ten editions
in Maxwell's lifetime and posthumously through 1908. The single
most-cited passage is the introduction of *Maxwell's demon* in
Chapter XXII, "Limitation of the Second Law of Thermodynamics":

> If we conceive of a being whose faculties are so sharpened that
> he can follow every molecule in its course, such a being, whose
> attributes are still as essentially finite as our own, would be
> able to do what is at present impossible to us…

The demon sits at a frictionless trapdoor between two compartments
of gas at the same temperature; he opens the trapdoor for fast
molecules going one way and slow molecules going the other,
producing a temperature difference without doing work. The thought
experiment has had a downstream history in physics, information
theory, and the foundations of computation that is out of all
proportion to the brevity of its presentation. Szilard 1929 reduced
the demon's operation to a measurement-and-record cycle and showed
the measurement itself produces compensating entropy. Landauer 1961
("Irreversibility and Heat Generation in the Computing Process",
*IBM Journal of Research and Development* 5, 183–191) proved that
the *erasure* of information has an irreducible thermodynamic cost
of at least *kT ln 2* per bit. Bennett 1973 and 1982 unified the
analysis. The demon is, after Schrödinger's cat, the most cited
thought experiment in the foundations of physics.

### *A Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism* (1873)

The systematic exposition. Two volumes, four parts: electrostatics,
electrokinematics, magnetism, electromagnetism. The notation is
Hamilton's quaternions augmented with Maxwell's own conventions; this
made the *Treatise* harder to read than it needed to be and is one
of the reasons the four-vector reduction (Heaviside, Hertz) was so
quickly absorbed once it appeared in the late 1880s. The mathematical
content is the field equations of 1865, with much fuller discussion
of boundary conditions, of the physical interpretation of the
displacement current, of the electromagnetic theory of light, and
of the application of the equations to specific problems
(propagation in media, refraction, polarisation, the Faraday effect).
The work also contains the first systematic discussion of the
*stress tensor of the electromagnetic field* — the Maxwell stress
tensor — and of the momentum carried by the electromagnetic field,
which would become important in twentieth-century field theory.

The third edition (J. J. Thomson, 1891) is the canon
edition-of-record per the physics pass-1 entry — Thomson's notes
incorporate the Heaviside–Hertz reformulation and the experimental
results of the 1880s, including Hertz's demonstration of free
electromagnetic waves. This is the edition specialists cite.

### *The Electrical Researches of the Honourable Henry Cavendish* (1879)

Edited from manuscripts in the Cavendish family papers, supplied to
Maxwell by the seventh Duke of Devonshire. The manuscripts (mostly
1771–1781) anticipate Coulomb's inverse-square law for electrostatic
force, Faraday's specific inductive capacity (Cavendish had measured
the dielectric constants of several substances), Ohm's law, and
several other nineteenth-century electrical results. Cavendish had
not published any of this in his lifetime; Maxwell's edition is the
posthumous publication, with copious editorial notes, that
established Cavendish's priority on most of the points. The
edition is one of the standard texts in the history of
eighteenth-century experimental physics.

### *Scientific Papers* (1890, posth.)

Edited by W. D. Niven; Cambridge University Press, 2 vols. Reprinted
by Dover (Mineola, NY) in 2003. Contains every published paper
except the books. The standard reference edition.

## 4. Foundation contributions, branch by branch

### 02-physics

**`electromagnetism/` — the field theory.** *On Faraday's Lines of
Force* (1855–56), *On Physical Lines of Force* (1861–62), *A
Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field* (1865), and *A
Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism* (1873) constitute the founding
texts of classical electromagnetism. The displacement current is
Maxwell's; the prediction that light is an electromagnetic wave is
Maxwell's; the equations are Maxwell's, in their content if not in
their modern symmetric notation. The downstream tree includes
Hertz's 1887–88 confirmation of electromagnetic waves, the entire
electromagnetic-communication economy from Marconi onwards, the
incompatibility of Maxwell's equations with Galilean kinematics that
forced Einstein to special relativity in 1905, and the field-theoretic
template that quantum electrodynamics (Tomonaga, Schwinger, Feynman,
Dyson, late 1940s) generalised to the quantum case.

**`statistical-mechanics/` — the velocity distribution and the
demon.** The 1860 *Illustrations* and the 1867 *Dynamical Theory of
Gases* are originator-tier texts of statistical mechanics, jointly
with Boltzmann 1872. The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution is the
canonical first result of equilibrium statistical mechanics. The
1871 *Theory of Heat* introduces Maxwell's demon, which has shaped
the foundations of statistical mechanics, the thermodynamics of
information, and the energetic theory of computation for a century
and a half.

**`classical-mechanics/` — Saturn's rings.** *On the Stability of
the Motion of Saturn's Rings* (1859) is foundation-tier in the
dynamics of self-gravitating particle systems. The downstream
includes the modern theory of planetary rings, of accretion disks
in astrophysics, and of the spiral structure of galaxies (where
similar stability analyses are foundational).

### 09-art (cross-branch)

**`color-form/` — trichromatic colour vision.** Maxwell's
quantitative colour-matching experiments at Edinburgh and Aberdeen
(1855–61), the colour box and colour wheel as instruments for
psychophysical measurement, and the 1861 colour photograph
demonstration are the empirical foundation of the Young–Helmholtz
trichromatic theory of colour vision. The cross-branch link sits in
`09-art/color-form/` because the modern science of colour
reproduction — printing, photography, screen display — descends
directly from Maxwell's three-colour principle. Helmholtz's
*Handbuch der physiologischen Optik* (vol. 2, 1860) gives the
theoretical apparatus; Maxwell's experiments give the numerical and
demonstrative foundation. The cross-branch listing is fair to both.

### 03-chemistry (cross-branch)

**`thermodynamics/` and `spectroscopy/` — statistical mechanics as
a bridge.** Maxwell did not himself work in chemistry, but the
kinetic theory of gases and the statistical interpretation of
thermodynamics that he founded are the apparatus by which physical
chemistry developed. Boltzmann, Gibbs, Nernst, and Ostwald all built
on Maxwell's statistical-mechanical foundation. The cross-branch
listing is included for completeness; the contribution to
`03-chemistry` is mediated, not direct.

## 5. What Maxwell got wrong

Honest sections on what the figure got wrong are required by the
editorial conventions in `bios/README.md`. The list for Maxwell is
shorter than for most foundation-tier figures, partly because he was
careful and partly because he died young.

**The mechanical ether of *On Physical Lines of Force* (1861–62).**
The vortex-and-idle-wheel model was a heuristic device, not a
physical claim — Maxwell himself was explicit on this point — but
in the popular history of physics it is often treated as a literal
proposal that the ether contained mechanical machinery. The 1865
*Dynamical Theory* drops the model and derives the same equations
from the field Lagrangian directly, which is the form Maxwell wanted
to leave the theory in. The model is not a mistake in the sense of
"Maxwell believed something false"; it is a mistake in the weaker
sense of "Maxwell published a model that, taken literally, is false".
The ether itself, as a propagation medium for electromagnetic waves,
was abandoned by Hertz in his 1888 papers (which derive the wave
results without the ether scaffolding) and definitively eliminated
by Einstein's 1905 *Zur Elektrodynamik bewegter Körper* (*Annalen
der Physik* 17, 891–921), in which the postulate that the speed of
light is independent of the source removes the need for any
preferred frame.

**The inverse-fifth-power assumption in the 1867 gas paper.** The
choice was made for mathematical tractability — the collision
integrals are analytically soluble for the inverse-fifth-power case
and difficult otherwise. The result (the Maxwell distribution) is
correct independently of the assumption; Boltzmann's 1872
generalisation removes the restriction. This is not so much a
mistake as a methodological compromise that Boltzmann then improved.

**Nothing of canon-tier consequence got rejected.** Saturn's rings,
the field theory, the velocity distribution, the demon, the colour
work — every major position Maxwell took was either confirmed
within his lifetime or stood unchallenged afterwards. There is no
Maxwell equivalent of Helmholtz's vortex-atom programme or of
Einstein's later cosmological constant. The honest list of mistakes
is short because Maxwell was unusually careful about the scope of
his claims.

## 6. The 1861–62 vs 1865 question

The historical micro-question that occupies most close studies of
Maxwell's electromagnetic work is the relation between the four-part
*On Physical Lines of Force* (1861–62) and the *Dynamical Theory of
the Electromagnetic Field* (1865). The papers contain the same
equations but reach them by different routes: 1861–62 via the vortex
mechanical model, 1865 via the Lagrangian formulation of the field
as a dynamical system. The standard view, fixed by Harman's *The
Natural Philosophy of James Clerk Maxwell* (CUP, 1998) and Siegel's
*Innovation in Maxwell's Electromagnetic Theory* (CUP, 1991), is
that Maxwell discovered the displacement current and the
electromagnetic theory of light using the model in 1861, then
recognised in 1864–65 that the model was unnecessary scaffolding and
that the field equations could be derived directly from the energy
of the field treated as a dynamical system. The 1865 paper is
therefore the mature statement; the 1861–62 paper is the discovery
record.

This matters for the canon entry because the *Treatise* (1873) takes
the 1865 derivation as its base and the model is mentioned only in
historical asides. Anyone reading the *Treatise* without prior
knowledge of the 1861–62 paper would not guess that the equations
had ever been derived from a mechanical model; the model is, in
Maxwell's mature view, dispensable.

## 7. Lineage

### Teachers

**James David Forbes** (1809–1868). Edinburgh natural philosopher.
Maxwell's principal undergraduate teacher and the figure who put him
onto colour vision as a research problem. Forbes was a serious
experimentalist in the Scottish tradition (his glaciology work in
the Alps in the 1840s is the principal nineteenth-century empirical
basis for the theory of glacial motion), and his laboratory at
Edinburgh was open to Maxwell during the 1847–50 years.

**William Hopkins** (1793–1866). The Cambridge private tutor known as
the *wrangler-maker*. Hopkins's pupils included Stokes, Thomson
(Kelvin), Tait, Routh, Cayley, and Maxwell — an extraordinary
concentration of nineteenth-century mathematical talent passing
through one tutor's rooms. Hopkins did not produce major original
research himself; his contribution to the canon is the training
pipeline he ran for the tripos. Maxwell sat the tripos in January
1854 and graduated second wrangler under Hopkins's preparation.

**George Gabriel Stokes** (1819–1903). Lucasian Professor of
Mathematics at Cambridge from 1849; set the Smith's Prize papers
that Maxwell sat in 1854 (the paper containing the first appearance
of Stokes's theorem); communicated several of Maxwell's papers to
the Royal Society, including the 1867 *Dynamical Theory of Gases*.
Senior figure and mathematical resource throughout Maxwell's career;
not a teacher in the strict sense but consistently generous.

**William Thomson, Lord Kelvin** (1824–1907). Glasgow natural
philosopher, Hopkins pupil ahead of Maxwell at Cambridge. Long and
detailed correspondence with Maxwell from the early 1850s onwards;
intellectual partner in electromagnetic theory (the 1856 vortex-atom
hypothesis would later use Helmholtz's vortex theorems and provided
the mathematical setting Maxwell drew on for parts of *On Physical
Lines of Force*). Kelvin was the older counterpart in the British
mathematical-physics tradition and the figure with whom Maxwell
worked out, by long letter, many of the difficulties of the
electromagnetic programme. Their disagreements were as productive as
their agreements: Kelvin never accepted the 1865 field-theoretic
derivation as physically satisfactory, and the historical record of
his objections (preserved in the Kelvin papers at Cambridge and
Glasgow) is one of the most useful sources for the alternative
positions of nineteenth-century electromagnetism.

**Michael Faraday** (1791–1867). Royal Institution. Forty years
older than Maxwell. The most important intellectual relationship of
Maxwell's life. Faraday was the great experimentalist whose
*Experimental Researches in Electricity* (1839–55) provided the
physical content of Maxwell's mathematical theory. The relationship
was conducted partly by correspondence, partly by Maxwell's visits
to the Royal Institution. Faraday's 1857 letter on the first
electromagnetic paper ("almost frightened when I saw such
mathematical force made to bear upon the subject…") is the founding
document of the relationship as a public matter. Maxwell consciously
saw his role as the mathematical translator of Faraday's
experimental intuitions; the *Treatise* opens with an
acknowledgement of Faraday's primacy that is unusual in nineteenth-
century scientific writing for its directness.

### Collaborators and contemporaries

- **Peter Guthrie Tait** (1831–1901). Edinburgh natural philosopher
  from 1860; close personal friend from boyhood (they had been at
  Edinburgh Academy together); collaborator on quaternion methods
  and on thermodynamic exposition. Tait's *Sketch of Thermodynamics*
  (1868) is the British counterpart to *Theory of Heat*. The
  Maxwell–Tait correspondence (preserved in the Cambridge Maxwell
  papers and in the Tait papers at Edinburgh) is a central source
  for the Scottish and Cambridge thermodynamics of the 1860s and
  1870s and includes most of Maxwell's surviving private joking,
  which was extensive and characteristic.
- **William Thomson, Lord Kelvin.** Treated above.
- **George Gabriel Stokes.** Treated above.
- **Hermann von Helmholtz.** Mutual admiration and mutual citation,
  but no close personal contact. Helmholtz was offered the Cavendish
  chair before Maxwell and declined. The 1881 Faraday Lecture by
  Helmholtz, on the granular nature of electricity, would have been
  delivered to a Royal Institution audience that included many of
  the figures (Tyndall, Rayleigh, the Cavendish circle) who had
  shaped Maxwell's career.
- **Ludwig Boltzmann** (1844–1906). Vienna physicist. Independent
  developer of the statistical foundations of thermodynamics; the
  joint name "Maxwell–Boltzmann" attaches to the velocity
  distribution, but Boltzmann's H-theorem (1872) and his statistical
  interpretation of entropy (*S = k log W*, on his Vienna tombstone)
  are independent achievements.
- **Josiah Willard Gibbs** (1839–1903). Yale physicist; Gibbs's
  *On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances* (*Trans. Conn.
  Acad.* 1875–78) is the systematic completion of the statistical
  mechanics Maxwell and Boltzmann had begun. Gibbs and Maxwell did
  not correspond; Maxwell read Gibbs's 1873 graphical thermodynamics
  papers and praised them in print in *Theory of Heat* (4th ed.,
  1875), which was Gibbs's first significant European recognition.
- **Thomas Sutton** (1819–1875). Photographer and inventor of the
  single-lens reflex camera; took the three plates for the 1861
  colour photograph demonstration under Maxwell's direction.
- **William Spottiswoode** (1825–1883). Mathematician and printer;
  his firm printed several of Maxwell's books. President of the
  Royal Society 1878–83.
- **John William Strutt, Lord Rayleigh** (1842–1919). Cambridge
  physicist; Maxwell's eventual successor in the Cavendish chair
  (1879–84); Nobel Prize 1904 for the discovery of argon. Rayleigh
  ran the Cavendish on the lines Maxwell had set, with greater
  personal wealth (he was an Essex landowner of substantial means)
  enabling him to fund equipment and assistants from his own
  pocket.

### Students at the Cavendish (1871–1879)

The Cavendish under Maxwell was new, small, and his tenure short.
The named direct students are few — George Chrystal, Richard Glazebrook,
Donald MacAlister, Ambrose Fleming (later of vacuum-tube fame),
J. H. Poynting (of Poynting's theorem and the Poynting vector). Maxwell
died too young to have a long student lineage.

The Cavendish lineage that mattered, however, is institutional rather
than personal: the laboratory Maxwell founded would, under his
successors, become the British physics pipeline.

- **John William Strutt, Lord Rayleigh** (1842–1919). Cavendish
  Professor 1879–1884.
- **Joseph John Thomson** (1856–1940). Cavendish Professor 1884–1919.
  Discovery of the electron, 1897.
- **Ernest Rutherford** (1871–1937). Cavendish Professor 1919–1937.
  Discovery of the atomic nucleus (with Geiger and Marsden, 1909–11),
  splitting of the atom (1917, formally), the proton, and the
  beginning of nuclear physics.
- **William Lawrence Bragg** (1890–1971). Cavendish Professor
  1938–1953. X-ray crystallography (with father William Henry Bragg,
  Nobel Prize 1915, the only father-and-son joint Nobel); the
  Cavendish under Bragg was the laboratory in which Watson and Crick
  did the DNA work in 1953.

The downstream institutional tree is implausibly large. The
electron, the isotope (Aston, 1913), the nuclear atom, the splitting
of the atom (Cockcroft and Walton, 1932), the neutron (Chadwick,
1932), and the structure of DNA (Watson and Crick, 1953) all came
out of the laboratory Maxwell founded.

## 8. Controversies

### Action at a distance vs the field

The dominant continental tradition in electromagnetism through the
mid-nineteenth century was the action-at-a-distance theory of Ampère
and Weber, in which the fundamental physical objects were point
charges and currents interacting at a distance via instantaneous
forces. The British (Faradayan) tradition, of which Maxwell is the
mathematical exponent, took the field as the fundamental object and
treated the action between charges as mediated by states of strain
in the field. The two pictures gave the same predictions for most
laboratory situations through the 1860s and 1870s, and the dispute
remained unresolved during Maxwell's lifetime. Hertz's 1887–88
demonstration of free electromagnetic waves — waves propagating
through space at finite speed, in regions where there were no
charges or currents — was the experiment that decided the question
in favour of the field theory. The action-at-a-distance theories
could not produce free waves; the field theory required them, with
the speed predicted by Maxwell from the ratio of electromagnetic
to electrostatic units.

### Quaternions vs vectors

Maxwell wrote the *Treatise* in a notation that combined Hamilton's
quaternions with his own conventions; this made the book harder to
read than it needed to be. Heaviside in the 1880s developed the
modern vector notation (gradient, divergence, curl) and reduced
the twenty equations of 1865 to four; Hertz independently arrived
at a similar reduction. The Heaviside–Hertz reformulation made the
field theory accessible to a much wider audience and is the form
in which the equations have been taught ever since. The historical
controversy — whether the quaternion notation hindered the reception
of the *Treatise* — is genuine; the consensus is that it did, and
that the 1888–92 Heaviside reformulation is the reason the theory
became the European standard rather than a British curiosity.

### The H-theorem and the foundations of statistical mechanics

Boltzmann's H-theorem (1872) was attacked by Loschmidt (1876) on
reversibility grounds — if the underlying mechanics is reversible,
how can H decrease monotonically? — and by Zermelo (1896) on
recurrence grounds. The dispute is post-Maxwell, but the apparatus
under attack was Maxwell's. Maxwell's own position, in the 1871
*Theory of Heat* and in the late letters, was that the second law
is statistical rather than absolute and that the apparent paradoxes
of Loschmidt and Zermelo dissolve once this is seen — which is the
modern view. Maxwell was therefore on the right side of a dispute
he did not live to see fully formulated.

### Maxwell's demon and the second law

The demon was a controversy from the moment it was published.
Critics through the 1870s and 1880s objected that the demon either
violated the second law or depended on a fundamentally implausible
discrimination capacity. Maxwell's own answer — that the second law
is statistical and that the demon's discrimination is practically
impossible but not mechanically impossible — has been refined by
twentieth-century work (Szilard, Landauer, Bennett) but not
overturned.

## 9. Politics and religion

Politically Maxwell was a quiet conservative of the Galloway country
type — landowner, Episcopalian, deeply attached to Glenlair, not
much interested in national politics. He served on no public
committees of national importance, took no political positions in
print, and avoided the larger political controversies of his time
(the second Reform Act, the disestablishment of the Irish Church).
The closest he came to a public position was his opposition, with
Tait, to the Cambridge undergraduate movement of the late 1860s for
the abolition of compulsory chapel; both Maxwell and Tait considered
the theological education of the British university tradition worth
preserving against utilitarian reform.

Religiously Maxwell was a serious Scottish Presbyterian who attended
Episcopal services in Cambridge for the practical reason that the
Trinity chapel was Anglican. The theology essays preserved in
Campbell and Garnett's 1882 *Life* — particularly the long letter to
his cousin on the relation of natural philosophy to revealed
religion — show a careful, reflective, and undogmatic mind that took
both science and Christian doctrine with full seriousness and saw no
conflict between them. The line he is most often quoted as having
said, on his deathbed, was something to the effect that "I have been
thinking how very gently I have always been dealt with"; the line is
recorded by Campbell, who was with him at the end, and is consistent
with the religious temper of the surviving letters. Maxwell composed
a private prayer ("Almighty God, who hast created man in Thine own
image, and made him a living soul that he might seek after Thee, and
have dominion over Thy creatures, teach us to study the works of Thy
hands…") which was found among his papers and printed by Campbell
and Garnett. He wrote no public theology; the religious life is in
the private letters and the prayer.

The combination — first-rate mathematical physics, deep religious
seriousness, and no public friction between the two — is unusual
enough in nineteenth-century British science to be worth noting.
Maxwell is not Tyndall (anti-clerical, Belfast Address 1874); he is
not Huxley (combative agnostic); he is not the popular caricature of
the Victorian scientist at war with the Victorian Christian. He is
the version of the British natural philosopher in which both
identities sit comfortably together.

## 10. Personal life and personality

The standard picture of Maxwell, fixed by Campbell and Garnett's
1882 biography and refined by Mahon's *The Man Who Changed
Everything* (Wiley, 2003), is consistent across the sources: witty,
modest, wide-reading, affectionate, and personally happy in a way
that a nineteenth-century scientific career did not generally
produce. He wrote light verse fluently in English and in Scots —
the *Rigid Body Sings* (a parody of Burns's *Comin' Thro' the Rye*
on the kinematics of rigid body collisions, written for the Red
Lion dinner of the British Association in 1874) is the most-quoted
example, but there are dozens more in the *Scientific Papers* and
in Campbell and Garnett. He wrote serious poetry as well, of which
*A Paradoxical Ode* and the lines on the death of his father are
the most often anthologised.

The marriage to Katherine Mary Dewar in 1858 was, by every account,
a happy one. Katherine was unwell for much of their married life;
Maxwell's care for her, especially during her long illness in the
late 1860s while he was writing the *Treatise* at Glenlair, is
recorded with characteristic understatement in the surviving
letters. They had no children. Maxwell's affection for his many
nephews and nieces — the Galloway and Edinburgh Clerk and Maxwell
families were large — is the substitute family record.

He read voraciously and across fields: Plato in Greek, Lucretius in
Latin, contemporary poetry (he was an early admirer of Tennyson and
Browning), the German Romantics, the Scottish theological tradition
(particularly Thomas Erskine of Linlathen, whose theology of divine
love was the closest thing Maxwell had to a personal religious
mentor). The reading shows in the prose of the *Treatise* and the
*Theory of Heat*, both of which are written in a clear, precise,
slightly sly nineteenth-century English that has aged better than
most scientific prose of the period.

The single anecdote that most clearly shows the temper of the man:
when an undergraduate at Trinity asked him, in his Cavendish years,
how to learn enough mathematics to read the *Treatise*, Maxwell is
reported to have said, "It depends on what you wish to read. If you
wish to read all of it, you will need to know everything. If you
wish to read most of it, you will need to know most things." The
remark is in Campbell and Garnett; it is characteristic.

## 11. Archives

- **King's College, Cambridge.** The principal Maxwell papers:
  scientific manuscripts, correspondence, notebooks, and the family
  letters. The papers were given to King's by the family in the
  1920s and have been catalogued repeatedly (most recently by the
  Maxwell at King's project led by the College Library).
- **Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge.** Institutional records of the
  Cavendish from 1871; instruments designed or used by Maxwell;
  some manuscript material.
- **Royal Society Library, London.** Correspondence with the Royal
  Society, manuscripts of papers communicated to *Philosophical
  Transactions*, and parts of the *Treatise* manuscript.
- **University of Edinburgh, Centre for Research Collections.**
  Edinburgh-period material, including Forbes correspondence and
  the family papers from the Clerk Maxwell side.
- **National Library of Scotland, Edinburgh.** Some Maxwell family
  papers and Galloway estate records.
- **Glasgow University Library.** Kelvin–Maxwell correspondence
  (the Glasgow side; the Cambridge side is in the Kelvin papers at
  Cambridge University Library).
- **Royal Institution, London.** Faraday–Maxwell correspondence
  (Faraday side); notes for the 1861 colour-photograph lecture.
- **Trinity College, Cambridge.** Trinity fellowship records,
  undergraduate examination books.

The *Scientific Letters and Papers of James Clerk Maxwell* edited
by P. M. Harman (Cambridge University Press, 3 vols., 1990, 1995,
2002) is the modern reference edition of the correspondence and
unpublished papers and is the standard scholarly source for any
serious work on Maxwell.

## 13. Disputed and unresolved questions

- **The 1861–62 vs 1865 derivation question.** Treated in §6.
  Settled in favour of the view that the model was scaffolding for
  the discovery, not part of the mature theory.
- **The reception delay of the *Treatise*.** Why did the *Treatise*
  take fifteen years (1873 to roughly 1888) to be widely understood
  and accepted on the Continent? The standard answers are: the
  quaternion notation, the absence of a clean four-vector form
  (supplied later by Heaviside and Hertz), and the experimental
  confirmation that Hertz's 1887–88 work supplied. Hunt's *The
  Maxwellians* (1991) is the standard treatment. The view that
  Maxwell's prose style was responsible for the delay (sometimes
  proposed in popular accounts) does not survive close reading;
  the *Treatise* is well written, just notationally awkward.
- **The exact priority relation of Maxwell and Boltzmann on the
  velocity distribution.** Maxwell published the result in 1860;
  Boltzmann's first paper on the distribution is 1868. Maxwell's
  derivation is shorter and less rigorous; Boltzmann's is fuller
  and supplies the dynamical apparatus the 1860 paper lacked. The
  joint name "Maxwell–Boltzmann" is fair to both and the priority
  is Maxwell's; this is settled.
- **The status of Maxwell's demon.** Treated in §8. Vindicated by
  twentieth-century information theory (Szilard, Landauer, Bennett)
  in the form Maxwell originally intended it: the second law is
  statistical, the demon's discrimination is practically impossible
  but not mechanically impossible, and the cost of the demon's
  measurement and erasure recovers the second law in the modern
  formulation.
- **Maxwell and the ether.** Did Maxwell believe in a literal
  mechanical ether? The textual record, careful reading of the 1865
  paper and the *Treatise*, supports the view that he believed in
  *some* propagation medium for electromagnetic waves but that the
  specific vortex machinery of 1861–62 was a model in the modern
  sense. The popular history that has Maxwell defending the
  mechanical ether to his death is wrong; Maxwell's mature view, in
  the *Treatise*, is the field-theoretic view that the equations
  describe a dynamical system without committing to a specific
  microphysical model.
