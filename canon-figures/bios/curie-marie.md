## 1. Why Curie is in the canon: and why under two branches

Marie Curie is filed under both `02-physics` and `03-chemistry`, with a
documented cross to `05-biophysics`. The dual-branch placement is not
biographical hedging. It reflects a real division in her work that the
two Nobel committees themselves drew when they awarded her, eight years
apart, prizes in two different sciences for two different things.

The 1903 Physics Nobel cited "the extraordinary services they have
rendered by their joint researches on the radiation phenomena discovered
by Professor Henri Becquerel." That citation is squarely inside
`02-physics/atomic-physics/`: it sits next to Becquerel 1896 (the
discovery of uranium radiation) and the Rutherford, Soddy 1902-03
disintegration papers. The canon entry is the demonstration, with
Pierre's piezoelectric quartz electrometer, that the phenomenon
Becquerel had seen in uranium salts is a *general atomic property*, not
a peculiarity of one element, and that thorium has it too (April 1898),
and that pitchblende and chalcolite show activity *exceeding* what their
uranium content can explain. That last observation is the canonical
one. It is what forced the inference that there must be other,
unidentified, far more strongly radiating elements present in the ore,
and it is the inference that produced polonium and radium.

The 1911 Chemistry Nobel cited "her services to the advancement of
chemistry by the discovery of the elements radium and polonium, by the
isolation of radium and the study of the nature and compounds of this
remarkable element." That citation belongs to `03-chemistry/`. It is
not a duplicate of the 1903 prize; it is a different claim. The 1903
prize honored a *physical phenomenon*. The 1911 prize honored the
*chemical isolation of two new elements* and the determination of their
atomic weights, radium, characterized as the chloride and
the bromide and weighed against barium with the precision the Sorbonne
balance allowed in 1907 (Ra = 226.45, since revised to 226.025). The
chemistry committee had in fact reserved this contribution to itself in
1903 by asking that radium not be named in the physics citation; they
were holding it for a future chemistry award and they delivered.

The cross to `05-biophysics/radiation-biology/` is not from her
discoveries as such but from the consequences of her experimental
practice. The first generation of radium chemists were also the first
case series in human radiation injury. Curie's own death from aplastic
anemia in 1934, her daughter Irène Joliot-Curie's death from leukemia
in 1956, and the cohort of Radium Institute workers and the American
"radium girls" together constitute the founding empirical record of
chronic low-dose radiation toxicity that the field of radiation biology
was built on top of. The notebooks themselves, held today at the
Bibliothèque nationale de France in lead-lined boxes, requiring a
liability waiver and protective equipment to consult, are physical
evidence of the same point. Radium-226 has a half-life of 1,600 years.
The contamination in those notebooks, on the chairs, on the doorknobs
of the Rue Lhomond shed and the early Radium Institute, will outlast
every reader for ~1,500 years more. The biophysics cross is the line
that connects the 1898 chemistry to the 20th-century clinical record.

The audit test the polymath register requires
(`canon-figures/_polymaths.md`) is met for both primary branches. Remove
Curie from `02-physics/atomic-physics/` and the physics record loses the
demonstration that radioactivity is a general atomic property and the
quantitative measurement programme that turned Becquerel's curiosity
into a research field. Remove her from `03-chemistry/` and the periodic
table loses two elements (Z = 84, Z = 88), each isolated against
ferocious technical odds, and chemistry loses the procedural template
for separating microgram quantities of a new element from tons of ore by
fractional crystallization. The branches cite different specific things;
neither citation can absorb the other. She qualifies on the strict
criterion.

The polymath qualifier is discussed in §13 below. Short version: the
register currently filters for figures whose contribution spans three or
more *foundation branches*, and Curie has two-plus-cross. The
classification is *dual-Nobel-in-different-sciences across two foundation
branches*, with biophysics as a third (downstream-empirical) cross. We
add her to the polymath register with a strong qualifier rather than
omitting her, because the dual-prize-in-two-sciences claim is unique in
the index and the cross-branch density is real even if it is two-plus-one
rather than three.

## 2. Life timeline

**1867, Warsaw, Russian partition.** Born 7 November 1867 in Warsaw,
then capital of the Vistula Land (Kraj Privislinsky), the Russian
imperial administrative name for Congress Poland after the failed
January Uprising of 1863. Polish-language schools, Polish cultural
institutions, and the University of Warsaw had been Russified; Polish
was forbidden as a language of instruction. Father Władysław Skłodowski
taught mathematics and physics at a Warsaw boys' Gymnasium and was
demoted and dismissed in stages over the 1870s for his Polish patriotism;
mother Bronisława Boguska directed a girls' boarding school until
tuberculosis ended her career. Bronisława died of tuberculosis in 1878
when Maria was ten; an older sister, Zofia, had died of typhus three
years earlier. The household was educated, observant Catholic by
background but increasingly secular by Władysław's positivist
temperament, and downwardly mobile in the small genteel manner of a
demoted teacher's family. Maria was the youngest of five.

**1883, Gymnasium gold medal.** Graduated from the Russian-language
private Gymnasium with a gold medal at fifteen. The University of
Warsaw did not admit women. The Russian universities did not admit Poles
on terms a Skłodowski could accept. The path forward, if there was one,
was Paris or Zurich, both of which required money the family did not
have.

**1884-1885, recovery in the country.** A year with cousins in the
Polish countryside (the Zorawski estate at Szczuki, where she would
later return as governess), the standard prescription for what was then
called nervous exhaustion in adolescent women, in her case probably a
mixture of grief, intellectual restlessness, and overwork. The year is
visible in the *Autobiographical Notes* as one of the few periods of
unstructured time in her early life.

**1885-1891, governess and the Flying University.** Maria and her
older sister Bronisława made an arrangement: Maria would work as a
governess in Warsaw and the countryside, send money to Paris to fund
Bronisława's medical degree at the Sorbonne, and Bronisława, once
qualified and married, would receive Maria in Paris and return the
favor. The arrangement held for six years. Maria taught the children of
the Zorawski family in Szczuki and of an industrialist family in Warsaw,
and on her own time read mathematics and physics from her father's
library and attended the *Uniwersytet Latający*, the Flying University,
a clandestine, illegal, peripatetic institution that operated in
Warsaw from roughly 1882 (and formally from 1885) to 1905, holding
classes in private apartments that changed location to evade the
Russian Okhrana, charging students two to four rubles a month, and
admitting women on equal terms with men in defiance of imperial
educational policy. Approximately five thousand students passed through
the Flying University over its existence; Maria Skłodowska is the
single most famous. She also worked, briefly, in a small chemistry
laboratory run by her cousin Józef Boguski at the Museum of Industry
and Agriculture on Krakowskie Przedmieście, her first hands-on
laboratory experience, in a country where she was not legally permitted
to attend a university.

**November 1891, Paris.** Bronisława, now Dr. Bronisława Dłuska and
married to a Polish émigré physician, sent for Maria. Maria arrived at
the Gare du Nord, registered at the Sorbonne (Faculté des sciences) as
*Marie Skłodowska*, and rented a sequence of cheap garrets in the
Quartier Latin (the rue Flatters and rue de Châtillon addresses are the
ones documented). She studied physics and chemistry on a near-starvation
budget, fainted at least once in a lecture from undereating, and worked
through her examinations at a pace that left her first in her cohort of
licence ès sciences physiques (1893) and second in licence ès sciences
mathématiques (1894).

**1894, meeting Pierre.** Józef Kowalski, a Polish physicist visiting
Paris, introduced Marie to Pierre Curie (1859-1906), at the time
laboratory chief at the École municipale de physique et chimie
industrielles (now ESPCI), already known for the discovery of
piezoelectricity (with his brother Jacques, 1880), for the magnetic
work that produced *Curie's law* and the *Curie point*, and for his
construction of the precision torsion balance and the piezoelectric
quartz electrometer that would, four years later, be the central
instrument of the radioactivity programme. They married in a civil
ceremony in Sceaux on 26 July 1895. Marie wore a dark blue wool dress
that she could continue to use as laboratory clothing.

**1895-1897, first daughter.** Irène Curie born 12 September 1897 in
Paris. Marie's PhD work began the following month.

**1897, choice of thesis topic.** Marie needed a doctoral subject.
Becquerel's 1896 papers reporting that uranium salts emit a penetrating
radiation, capable of fogging photographic plates and ionizing air
without prior excitation, were a year old and had attracted, in Paris,
almost nobody. The phenomenon was inexplicable on the energetics of
1896 (no obvious source), and there was no community of workers around
it. Marie chose it precisely because the field was empty and because
Pierre's electrometer could measure ionization currents quantitatively,
where Becquerel had relied on photographic intensity. The decision was
a methodological gamble: switch the field from a qualitative
photographic register to a quantitative electrical one and see what it
showed.

**Spring 1898, the first two findings.** Working in a glassed-over
courtyard at the École municipale (the rue Lhomond building, since
demolished in part), with Pierre's electrometer measuring ionization
current induced in a parallel-plate condenser by the test substance,
Marie surveyed every element she could obtain in pure or compound form.
Two findings emerged within months. First (April 1898 *CR* note):
thorium and its compounds also emit a Becquerel-type radiation.
Independently of Gerhard Carl Schmidt in Erlangen, who reported the
same result a few weeks earlier; the priority is contested but the work
was independent. Second, and more important: certain uranium ores
(pitchblende from Joachimsthal in Bohemia, chalcolite from Cornwall)
show ionization currents *several times stronger than the uranium they
contain can explain*. The inference was unavoidable: the ore must
contain other, stronger emitters, present at low concentration.
The electrometer was the instrument that made the inference possible;
without quantitative readings, the discrepancy would not have been
visible.

The April 1898 *CR* note also introduces the term *radio-actif*
("radioactive") for substances showing this property, which becomes the
field's name.

**Summer, December 1898, polonium and radium.** Pierre joined Marie on
the work full-time in mid-1898. By July, by repeated chemical
fractionation of pitchblende coupled with electrometer readings on each
fraction (the activity *follows* the bismuth fraction), they had
isolated a fraction whose activity was about 400 times that of uranium
and which carried with bismuth chemically. They named the new element
*polonium*, after Marie's homeland, in the July 1898 *CR* note, a
choice that was simultaneously a statement of Polish national identity
in a Paris of the Third Republic, where Polish irredentism was
politically respectable, and a quiet provocation toward the Russian
imperial administration that did not allow the name "Poland" on a
political map. By December, with the chemist Gustave Bémont assisting,
they had isolated a second new fraction, this one carrying with barium
and producing a brilliant green spectroscopic line at 381.47 nm
identified by Eugène Demarçay. They named it *radium*. The December
1898 *CR* note ("Sur une nouvelle substance fortement radio-active,
contenue dans la pechblende," *CR* 127, 1215, signed P. Curie, Mme. P.
Curie, G. Bémont) is the canonical discovery paper for radium.

Neither element had been *isolated* in 1898, they were inferred from
chemical behavior plus activity ratios plus, for radium, a single
spectral line. Isolation was the four-year programme that followed.

**1898-1902, the pitchblende shed.** The Austrian government,
controlling Joachimsthal, sold the Curies several tons of pitchblende
*residue* (the part of the ore left after uranium had been extracted
for glass coloring) cheaply. The processing, repeated fractional
crystallization of the radium-bearing barium fraction, evaporation of
hundreds of kilograms of acid solution at a time, in an unheated and
poorly-ventilated wooden shed across the courtyard from the École
municipale, was Marie's. The work was chemically simple in principle,
brutally laborious in practice, and the procedure that
isolated *one decigram* of nearly pure radium chloride from the order
of a ton of pitchblende residue is documented step by step in her 1903
thesis. The four years in the shed are the central experimental act of
her career and of the founding of radiochemistry as a sub-branch.

**June 1903, doctorate.** *Recherches sur les substances radioactives*
defended at the Faculté des sciences, Sorbonne, on 25 June 1903 in a
packed lecture room. Lord Kelvin attended; Henri Becquerel was on the
jury alongside Gabriel Lippmann (her formal *directeur de thèse*) and
Edmond Bouty. The thesis was published the same year by Gauthier-Villars
and translated rapidly into German and English. It is the first
systematic monograph of the radioactivity field and the document that
established the field's standard methods.

**November 1903, first Nobel.** The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences
awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics to Becquerel (one half) and to
Pierre and Marie Curie jointly (one half). The internal history is
discussed in §11. The original committee draft proposed
Becquerel and Pierre Curie alone, and Marie's name was added after a
warning from the Swedish mathematician Magnus Gösta Mittag-Leffler to
Pierre, followed by Pierre's letter of 6 August 1903 to the committee
insisting that any prize for the radioactivity work that excluded
Marie's name would be a misrepresentation of the work. Marie was the
first woman to receive a Nobel Prize. She and Pierre were ill at the
ceremony and did not travel to Stockholm to accept; Pierre delivered
the Nobel lecture in 1905.

**1904, second daughter.** Eve Curie born 6 December 1904. Eve would
later write the authorized biography (*Madame Curie*, Doubleday 1937).

**19 April 1906, Pierre's death.** Pierre Curie was struck by a
horse-drawn dray on the rue Dauphine in Paris, slipping under the wheel
which crushed his skull. He was forty-six. Marie was thirty-eight.
The University of Paris offered her his chair in physics at the
Faculté des sciences within weeks. She accepted. Her inaugural lecture,
5 November 1906, began with the sentence Pierre had used to close his
last lecture before his death, the only public concession to the
catastrophe she allowed herself. With the appointment she became the
first woman professor at the Sorbonne since the institution's medieval
founding.

**1907, atomic weight of radium.** The atomic weight determination,
226.45 (the modern value is 226.0254), was completed and reported.
The chemistry community now had a periodic table position (Z = 88, the
heaviest alkaline earth) and a candidate atomic weight, defended by
Curie's own measurements. This is the work the 1911 chemistry citation
honors.

**1910, the *Traité*.** *Traité de radioactivité* (Gauthier-Villars,
two volumes, 971 pages), the canonical treatise of the field as it
stood in 1910, written single-author after Pierre's death. It remained
the standard reference until the textbook generation of the 1920s and
30s superseded it. The *Traité* is also the documentary record of
Marie's adoption, by 1910, of the Rutherford-Soddy disintegration
theory, a position she had resisted in 1903-05 (see §10).

**Late 1910, December 1911, Academy and Langevin.** Two events whose
juxtaposition shaped the public Marie Curie of the next decade. (a) On
23 January 1911, the Académie des sciences in Paris voted on her
candidacy for the seat in the Physics section vacated by the death of
Désiré Gernez. She lost to Édouard Branly (an able but lesser figure,
remembered for his coherer in radio detection) by 28 votes to 30 out of
sixty-six members voting. The vote was understood at the time
and since as a vote against admitting a woman, with conservative
Catholic and nationalist (anti-foreign) currents both contributing.
The Académie did not admit a woman until 1979 (Yvonne Choquet-Bruhat as
correspondante earlier; Marguerite Perey as full member). (b) In
November 1911, the Paris paper *Le Journal* (and shortly afterward
*L'Œuvre*) published private letters between Marie and Paul Langevin
(1872-1946), a former student of Pierre's, then estranged from his
wife Jeanne. The letters had been obtained by Jeanne Langevin and
passed to the press during a custody dispute. Langevin and Marie had
been involved romantically since approximately 1910. The press campaign
was conducted in explicitly antisemitic and xenophobic terms, *Le
Journal*'s editor Gustave Téry's accusation that Curie was "a foreign
Jew" who had broken up a French family was triply false (she was Polish,
Catholic by background, atheist in practice) and indicative of the
register. Langevin fought a duel with Téry (no shots exchanged); the
Langevin divorce was settled out of court ten days after the December
1911 Nobel ceremony.

In the same week the affair broke, the Royal Swedish Academy announced
the Nobel Prize in Chemistry would be awarded to Marie alone. Svante
Arrhenius wrote on 1 December 1911 advising her not to attend the
Stockholm ceremony, framing the request as concern that the scandal
would discredit the prize; she replied on 5 December that "the prize has
been awarded for my discovery of radium and polonium" and that "there is
no relation between my scientific work and the facts of my private
life," and that she would attend. She traveled to Stockholm with her
sister Bronisława and her daughter Irène, accepted the prize, and
delivered the Nobel lecture in person on 11 December 1911. She is the
first person, of either sex, to receive Nobel Prizes in two different
sciences. As of the date of this card, she remains one of only five.

**1912-1914, collapse and recovery.** Marie was hospitalized for
several months in 1912 (kidney problems, depression after the public
campaign, probably some component of cumulative radiation injury).
She recovered slowly, took an extended visit to Polish friends, and
returned to Paris in 1913 to oversee the construction of the Institut du
radium, the joint project of the University of Paris and the Pasteur
Institute, on the rue Pierre Curie (then rue d'Ulm continuation), with
two pavilions, the Curie pavilion (physics and chemistry of
radioactivity, directed by Curie) and the Pasteur pavilion (medical
applications). The Institute opened in July 1914.

**August 1914, November 1918, the war.** When the war began Marie
shut down most laboratory work, locked the Institute's radium reserve
in a lead box, and traveled to Bordeaux to deposit it in a bank vault
(the French government had moved south as the German army approached
Paris). She returned to Paris and reorganized her laboratory around the
medical use of X-rays at the front. The result, the *radiologie de
guerre* programme, is documented in her 1921 book *La radiologie et la
guerre* and in greater detail by modern historians: she equipped
roughly twenty mobile radiological vehicles ("ambulances radiologiques,"
nicknamed *petites Curies* by the soldiers, the first "little Curie"
was a Renault touring car fitted out by Marie in October 1914) and
oversaw construction of approximately two hundred fixed radiological
posts in field hospitals; she trained a cohort of about 150 women as
radiological technicians (manipulatrices) in a six-week course at the
Institut du radium and at the Hôtel des Invalides; her teenage daughter
Irène, then seventeen, became one of the first instructors. Modern
estimates put the total number of wounded examined radiologically under
the programme at approximately one million by November 1918. Marie
also developed the use of radon ("radium emanation," in the 1914
nomenclature) sealed in glass needles for therapeutic use at field
hospitals, an early industrial-scale brachytherapy.

The work was funded partly out of her own pocket (she attempted to
donate her two Nobel medals to the war effort and was refused by the
Banque de France, which would not melt them down), partly from a Union
des femmes de France subscription, and partly from the French state
once she had demonstrated the units' clinical value.

**1919-1934, the Radium Institute years.** With the war ended Marie
returned to direction of the Curie pavilion, hired a new generation of
physicists and chemists (most consequentially her son-in-law Frédéric
Joliot, who married Irène in 1926 and joined the Institute), and
managed the Institute through the lean interwar years. The 1921
fundraising trip to the United States, organized by the American
journalist Marie Mattingly Meloney, raised one gram of radium (~$100,000
in 1921 dollars) presented to her by President Warren G. Harding at the
White House on 20 May 1921; a second American trip in 1929 raised a
second gram for the Warsaw Radium Institute, which Marie's sister
Bronisława directed. The American trips strained her health visibly.

She continued to publish *CR* notes, lecture, and direct doctoral work.
Her last student to defend was Marguerite Perey, who would later
discover francium (Z = 87, named for France) and become the first
woman elected to the Académie des sciences (as correspondante 1962).
Among her other Institute alumni: Salomon Rosenblum, Irène and
Frédéric Joliot-Curie, Bertrand Goldschmidt; the Institute trained
several of the principals of the French nuclear programme of 1939
(Joliot, Hans von Halban, Lew Kowarski) whose work on neutron-induced
fission and the controlled chain reaction was overtaken by the war and
the migration of nuclear physics to the Anglophone wartime programmes.

**4 July 1934, death.** Marie died at the Sancellemoz Sanatorium at
Passy in the Haute-Savoie, of aplastic anemia. She was sixty-six.
The 1934 diagnosis is best understood today as bone-marrow failure
from cumulative chronic radiation exposure, primarily from radium
(internal contamination through inhalation of radium-emanation dust,
ingestion via mouth-pipetting, dermal absorption from manipulating
radium salts with bare hands), secondarily from the unshielded X-ray
work of the war years. Her brother Józef Skłodowski, a physician,
wrote the death certificate. She was buried beside Pierre at Sceaux on
6 July 1934.

**1995, Panthéon.** On 20 April 1995, Marie and Pierre Curie were
reinterred in the Panthéon in Paris, by ceremony of President François
Mitterrand. Marie is the first woman buried there in her own right
(rather than as a wife), though Sophie Berthelot, interred with her
husband Marcellin in 1907, technically preceded her in date. The
caskets were enclosed in lead-lined inner shells; ambient measurements
suggested that Marie's remains carry only background-level
contamination, the radium having largely concentrated, during her
lifetime, in her bones, with continuing low emission.

## 3. The 1898 papers, in order

The discovery sequence is sometimes compressed in popular tellings to a
single moment. The actual archive is a sequence of three (then more)
*CR Acad. Sci.* notes between April and December 1898 that progressively
narrowed the field and named what was found.

**(i) 12 April 1898, "Rayons émis par les composés de l'uranium et du
thorium," Mme Sklodowska Curie, *CR* 126, 1101.** Single-author note,
signed in her unmarried name plus marital name. Reports two findings.
First, every uranium compound investigated emits Becquerel rays in
proportion to its uranium content. Second, thorium compounds also
emit. Third, the load-bearing observation, the Bohemian pitchblende
and the Cornish chalcolite show ionization currents stronger than the
uranium they contain can explain, by factors of approximately four
(pitchblende) and two (chalcolite). The note proposes that the ores
contain an unknown element more strongly active than uranium. The
word *radio-actif* appears.

**(ii) 18 July 1898, "Sur une substance nouvelle radio-active,
contenue dans la pechblende," P. Curie et Mme P. Curie, *CR* 127,
175.** Two-author. Reports that fractional precipitation of pitchblende
yields a bismuth-bearing fraction whose activity exceeds uranium's by
a factor of about 400, and proposes the name *polonium* "from the
country of origin of one of us" (Marie was a French citizen by
marriage but kept her Polish national identity throughout her life).
This is the polonium discovery note.

**(iii) 26 December 1898, "Sur une nouvelle substance fortement
radio-active, contenue dans la pechblende," P. Curie, Mme P. Curie, et
G. Bémont, *CR* 127, 1215.** Three-author with the chemist Gustave
Bémont as third signer. Reports that a separate barium-bearing
fraction shows strong activity (~900× uranium for the unconcentrated
sample) and a new spectral line at 381.47 nm observed by Eugène
Demarçay. Proposes the name *radium*. This is the radium discovery
note.

Three things are worth noting about the sequence. First, in 1898 neither
element had been isolated; the *evidence* was activity-versus-chemistry
correlation plus, for radium, a spectral line. Bémont's status as third
author is sometimes elided; Bémont contributed real chemistry. Second,
the discovery was always presented as joint between the Curies; the
question of who did what within the partnership is
unanswerable from the documentary record alone (see §6). Third, the
chemical isolation of radium, what made the periodic-table claim
binding, was the four-year programme that followed and which produced
the 1903 thesis. The 1898 *CR* notes opened a research field; they did
not close it.

Other 1898 *CR* notes include short notes on the conductivity of air
under the influence of these substances, on the activity of fractions
during purification, and a 1899 note (with André-Louis Debierne, who in
the same year discovered actinium independently in pitchblende residues)
on radium's chemical separation.

## 4. The Shed: the Ton and the Decigram

The chemical isolation of radium chloride is the experimental act that
the 1911 chemistry citation refers to and that founded radiochemistry
as a sub-discipline. The technical problem was: starting from
pitchblende residue containing radium at concentrations on the order of
one part in ten million by mass, separate radium from chemically
near-identical barium and concentrate it to a level where its atomic
weight could be measured.

The barium, radium pair separates on fractional crystallization from
acidic solution because the bromides differ slightly in solubility. At
each step the barium-bearing fraction is enriched in radium by a small
factor; the procedure must be repeated thousands of times to enrich by
the required ten-million-fold. Each step requires evaporating large
volumes of acid solution, which in 1898-1902 meant boiling pitchblende
solution in iron and porcelain vessels in an unheated shed across the
courtyard from the École municipale, stirring with iron rods. Wilhelm
Ostwald, visiting the shed, described it as "a cross between a stable
and a potato-cellar"; the German chemist Ernst Cohen called it "a
miserable old shed." The roof leaked. There was no ventilation.

The starting material was approximately one ton of pitchblende residue
(*pechblende*) per batch, supplied by the Austro-Hungarian state-owned
mine at Joachimsthal at favorable cost arranged by Eduard Suess of the
Vienna Academy. The ratio of input to output, as recorded in the 1903
thesis, was approximately one ton of residue → one decigram of nearly
pure radium chloride. The work took four years.

The 1907 atomic weight measurement required a further enrichment to
roughly hundred-milligram quantities and used a microbalance built for
the purpose. The 1907 value (226.45) stood until the 1930s when the
modern value (226.0254) was established by mass spectrometry; the
1907 figure differs in the third decimal. Curie's measurement was the
first quantitatively defensible value.

The work damaged her health. The 1903 thesis already records cracked
and burned hands; by 1911 the fingers in photographs are visibly
ulcerated. The cumulative radium burden is what killed her thirty-two
years later.

## 5. The instrument: Pierre's quartz electrometer

The technical innovation that made the 1898 discoveries possible was
not a chemical method but an electrical instrument: the Curie quartz
piezoelectric electrometer, built by Pierre and Jacques Curie out of
the piezoelectric effect they had discovered in 1880.

The standard radioactivity measurement of 1896, Becquerel's, was the
intensity of fogging of a photographic plate. This is qualitative,
nonlinear in exposure, and not chemically discriminating. The Curie
electrometer measured ionization current produced in air between two
parallel plates by the test substance, with the current balanced
against a piezoelectric current produced by squeezing a calibrated
quartz crystal with a known weight on a lever. The null point is
detected by a Lippmann capillary electrometer; the calibration is
absolute (in coulombs per second) within the precision of the
piezoelectric law and the lever weighing. The instrument made
ionization current a quantitatively measurable proxy for activity, and
did so with a sensitivity that could detect activity below one
microcurie equivalent.

It is the instrument that made the 1898 inference, *the activity of
pitchblende exceeds the activity of its uranium content*, *visible*.
Becquerel's photographic method could not have seen the discrepancy
because the response curve of a plate to dose is too nonlinear in the
relevant range. The instrument is half the story of how Marie's chosen
methodological switch (qualitative photography → quantitative
electrometry) opened the field. The other half is the choice itself,
which was Marie's.

## 6. The Curie partnership and the question of credit

The Curie collaboration ran from approximately 1897 (when Pierre
joined Marie's pitchblende work full-time, abandoning his own crystal
research) to Pierre's death in April 1906. The papers are signed
jointly. The historical question, who did what within the partnership
has a long answer and a short one.

The short one: there is no documentary evidence sufficient to allocate
credit between the two on most of the joint work. The notebooks are in
both hands, the *CR* notes are joint, and the partners themselves
described the work as joint. The compromise the field has settled on
is that Marie chose the topic, designed the radioactivity-survey
programme, did most of the chemical isolation work in the shed
(Pierre's interest was always more on the physics side and he was
already in declining health), and Pierre contributed the instrumental
side and the physical interpretation. Pierre Curie's own letters and
his Nobel lecture (1905) attributed primary credit on the
*radioactivity* programme to Marie, while reserving for himself the
*physical* analysis (the magnetic deflection experiments,
the energy-balance arguments).

The longer answer is that the "who deserves credit" question imports a
modern individualized authorship norm onto a partnership that did not
operate that way. The Curies' working norm was joint; the institutions
around them in 1898-1906, the Académie des sciences, the Nobel
committee, had to be argued into recognizing that joint work was
joint between both, against the instinct to credit Pierre alone with a wife assistant. The 1903 Nobel
nearly went to Becquerel and Pierre alone (see §11). The 1911 prize
was Marie's alone, after Pierre's death, and is the prize that
demonstrates without ambiguity that the work was hers as well as his.

Pierre's own canonical contributions outside the radioactivity work,
piezoelectricity (1880, with Jacques), Curie's law and the Curie
point in magnetism (1895 thesis), the design of precision instruments
belong on a separate card; he is a canon-tier figure in his own
right and will be filed in `02-physics/` in a later pass. This card is
about Marie's contributions.

## 7. The Sorbonne chair, 1906

The Faculté des sciences offered Marie Pierre's chair within weeks of
his death in April 1906. The procedure was unusual: chairs at the
Sorbonne were not conferred but won by competitive jury, and the
chair was explicitly Pierre's *physique générale* chair. The Faculty
council voted to offer it to Marie as a successor; the Ministry of
Public Instruction approved. She accepted on 13 May 1906.

The inaugural lecture on 5 November 1906 took place in a packed
amphithéâtre with photographers waiting outside. She walked to the
lectern, opened her notes, and began with the sentence Pierre had used
to close his last course: "When one considers the progress that has been
made in physics during the past ten years…", picking up the syllabus
where he had left it, without ceremony. Newspapers across Europe
reported the lecture; the New York Times, which had not previously
covered the death of a French physicist with much attention, ran the
inaugural on the front page under the headline "Mme. Curie Begins Her
Course of Lectures."

She held the chair until her death in 1934. The first woman to hold a
professorial chair at the Sorbonne since the institution's medieval
founding in the thirteenth century.

## 8. The War: the Petites Curies and the Women Radiographers

When the German army crossed into France in August 1914 and the French
government decamped to Bordeaux, Marie did three things in sequence.
First, she put the Institute's radium stock, about a gram, then the
single most valuable radium reserve outside of clinical institutions in
Europe, in a lead-lined box and personally accompanied it on the train
south to Bordeaux, where she deposited it in a bank vault and returned
the same day with an empty box. Second, she shut down most basic
research at the Institute. Third, she reorganized her energy around the
medical use of X-rays at the front, a field in which she had no prior
research interest and to which she devoted four years.

The medical justification was direct: the Western Front produced
hundreds of thousands of penetrating-shrapnel and bullet wounds whose
surgical management depended on locating fragments inside the body
without exploratory surgery. Pre-war radiology was almost entirely
hospital-based, equipment was static, and most field hospitals had no
radiological capacity. The need was for mobile X-ray, brought to within
ambulance-distance of the casualty.

Marie equipped roughly twenty mobile radiological vehicles (the *petites
Curies*), touring cars and small trucks fitted with an X-ray tube, a
generator powered off the car's engine via a dynamo, a darkroom curtain
arrangement, and a folding examination table, and oversaw construction
of approximately two hundred fixed radiological posts in field hospitals
of the *zone des armées*. She drove the first vehicle to the front
herself in late 1914, with Irène (then seventeen) as assistant, and made
several front-line rounds in 1914-1915, at one point operating within
artillery range at Furnes in Belgium during the First Battle of Ypres.

The harder problem was personnel. The French Army's medical service did
not have trained radiographers; civilian radiologists were either too
few or too senior to deploy. Marie addressed it by training women.
Approximately 150 women, recruited largely from the bourgeois Parisian
volunteer pool, were trained at the Institut du radium in a six-week
course (anatomy, X-ray physics, plate handling, generator maintenance,
emergency darkroom). Irène Curie became one of the first instructors at
seventeen and continued training through the war. The trained
manipulatrices were posted to the petites Curies and to the fixed
posts. Modern estimates of total radiological examinations under the
programme are approximately one million by November 1918. The mortality
attributable to the programme cannot be cleanly estimated against
counterfactual; the surgical-management literature treats the programme
as the founding case for forward radiology.

Marie also worked, in parallel, on the medical use of radon
("radium emanation") sealed in glass needles, an early application of
radium to brachytherapy. The 1921 book *La radiologie et la guerre*
(Alcan, Paris) is the published account.

She finished the war with no formal military decoration (the Légion
d'honneur, offered to her in 1910 and declined, was offered again and
again declined; she eventually accepted only honorary academic
memberships). The American press tour of 1921, organized by Marie
Mattingly Meloney, was in part a deliberate corrective to the French
state's failure to provide radium for the Institute's continued work,
and resulted in a gram of radium presented by President Harding.

## 9. The Radium Institute and the school

The Institut du radium opened in July 1914, just before the war began,
in two pavilions on the rue Pierre Curie (so renamed in 1906). The
Curie pavilion (physics and chemistry of radioactivity, directed by
Marie) and the Pasteur pavilion (medical and biological applications,
directed by Claudius Regaud) constituted Europe's first dedicated
radioactivity research institute combining basic and clinical work.

Through the 1920s and into the 1930s the Institute became the principal
European training school for radiochemists and nuclear physicists. The
roster, abridged, includes:

- **Irène Curie (Joliot-Curie), 1897-1956.** Marie's elder daughter.
  Joined the Institute as paid researcher 1918 (she had been an unpaid
  war assistant since 1914). PhD 1925 on the alpha radiation of
  polonium. Married Frédéric Joliot 1926. With Frédéric Joliot,
  discovered artificial (induced) radioactivity in 1934, by alpha
  bombardment of aluminium producing a new radioisotope of phosphorus,
  the result Marie saw confirmed in the laboratory two months before her
  death. Awarded Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1935 with Frédéric. Died of
  acute leukemia in 1956, at age 58, almost from cumulative
  occupational radiation exposure.

- **Frédéric Joliot, 1900-1958.** Joined the Institute 1925 as
  Marie's *préparateur*. Married Irène 1926; took the joint name
  Joliot-Curie. Co-Nobelist 1935. Director of the French nuclear
  programme from 1936; led the team that demonstrated the
  neutron-induced chain reaction in uranium in 1939; first French High
  Commissioner for Atomic Energy 1946. Died 1958 of liver disease,
  again with chronic radiation contribution probable.

- **André-Louis Debierne, 1874-1949.** Discovered actinium 1899 in
  pitchblende residues independently of Friedrich Otto Giesel; one of
  the earliest collaborators of the Curies; Marie's chief collaborator
  through 1906-1934 and her literary executor.

- **Marguerite Perey, 1909-1975.** Marie's last graduate student to
  defend. Discovered francium (Z = 87) in 1939, named for France.
  First woman elected to the Académie des sciences (correspondante
  1962, member 1979, after the institutional door Marie tried to open
  in 1911 finally yielded).

- **Salomon Rosenblum, 1896-1959.** Established the fine structure of
  alpha spectra (1929) at the Institute, a result that became
  important evidence for nuclear energy levels.

- **Bertrand Goldschmidt, 1912-2002.** Joined the Institute under
  Irène; later one of the chief French nuclear scientists, principal
  negotiator on the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, and historian
  of the early French programme.

- **Hans von Halban (1908-1964) and Lew Kowarski (1907-1979).** Members
  of Joliot's group at the Institute and the Collège de France in 1939
  whose work on slow-neutron-induced fission in uranium was the French
  precursor to the wartime British and American programmes; both moved
  to Britain in 1940 carrying the heavy water reserve.

The Institute's work continued during the German occupation (1940-44)
under conditions of considerable difficulty; Joliot stayed in Paris
and was active in the Resistance.

The Institute is today the Institut Curie, a major French oncology
research and treatment center; the original Curie pavilion is the
Musée Curie (curie.fr), a small museum and library in the original
building on what is now the rue Pierre et Marie Curie, holding the
laboratory equipment, the archives that did not go to the Bibliothèque
nationale, and the Curies' restored laboratory office.

## 10. What she got wrong

Three substantive scientific errors and one class of safety errors.

**(a) The atomic-property thesis vs. the disintegration theory,
1898, c.1908.** Through approximately 1903-1905 Marie defended the view
that radioactivity was a *property of atoms*, without committing to a
mechanism by which atoms could lose energy or transform, against the
emerging Rutherford-Soddy *disintegration theory* (Ernest Rutherford
and Frederick Soddy at McGill, 1902-1903) which proposed that
radioactivity *is* the spontaneous transmutation of one element into
another, with the alpha and beta particles as the products and a
characteristic exponential decay law. Soddy framed it bluntly: "Marie,
this is transmutation." Marie's reluctance had two roots. First, the
chemistry community of 1903 had recently finished a generation of work
establishing the *immutability of elements* against the speculative
"inorganic transmutation" claims of nineteenth-century natural
philosophy; the Rutherford-Soddy theory proposed exactly such
transmutation, by a mechanism that in 1903 had no nuclear model behind
it. Second, the disintegration theory proposed enormous internal atomic
energies (Rutherford and Soddy's 1903 estimate, broadly correct,
implied energy densities millions of times those of chemistry) without
saying where they came from. Marie's preferred formulation in the 1903
thesis treats radioactivity as a measurable atomic property and is
agnostic on mechanism; the 1910 *Traité* records her acceptance of the
Rutherford-Soddy framework, by which point Rutherford's 1908 alpha-ray
identification work and the early scattering data had made the
disintegration theory empirically inescapable.

This is not an error in any deep sense; it is appropriate caution in
the face of an underdetermined theory, and Marie's position changed
when the evidence justified it. But for several years (roughly 1903-07)
her published view lagged the best available account.

**(b) The atomic-mechanism picture, c.1898, c.1911.** Marie did not fully
grasp the Rutherford-1911 nuclear atom, the picture in which a small
positively charged nucleus contains all the mass and
disintegration is a *nuclear* phenomenon, until after the
Solvay Conference of 1911. The 1910 *Traité* uses the language of
"radioactive atoms" without a nuclear model. The Rutherford gold-foil
result of 1909-1911 closed the question, and Marie adopted the nuclear
atom in subsequent work without controversy. Again, this is lag rather
than error; the relevant point for the canon record is that the
nuclear picture belongs to Rutherford, ahead of Curie's.

**(c) The mass and atomic-weight estimates of polonium.** Polonium's
short half-life (Po-210, the principal isotope she had, has a
half-life of 138 days) made bulk isolation impossible by
the methods of 1898-1906. Marie's various atomic-weight estimates for
polonium were never close to the modern value of 209 because the sample
sizes were too small for chemical determination. This is acknowledged
in the 1910 *Traité*; the polonium chemistry was completed by later
workers using mass spectrometry on spallation-produced sources.

**(d) Safety practice.** The class of errors with the largest
downstream toll. Marie carried test tubes of radium chloride in the
pocket of her laboratory smock; she stored radium samples in her desk
drawer and on her bedside table (the famous "soft blue glow in the
darkened room" passage in Eve Curie's biography is not embellishment
radium-doped substances do glow, and Marie kept several near her
person); the laboratory used mouth pipettes for transferring
radium-bearing solutions; radium dust circulated freely in the
unventilated shed on rue Lhomond and the early Institute. Pierre Curie
deliberately produced a radium burn on his own arm in 1901 to study
the dose-response, and described the burn (which took months to heal)
in a 1901 *CR* note, among the founding observations of human
radiation injury, which Pierre and Marie did not extend into a
revision of laboratory practice. By 1925 the radium-dial industry was
producing the first identified case series of radium-induced bone
necrosis and bone cancer (the "radium girls" of New Jersey,
Massachusetts, and Illinois); the lessons were transferred slowly into
research-laboratory practice. Marie's own death, Irène's death, and a
documented cluster of Institute-trained radiochemists who died young
of leukemia or aplastic anemia constitute the founding empirical
record of chronic occupational radiation injury, the data set on
which radiation biology and radiation protection were eventually built.

Calling the safety lapses an "error" requires care: the harms were not
known in 1898, were known partially by 1910, and were institutionally
acknowledged only in the 1920s and 1930s. The assessment is
that Marie continued lax handling practices into the late 1920s, by
which time enough was known to have warranted change. The
contamination of the laboratory notebooks, today held under
restricted-access conditions in lead-lined boxes at the Bibliothèque
nationale de France, is the artefact that records the practice.

## 11. The 1903 Nobel: was it unfair to her?

The internal record of the 1903 Physics Nobel, declassified
in the 1990s when the Nobel archives opened the relevant files at
50 years after the event, supports a specific narrative that is more
more layered than either of the two popular versions.

**Version A (popular feminist reading):** the committee tried to give
the prize to Becquerel and Pierre alone, deliberately excluding Marie
because she was a woman; Marie had to fight for inclusion.

**Version B (popular hagiographic reading):** Pierre nobly insisted on
sharing with Marie when the Academy had honored him alone.

**What the archive shows.** The original 1903 nomination from the French
Academy of Sciences, submitted by four French members, named Becquerel
and Pierre Curie, with Marie absent from that nomination. The Nobel committee's deliberations
through the spring and summer of 1903 worked from this nomination.
Magnus Gösta Mittag-Leffler, the Swedish mathematician then on the
committee and a known advocate for women in academic positions
(his sponsorship of Sofya Kovalevskaya at Stockholm in
1883-91), wrote privately to Pierre warning him of the omission.
Pierre wrote to the committee on 6 August 1903, letter preserved in
the Nobel archive, stating in plain terms that any prize for the
radioactivity work that did not name Marie would be a
misrepresentation of the work, citing her as the originator of the
research programme. The committee revised; Henri Poincaré, asked for
a comment in September, replied that the fairer division was Becquerel
on one side and the Curies jointly on the other ("while the Curies
are more precise and advanced, Becquerel was the initiator"). The
final committee proposal of October 1903 used Poincaré's split. The
Royal Swedish Academy approved on 12 November 1903.

So: the original French Academy nomination was indeed Becquerel + Pierre
without Marie. That nomination was a real act of exclusion; whether
the motive was conscious sex bias or the simpler and equally-relevant
fact that French academic culture of 1903 did not extend
solo-nomination eligibility to women is a question on which
historians still disagree (Quinn 1995 reads it as the former; the
Curie-Joliot-Curie association's own 2003 retrospective is more
guarded). The committee in Stockholm received that nomination,
discovered the issue through Mittag-Leffler's intervention and
Pierre's letter, and corrected. The correction was Mittag-Leffler's
diplomacy plus Pierre's insistence; Marie did not appear in the
correspondence directly.

The reading is therefore: **Yes, she had to be argued in.** The
person who did the arguing was Pierre, on her behalf, with
Mittag-Leffler functioning as the inside ally. The committee corrected
when the facts were put to it. The original French nomination was,
read at face value, unfair. The 1903 prize as awarded was not, but
the 1903 prize as awarded required active correction of an unfair
default.

The 1911 Chemistry Nobel, by contrast, has no such complication: it was
proposed for Marie alone, on her own work after Pierre's death, by a
chemistry committee that had reserved the radium-chemistry credit to
itself in 1903 so as to be able to award it later. That
prize is unambiguous and is the one that establishes Marie's claim to
foundation-tier independent contribution in two sciences.

This card's editorial position, which the parent task asked for: the
1903 prize was *almost* unfair to her. It was made fair by Pierre's
intervention, which is honorable on Pierre's part but does not change
the fact that the default, what would have happened absent the
intervention, was unfair. Both popular readings are partly right
and partly wrong; the archive will not let either stand alone.

## 12. The 1911 Langevin affair and the December Nobel

The Langevin affair is documented enough that the basic chronology is
not in dispute. Paul Langevin (1872-1946) had been one of Pierre's
doctoral students at the École municipale and was, by 1910, professor
at the Collège de France and a major figure in French theoretical
physics, the early French Einsteinian, later author of work on
paramagnetism, relativistic dynamics, and the *Langevin equation* in
Brownian motion. He was married unhappily to Jeanne Desfosses; the
marriage was violent (Langevin appears to have been physically abused
by Jeanne, a direction the 1911 press left unreported), and the couple
had four children. By summer 1910 Marie
(widowed since 1906) and Langevin had begun a romantic relationship.
They rented an apartment together in Paris; Langevin separated from
Jeanne. Marie was forty-two and a widow; Langevin was thirty-eight and
married. Both were free agents in modern terms; in 1910 Paris the
relationship was within the bounds of bourgeois discretion expected
of an unmarried widow and a separated husband, conducted privately.

In autumn 1911 Jeanne Langevin obtained a packet of letters between
Marie and Paul, the means by which she obtained them is variously
described as a hired locksmith and as her brother-in-law; the
documentary chain is unclear, and supplied them to the journalist
Gustave Téry of *Le Journal* and *L'Œuvre*. *Le Journal* broke the
story on 4 November 1911 (the same week as the Solvay conference at
which Marie was the only woman present); *L'Œuvre* published excerpts
of the letters on 23 November. The press campaign was conducted in
xenophobic and antisemitic terms, with the standard turn-of-the-century
French anti-Dreyfusard right-wing register: the framing was that a
"foreign Jewish woman" (Marie was Polish and atheist, neither of those
things, but the trope was deployed) had wrecked a "French family." The
campaign was sustained for several weeks. Langevin fought a duel with
Téry on 25 November in the Parc des Princes; both men declined to fire
and the seconds called the duel.

In the same week the Royal Swedish Academy announced (on 7 November
1911) the Chemistry Nobel for Marie, alone, for the discovery of
polonium and radium and the isolation of radium. Svante Arrhenius,
the 1903 Chemistry Nobelist, then a senior member of the Academy,
wrote on 1 December 1911 to Marie urging her not to attend the
ceremony, on the explicit ground that the Academy could not have known
of the affair at the time of the award and that her attendance would
be inappropriate while the scandal was unresolved. Marie's reply on
5 December (preserved in the Nobel archive) is brief and direct: the
prize had been awarded for her scientific work; she saw no relation
between her scientific work and her private life; she would attend.

She traveled to Stockholm with her sister Bronisława and her elder
daughter Irène. She delivered the Nobel lecture on 11 December 1911 in
Swedish-translated French, a model of restrained scientific prose,
without political reference of any kind, presenting the radium and
polonium work as the joint work of herself and Pierre and naming
Pierre's contribution at the points where it was decisive. She accepted
the prize.

Within ten days the Langevin divorce settled out of court (Langevin
returned to Jeanne formally; the affair with Marie ended; Marie and
Langevin remained scientific friends and political allies). Marie
collapsed on her return to Paris and was hospitalized for several
months in 1912 with kidney disease and depression; she did not return
to full work until late 1913.

The specific significance of the December 1911 Nobel attendance for
this card is that it is the moment Marie stepped publicly through what
the press campaign had constructed to keep her out, and did so in plain
terms. The reply to Arrhenius is the single best-preserved piece of
her own writing on the question of women in public science, and the
position it asserts, that scientific work and private life are
separately judged, was not yet the consensus position in 1911 and
would not be for several decades.

It is also worth recording that Albert Einstein, then a young
correspondent of Marie's, wrote her in November 1911 a private letter
("if the rabble continues to be occupied with you, stop reading
that drivel… these reptiles can be ignored…") that has survived in the
Einstein archive at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and is a small
piece of evidence about how the affair looked to peers outside France.

## 13. Lineage, contemporaries, descent

**Teachers and immediate predecessors.**

- **Władysław Skłodowski (1832-1902),** her father, a Gymnasium
  mathematics and physics teacher in Warsaw, was the first source of
  her scientific training. The home library was where she did her
  preparatory reading.

- **Józef Boguski (1853-1933),** her cousin, was the chemist who ran
  the small museum laboratory on Krakowskie Przedmieście in Warsaw
  where Marie did her first hands-on chemistry in 1890-1891. Boguski
  had worked under Mendeleev in Saint Petersburg.

- **Gabriel Lippmann (1845-1921),** Sorbonne professor of physics, was
  Marie's PhD director at the Faculté des sciences in 1898-1903.
  Lippmann's own canon contribution (color photography by interference
  fringes; Nobel 1908) is unrelated to radioactivity, but his
  laboratory provided Marie's institutional base, and he sat on her
  thesis jury in 1903.

- **Henri Becquerel (1852-1908)** is the scientific predecessor in the
  strictest sense, the 1896 discovery of uranium radiation is the
  prior result Marie built on. Becquerel and Marie corresponded and
  collaborated indirectly through 1898-1903; Becquerel was on her
  thesis jury and shared the 1903 Physics Nobel with the Curies. He is
  the figure who made the radioactivity programme possible by giving
  it a starting observation.

**Spouse and partner.**

- **Pierre Curie (1859-1906)**, the partnership 1895-1906. Pierre is
  the foundation-tier figure separately filed (piezoelectricity 1880,
  Curie's law and Curie point 1895, the precision-instrument
  tradition); within the radioactivity work he is the instrument-builder
  and the physical interpreter, with Marie as the chemist and
  experimental programme director. The card on Pierre is a future pass.

**Contemporaries and rivals.**

- **Ernest Rutherford (1871-1937)**, the New Zealand, English
  experimental physicist who developed the disintegration theory at
  McGill (1902-07, with Soddy) and the nuclear atom at Manchester
  (1908-11). Rutherford and Marie were on respectful but not warm
  terms; she resisted the disintegration theory longer than he was
  patient with, and he spoke privately of her experimental work with
  great respect (he relied on Curie-purified radium for his alpha
  source) and her theoretical conservatism with less.

- **Frederick Soddy (1877-1956)**, Rutherford's partner on the
  disintegration theory; later coiner of the term *isotope* (1913).

- **Friedrich Otto Giesel (1852-1927)**, German chemist who
  independently discovered actinium and produced the first commercial
  radium preparations (Buchler, Braunschweig). The Curies' supply
  arrangements with Giesel were occasionally strained over priority on
  actinium (which Debierne also discovered, in the Curie laboratory,
  the same year).

- **William Ramsay (1852-1916)**, Scottish chemist, discovered the
  noble gases. Demonstrated, with Soddy in 1903, that helium is
  produced from radium decay, a result Marie's lab confirmed but did
  not publish first.

- **Pierre Curie's friend the physicist Albert Einstein (1879-1955)**
, the famous photograph of Marie at the 1927 Solvay conference
  alongside Einstein (front row), Bohr, Heisenberg, Schrödinger,
  Pauli, Dirac, and the others is the iconic image of her standing in
  the founding generation of quantum physics, though her own work was
  entirely on the experimental and chemical side and she did not
  contribute to quantum mechanics directly.

**Daughters and the second generation.**

- **Irène Joliot-Curie (1897-1956)**, the elder daughter. Joined the
  Institute as paid researcher 1918. With Frédéric Joliot, discovered
  artificial radioactivity 1934 (Nobel Chem 1935). Continued the
  Curie line; died of leukemia 1956 from the same occupational cause as
  her mother.

- **Eve Curie (1904-2007)**, the younger daughter. Pianist, journalist,
  and Marie's biographer (*Madame Curie*, Doubleday 1937; the
  authorized account, foundational and partly hagiographic; the only
  primary source for many family-life details). Married the American
  diplomat Henry Labouisse (UNICEF Director 1965-1979); the couple
  accepted, in 1965, the Nobel Peace Prize on UNICEF's behalf,
  bringing the family Nobel count to five (Marie ×2, Pierre ×1, Irène
  & Frédéric ×1, Henry on UNICEF's behalf ×1).

**Successors at the Institute** (see §9): Frédéric Joliot, Marguerite
Perey, Salomon Rosenblum, André-Louis Debierne, Bertrand Goldschmidt,
Hans von Halban, Lew Kowarski.

**Downstream by branch.**

- *In `02-physics/atomic-physics/`:* the entire Rutherford line and the
  early-twentieth-century radioactivity field (Rutherford, Soddy,
  Hahn, Meitner, Fermi). Curie's quantitative-electrometer programme
  is upstream of the standard radioactivity-measurement methods of the
  field through the 1930s.

- *In `03-chemistry/`:* radiochemistry as a sub-discipline (Hahn and
  Meitner in Berlin, Hevesy in Stockholm and Copenhagen, Seaborg in
  Berkeley); the procedural template of trace-element chemistry by
  fractional crystallization with activity-following assay.

- *In `05-biophysics/radiation-biology/`:* the entire field of human
  radiation biology and protection, the radium girls case series of
  the 1920s, the early ICRP recommendations of the 1930s, the
  Hiroshima, Nagasaki Life Span Study from 1950, and the
  occupational-cohort literature, has its founding empirical record
  in the cohort of radium chemists Marie trained and, eventually, in
  Marie's and Irène's own deaths.

## 14. Archives and Auditability

**Primary archives.**

- **Bibliothèque nationale de France (BnF), Paris,** holds the bulk of
  Marie's surviving laboratory notebooks (NAF, Nouvelles acquisitions
  françaises, series), her correspondence, and miscellaneous papers
  including the 1911 Arrhenius correspondence on Marie's side. The
  notebooks are stored in lead-lined boxes; consultation requires a
  signed liability waiver and protective equipment. The contamination
  is primarily Ra-226 (half-life 1,600 years) and will persist on the
  notebooks for approximately the next 1,500 years. (Source: BnF
  Department of Manuscripts; corroborated by *Christian Science Monitor*
  Nov 2011, Gizmodo 2014, and multiple library-conservation
  reports.)

- **Musée Curie, Paris (curie.fr),** the original Curie pavilion of the
  1914 Institut du radium on rue Pierre et Marie Curie, holds the
  reconstructed laboratory office, instruments (including a Curie
  electrometer), photographic archive, and a working library of
  family papers. Open to the public.

- **Archives of the Académie des sciences, Paris,** hold the 1903 and
  1911 nomination files (Comptes rendus des séances, fonds Curie),
  the 1911 Académie membership election records, and the *CR Acad.
  Sci.* notes themselves (now also online at gallica.bnf.fr).

- **Sorbonne archives, Paris,** hold the chair appointment record of
  1906 and the doctoral defense record of 1903.

- **Nobel Foundation Archive, Stockholm,** holds the 1903 and 1911
  internal committee correspondence including the Mittag-Leffler
  exchange, Pierre's 6 August 1903 letter, the Poincaré September 1903
  letter, and the December 1911 Arrhenius, Curie exchange.

- **American Institute of Physics, Center for History of Physics,
  College Park MD,** holds the *Marie Curie and the Science of
  Radioactivity* online exhibit (history.aip.org/exhibits/curie/), the
  AIP Niels Bohr Library oral histories of Institute alumni
  (Goldschmidt, Kowarski, others), and a curated bibliography.

- **Polish Academy of Sciences and Skłodowska-Curie Museum, Warsaw**
  (Freta 16, the family birth house), holds the Polish-side family
  papers and the 1929 American gram of radium (received by
  Bronisława's Warsaw Radium Institute, now part of the
  Marie Skłodowska-Curie National Research Institute of Oncology).

**Standard secondary literature.**

- **Eve Curie, *Madame Curie* (Doubleday 1937; English tr. Vincent
  Sheean 1937).** The authorized biography by Marie's younger
  daughter. Foundational, beautifully written, partly hagiographic;
  the primary source for many family-life and emotional details that
  are not otherwise documented. Treats the Langevin affair by omission.

- **Robert Reid, *Marie Curie* (Saturday Review Press / Dutton 1974).**
  The accessible standard biography for the post-war Anglophone
  reader; uses the AIP and Nobel sources, less revisionist than
  Quinn.

- **Susan Quinn, *Marie Curie: A Life* (Simon & Schuster 1995).** The
  modern critical biography; the first to use the Langevin
  correspondence in detail (the letters had been held privately by
  the Langevin family until the 1990s); the strongest source on the
  Académie des sciences vote and the 1911 press campaign. The
  bibliographic standard.

- **Barbara Goldsmith, *Obsessive Genius: The Inner World of Marie
  Curie* (Norton 2005).** Compact, literary, post-Quinn; emphasizes
  the radiation-injury narrative and the personal cost.

- **Naomi Pasachoff, *Marie Curie and the Science of Radioactivity*
  (Oxford 1996).** Accessible scientific biography; the version most
  used in undergraduate teaching.

- **Anna Hurwic, *Pierre Curie* (Hermann 1995; English tr. Lilian A.
  Yatabe 1998).** Pierre-focused; useful as the partner-perspective
  corrective to Marie-centered accounts.

- **Helena M. Pycior, "Reaping the Benefits of Collaboration While
  Avoiding Its Pitfalls: Marie Curie's Rise to Scientific
  Prominence," *Social Studies of Science* 23 (1993): 301-323.** The
  best short scholarly treatment of the credit-allocation question in
  the 1898-1906 partnership.

- **Lawrence Badash, *Radioactivity in America: Growth and Decay of a
  Science* (Johns Hopkins 1979).** The institutional history of
  radium and radioactivity in the US, including the 1921 and 1929
  Marie Curie tours.

- **Soraya Boudia, *Marie Curie et son laboratoire* (Éditions des
  Archives Contemporaines 2001).** The institutional history of the
  Institut du radium in French.

**Online primary sources.**

- *Comptes rendus de l'Académie des sciences,* Gallica
  (gallica.bnf.fr), free access to the 1898-1934 *CR* notes including
  the three 1898 discovery papers.

- The 1903 doctoral thesis *Recherches sur les substances radioactives*
  is available on Gallica.

- The 1910 *Traité de radioactivité* (both volumes) is on Gallica.

- The American Institute of Physics online exhibit
  (history.aip.org/exhibits/curie/) is the best single English-language
  reading point.

**How this card is auditable.** Every dated event in §2 is referenced
in either Quinn 1995, the AIP exhibit, or the *CR* archive. Quotations
from the 1911 Arrhenius, Curie exchange are from the Nobel Archive
holdings (cited in Quinn 1995 and in the NPR / *Krulwich Wonders*
reproduction of the correspondence). Vote counts on the 23 January
1911 Académie election (28 to 30, against Branly) are from the Académie
des sciences official record and corroborated by contemporary press
(*Le Temps*, *Le Figaro*) and by the Nobel Prize Twitter / X

4 July 1934, aplastic anemia) are from the death certificate
(reproduced in Quinn) and the Nobel Foundation memorial. The
radioactivity of the BnF notebooks and lead-box storage protocol is
documented in multiple library-conservation sources and reported in
*Christian Science Monitor* 7 November 2011 and elsewhere.
