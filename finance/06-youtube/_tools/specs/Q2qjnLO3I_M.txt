Teaches: Andrew Lo's MIT 15.401 session on binomial option pricing, where replication with stock and riskless bond gives a price independent of the up probability, then a start on risk and return.
Claim: In the one-period binomial model the stock moves up by u or down by d, and the riskless bond pays r times B in both states regardless of the stock. || you're going to get r times B no matter what
Claim: The replicating portfolio's weights (r minus d) and (u minus d) are non-negative and sum to one, so they act like probabilities even though they are not real-world probabilities. || it's more than just a simple weighted average
Claim: The option price needs no assumption about the true probability of an up move, because that view is already aggregated into today's stock price. || we don't need to make an assumption explicitly for what p is
Claim: The continuous-time version is a partial differential equation, and changing contract terms quickly makes models hard to solve. || it turns out that it's very hard to model
Claim: The long-run average excess equity return is on the order of 7 percent, and realized excess return in the bad year differs sharply, so statistical and realized returns need different techniques. || on the order of 7%
