Teaches: Jake Xia (ex-Morgan Stanley trader and chief risk officer) opens MIT 18.S096 with market structure, product types, who trades, three trader types, and the choices that define an investment strategy, plus a two-month stock-picking game.
Claim: Math serves three jobs in markets: pricing models for options, forwards and swaps, risk management, and strategy discovery. || So how is this all related to math
Claim: Position sizing, when to sell and when to cut losses are quantitative decisions, and the lecturer says to let math drive them over greed and fear. || risk management is actually a very quantitative process
Claim: Market participants fall into three trader types: hedgers such as corporates, market makers who earn the bid-offer spread and flatten quickly, and speculators. || So the trader types we can categorize into the three
Claim: Market makers aim to earn the spread and avoid holding risk. || Their objective is to earn that spread, but not to take risk
Claim: Lenders and borrowers pairing up is the driver of financial relationships; bonds carry credit risk because the issuer can default on interest or principal. || So bond has a credit risk, meaning that the issuer may not always pay back
Claim: Funds that found real secrets will not disclose them and require lifetime non-competes. || they will ask you to sign a lifetime non-compete
Claim: Finding deterministic arbitrage today is hard; the speaker says those opportunities were picked up long ago, and behavioral finance is the reason statistical edges persist. || Those $100 bills have definitely been picked up
Claim: Strategy choice axes: public versus private markets, passive versus active, systematic versus discretionary, short versus long horizon, value versus growth. || Also, passive versus active
Architecture: none (introductory lecture; class game scores a stock pick by (G - L) / (G + L) over daily gains G and losses L).
