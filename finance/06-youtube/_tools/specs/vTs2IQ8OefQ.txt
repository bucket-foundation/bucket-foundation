Teaches: John Geanakoplos opens Yale's Financial Theory course: efficient-markets theory versus Shiller's psychology and his own leverage-cycle theory of incomplete markets and collateral, ten standard-theory puzzles, and an in-class double auction.
Claim: Efficient-market theory says competing traders push all information into prices, so a stock price tells you whether a company is doing well. || don't have to take the trouble to read all their financial reports, just look at their stock price
Claim: Normal distributions make crashes like the 50 percent Dow drop essentially impossible, which Mandelbrot used to argue returns have fat tails. || You never get these gigantic outliers if things are normally distributed
Claim: His leverage-cycle theory says housing down payments fell steadily until the 2006 peak, then collateral tightened and prices collapsed; margins down meant prices up and tougher margins meant lower prices. || the margins kept going down and down and down
Claim: A 1998 margin call forced bond holdings to be sold at terrible prices even though the bonds were sound, which he lived through at his own firm. || we'll get terrible prices for the bonds and we'll be driven out of business
Claim: Mortgage index prices fell from 100 to 60 and then to 20 starting ten months before the stock market peaked, a warning visible in credit markets first. || ten months before the stock market starts to go down
Claim: Bet sizing puzzle: even with a positive expected profit, a bookie or bettor facing 200,000 dollars of exposure should size so repeated betting survives. || not betting two hundred or three hundred thousand the first night with your friends at even odds
Claim: The in-class double auction of 16 students produced five trades among the bottom five sellers and top five buyers in two minutes, which he used to show markets route goods to those who value them most. || The market solves a complicated problem, and gets information incredibly quickly
