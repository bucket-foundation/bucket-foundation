Teaches: Andrew Lo's MIT 15.401 session on futures and options: margining and daily settlement, cost-of-carry pricing for forwards and futures, hedging a 1 million dollar portfolio with S&P futures, and calls and puts as insurance.
Claim: A futures contract starts at zero net present value and is marked to market daily, with gains and losses paid through a margin account that removes the credit risk built into forwards. || forward contracts contain enormous amounts of credit risk
Claim: A crude oil contract covers 1,000 barrels and an initial margin of 4,050 dollars controls the full notional, which is why leverage is the chain saw of finance. || each contract is for 1,000 barrels
Claim: Forward and futures prices follow no-arbitrage cost of carry: spot times (1 + r) plus storage cost minus convenience yield; gold has almost no storage cost so futures equal spot times (1 + r). || the convenience yield
Claim: An S&P 500 futures contract has a 250 dollar multiplier, so four contracts mimic a 1 million dollar index position, and selling five contracts hedges part of a 5 million dollar portfolio faster and cheaper than selling stocks. || you need four of those contracts
Claim: A call payoff is max of S minus K and zero, a put gives the right to sell at K, and a put is an insurance contract on a stock whose premium is the price of the put. || A put option is nothing more than an insurance contract on the value of a stock
Claim: Insurance via puts is available but costly when implied volatility is high, so the question is whether it is worth the price. || The implied volatility is up by at least an order of magnitude
