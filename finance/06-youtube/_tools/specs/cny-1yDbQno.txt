Teaches: Andrew Lo's MIT 15.401 session on equities: residual claim and limited liability, primary versus secondary markets, ECNs, and the dividend discount model with its constant-growth form and the limits of estimating it.
Claim: Bondholders have first claim on assets but capped upside, while equity is the residual claim with limited liability, so the most an owner loses is what was put in. || All you are at risk of losing is what you put in
Claim: Limited liability, access to public markets and voting rights together allow capital formation at a scale otherwise impossible. || the secret to unlocking the power of the masses for development of innovation and capital formation
Claim: Stock value is the expected discounted stream of dividends, and a stock that never pays anything would be worth zero; a liquidating dividend counts as a payment. || if a company never, ever pays dividends, well, then, it should be worth 0
Claim: Risky cash flows need a different discount rate for each horizon, like a yield curve for risky cash flows, which is why equities are harder to value than bonds. || we may have a yield curve for risky cash flows
Claim: With constant dividends the price is D over r, and with constant growth g it is D over (r minus g), so small changes in expected g produce large price shocks and bubbles. || if there are rapid changes in what people expect g to be
Claim: Using D over P alone makes every stock look overvalued because growth g is missing; the next-period dividend is D0 times (1 plus g). || Why is it, if you just use D over P, every single stock looks like it's overvalued
Claim: Forecasting these cash flows is inherently inaccurate, since firms miss their own next-year numbers, and companies avoid dividend cuts because a cut is read as bad news. || when a company cuts dividends, that's considered bad news
