Teaches: Peter Kempthorne's MIT 18.S096 linear algebra lecture applied to finance: portfolios as vectors, profit and loss, shorting and zero-cost portfolios, Markov chains and stationary distributions, then a one-period two-state market where replication, no arbitrage and completeness are linear algebra statements.
Claim: A portfolio is a vector of share quantities, and the day's profit and loss is the change in portfolio value, with the long-short difference of two portfolios giving the net gain of one over the other. || it's really profit and loss
Claim: Shorting means selling first and buying back later with borrowed stock, so a zero-net-value portfolio is possible and an arbitrage portfolio is one with zero cost and no chance of a negative payoff. || You basically sell first, buy back later
Claim: Black, Scholes and Merton option pricing rests on arbitrage-free markets. || is all based upon arbitrage-free markets
Claim: A stationary Markov chain has a stationary distribution that is the eigenvector with eigenvalue 1, and the other eigenvalues shrink to zero as the transition matrix is raised to powers. || it's only this one eigenvector that doesn't get shrunk to 0
Claim: In a one-period two-state model, a contingent claim such as a call is replicated by units of bond and stock, solving two linear equations. || units of the stock, units of the bond
Claim: No arbitrage holds when a pricing measure exists, with initial prices equal to a discount factor times expected terminal values; completeness asks that any payoff be realizable. || Q star being a probability distribution
Claim: Eigendecomposition A = S Lambda S inverse makes powers cheap, which feeds state equations and Kalman filters. || very useful in defining state equations and Kalman filters
