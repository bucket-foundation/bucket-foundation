Teaches: Robert Shiller (Yale, 2008) on banking: goldsmith origins and fractional reserve, commercial banks versus savings and loans, relationship banking as the answer to adverse selection and moral hazard, rating agencies, Basel capital rules and a history of banking crises up to the subprime crisis.

Claims:
- Executive option grants are followed by an uncanny tendency for the stock price to rise, which research linked to backdating of grant dates. "there's an uncanny tendency for the stock price to go up after the company awards large quantities of options" [0:04:09]
- Fractional reserve banking began with goldsmiths lending out gold held in their safes and issuing notes payable to the bearer. "Why I don't lend some of it out" [0:13:15]
- US commercial banks held about 10.8 trillion dollars in assets in Q3 2007, far more than savings institutions, so commercial banks are the banks that matter by size. "it is overwhelmingly commercial banks that matter" [0:24:02]
- Banks exist to solve adverse selection, where lenders without local knowledge get stuck with the worst borrowers, and moral hazard, where a borrower with fixed debts has an incentive to take big risks. "The adverse selection problem" [0:27:13]
- Banks fund short-term deposits with long-term loans and rarely call loans on borrowers who behave, which lets depositors earn long-term rates. "the depositor is earning interest of a level that can only be made on long-term loans" [0:35:14]
- Rating agencies (Moody's, Poor's, Standard and Poor's) carry great authority, but issuers paying for ratings introduced a moral hazard. "a kind of moral hazard to creep into their organization" [0:43:09]
- Basel I (1988) set risk-based capital requirements using four credit risk categories, and the lecturer notes real bank risks are too complex for those classes, which Basel II tried to address. "They define four credit risk categories" [0:48:01]
- Structured investment vehicles issued asset-backed commercial paper to hold long-term subprime loans, acting like banks outside bank regulation. "They're operating like banks, but they're not regulated as banks" [0:59:40]
- Deregulation without supervision recurs in the savings and loan, Mexican and Asian crises, and bailout expectations feed risk taking. "the government will pay for it" [0:55:16]
