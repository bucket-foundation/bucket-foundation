# Ses 9: Forward and Futures Contracts I | MIT OpenCourseWare | i_pLF9J3QPE | 1.32 h

Teaches: Andrew Lo on forward contracts (obligation, zero value at inception, long and short, counterparty risk, illiquidity), why firms hedge or do not, and how futures fix those defects through standardization, daily mark to market, a clearing corporation and margin. Delivered during the October 2008 crisis.

Claims:
- Lo calls suspending mark-to-market accounting the worst idea of the crisis; a price needs two consenting parties, and a number nobody will trade at is not a price. "proposed to suspend mark to market accounting" [0:06:37]
- Hedging views differ: Homestake Mining does not hedge, American Barrick hedges for earnings stability, Battle Mountain hedges up to 25%; a Guay and Kothari sample of 413 large firms found 57% used derivatives in 1997. "took a random sample of 413 large" [0:20:45]
- A forward is a binding commitment to buy at a future date at a price fixed today; it has zero value at inception because both sides would otherwise refuse, so the price is set where PV is zero (his example: spot $100 oil, $110 six-month forward). "is 110 today's spot price is 100" [0:28:31]
- Forward prices carry market information about the future: a forward at $90 against spot $100 signals expected supply or weaker demand. "at $90 whereas today it's at 100" [0:36:07]
- Forwards are customized, trade over the counter, deliver physically and carry counterparty risk; the worked soybean case has spot falling from $165 to $100, and a $5 per ton deposit is too small to stop the buyer from walking away. "make sure they can get that 100" [0:48:17]
- A clearing corporation sits between all parties. "metric tons divided by the appropriate discount" [1:02:43]
- Most futures are cash settled. "check the box that says cash settled" [1:18:38]
