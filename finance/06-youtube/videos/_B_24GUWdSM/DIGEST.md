# 4. Portfolio Diversification and Supporting Financial Institutions | YaleCourses | _B_24GUWdSM | 1.30 h

Teaches: The equity premium puzzle and Markowitz portfolio theory, built up from one risky asset plus borrowing, to two and three risky assets, the efficient frontier, the tangency portfolio, the mutual fund theorem, CAPM, beta and the Sharpe ratio. The VOC (1602) opens the lecture as the first listed company.

Claims:
- Siegel: US stocks earned a 6.8% a year geometric real return from 1871 to 2006 against 2.8% on short government debt, a premium of about 4% a year; Dimson, Marsh and Staunton found a premium in every country, from 3% (Belgium) to 6% (Sweden). "from 1871 to 2006 was 6 8%" [0:18:52]
- Borrowing example: a riskless rate of 5%, an asset with mean 20% and standard deviation 40%; borrowing 100 to hold 200 gives 35% expected return and 80% standard deviation; shorting 200 gives minus 25%. "is 35 and down here is 80" [0:35:33]
- Markowitz (1952): there is no best investment, only a trade-off between risk and return; the first ngram search shows "portfolio analysis" hardly existed before 1952. "before 1952 i guess it didn't exist" [0:28:14]
- With stocks and 10-year Treasuries (1983 to 2006 data), 100% bonds is dominated: a mix gives higher return at equal risk; adding oil moves the frontier left because oil correlates little with stocks. "bonds maybe you could be 100% stock" [0:49:09]
- Norway example: about 70% of its portfolio in oil; he suggested around 15% oil, 53% stocks and 32% bonds, or 21% oil and 79% stocks, with futures as the way to cut oil exposure. "reasonable 15% oil 53% stocks 32% bonds" [0:56:37]
- With a riskless asset, the best line is tangent to the frontier; every investor holds the tangency portfolio plus borrowing or lending, so the market portfolio equals the tangency portfolio. "tangency portfolio because everyone wants to invest" [1:05:05]
- CAPM: expected return equals the risk-free rate plus beta times the market premium; risk is covariance with the market.
