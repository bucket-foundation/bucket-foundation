Teaches: Flirting with Models interview with Jeff Rosenberg on the history and practice of systematic fixed income: credit option-adjusted spread models, why factor-language does not map onto bonds, engineered outcomes, implementation frictions and index effects.

Claims:
- Quant credit models at First Boston relied more on market pricing than on fundamental analysts' accounting-based views, and they flagged WorldCom and Enron early. "we had some real good early successes around WorldCom, Enron" [0:21:23]
- Fixed income history was driven by sell-side innovators such as Litterman at Goldman and the First Boston mortgage group, and post-2008 Dodd-Frank limits on proprietary risk equalized the buy side. "the sellside was the driver of the innovations" [0:28:05]
- Equity factor literature is cross-sectional, while in fixed income the macro factors of growth, inflation and real rates are simply interest rate risk, so the signal taxonomy follows the instrument. "that's called interest rate risk" [0:35:36]
- Signals for global rates are growth, inflation, liquidity, terms of trade, currency and monetary policy differentials, but a securitization transaction needs different signals. "What's going to drive global interest rates" [0:42:08]
- A systematic process is model determined, so outcomes can be engineered by optimizing the alpha forecast under constraints rather than just ranking and picking the top alphas. "It's about engineering outcomes" [0:49:15]
- In bonds, implementation is harder than in equities, so more people work on portfolio management than alpha research, and research must prove the bonds were available and liquid enough to trade at scale.
