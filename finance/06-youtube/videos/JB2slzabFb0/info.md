In this episode, we're joined by Ben Marshall, Professor of Finance and MSA Charitable Trust Chair in Finance at the Massey University Business School, for a wide-ranging conversation about estimating expected equity market returns, the cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio, and the permanent portfolio. Ben's work asks a question that sits underneath almost every asset allocation decision: if valuations tell us something real about the next ten years of returns, are we measuring valuations correctly in the first place?

We explore why historical averages make a poor forecast over a ten-year horizon, how survey-based and data-driven estimates differ, and why the valuation component does most of the work in a three-component model of yield, growth, and valuation. Ben then walks through the problem at the centre of his recent research: the traditional CAPE ratio weights stocks by their earnings while the index it forecasts is weighted by market value, and the earnings in its denominator come from companies that may no longer be in the index. We examine what happens when that mismatch is repaired, why the component CAPE reads today's US market as expensive but less expensive than the traditional measure does, and how both stand up to the frameworks that have defeated most return predictors. We finish with Ben's work on Harry Browne's permanent portfolio, an out-of-sample test of it since 2012, and what long-term investors should make of the Sharpe ratio.
