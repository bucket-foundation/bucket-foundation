# 13. Banks | YaleCourses | 1mDL1fKEVZM | 1.22 h

Teaches: Robert Shiller's Yale lecture on traditional deposit-taking banks: their origins (Sumerian interest, goldsmith bankers, Monte dei Paschi), the Diamond-Dybvig liquidity theory, deposit insurance, Basel I to III risk-weighted capital rules, and bank crises in Mexico, Asia and Argentina. Investment banks, central banks and shadow banking are deferred to other lectures.

Claims:
- Banks offer liquidity by borrowing short and lending long, which differs from spread income, where the borrowing rate is lower than the lending rate. "banks offer liquidity by borrowing short and lending long" [0:08:52]
- Diamond and Dybvig (Journal of Political Economy, 1988) model banks as liquidity providers with a good and a bad equilibrium that depends on expectations; this is the rationale for deposit insurance. "in the journal of political economy 1988" [0:24:32]
- Banks solve adverse selection with local loan officers who know the community, and moral hazard with constant monitoring and short-term loans that are renewed and can be cut off. "practice officially short term they keep renewing" [0:32:29]
- The FDIC (1933) has never failed; the FSLIC went bankrupt in the 1980s savings and loan crisis and the government paid a $150 billion tab. "and the total tab was $150 billion" [0:36:27]
- Northern Rock (2007): insurance was 90% up to GBP 75,000, and the run came from people with more than GBP 3,000 in the bank. "it was people who had more than gbp 3 000 in the bank" [0:39:12]
- Basel risk weights: 0% for OECD government bonds, 20% for municipal bonds and Fannie and Freddie, 50% for home mortgages, 100% for loans and everything else. "0% weight the 20% weight the 50%" [0:48:05]
- Basel III common equity is 4.5% of risk-weighted assets plus a 2.5% conservation buffer (7% in practice), plus an optional 2.5% countercyclical buffer (9.5%); a bank with $400 million of assets has $170 million of risk-weighted assets and needs $11.9 million. "9 5% of your risk weighted assets" [0:56:07]
- With $1 million of spare capital a bank could buy about $70 million of Fannie and Freddie bonds (0.2 weight) or make only about $14 million of business loans (1.0 weight), which pushed banks toward subprime-linked paper. "buy $70 million of fannie and freddie" [1:00:06]


Worked:
- Deposit insurance backed by a government that stands behind it has prevented big US runs since 1933; Shiller says the belief itself keeps the system in the good equilibrium. "big back run since 1933 it seemed" [0:34:42]
- Tightening capital in good times through the countercyclical buffer, since tightening during a crisis would stop lending and crash the economy. "to tighten up when times are good" [0:56:00]
- Convergent rules across countries, so strict regulation in one country does not push business elsewhere. "coherence across countries if one country regulates" [0:42:53]

Failed:
- State deposit insurance schemes before the FDIC mostly failed; the FSLIC ran out of reserves. "created deposit insurance schemes before the fdic" [0:34:04]
- Basel weights gave Fannie and Freddie a 20% weight on the view that the US government backed them; Basel II recommendations date from 2004. "capital then next up is 20% weight" [0:49:18]
- Mexico privatized banks without regulators; lending went from 10% to 40% of GDP from 1988 to 1994 and the system collapsed. "in 1988 lending was 10% of gdp" [1:04:26]
- Lehman and Bear Stearns were shadow banking, doing bank-like business without regulation, and regulators cannot keep up with innovation. "for example lehman brothers or bear stearns" [1:11:05]
