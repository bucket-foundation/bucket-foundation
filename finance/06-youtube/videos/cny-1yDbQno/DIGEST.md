# Ses 8: Equities | MIT OpenCourseWare | cny-1yDbQno | 1.26 h

Teaches: Andrew Lo's MIT 15.401 session on equities: residual claim and limited liability, primary versus secondary markets, ECNs, and the dividend discount model with its constant-growth form and the limits of estimating it.

Claims:
- Bondholders have first claim on assets but capped upside, while equity is the residual claim with limited liability, so the most an owner loses is what was put in. "All you are at risk of losing is what you put in" [0:05:14]
- Limited liability, access to public markets and voting rights together allow capital formation at a scale otherwise impossible. "the secret to unlocking the power of the masses for development of innovation and capital formation" [0:09:12]
- Stock value is the expected discounted stream of dividends, and a stock that never pays anything would be worth zero; a liquidating dividend counts as a payment. "if a company never, ever pays dividends, well, then, it should be worth 0" [0:19:09]
- Risky cash flows need a different discount rate for each horizon, like a yield curve for risky cash flows, which is why equities are harder to value than bonds. "we may have a yield curve for risky cash flows" [0:26:14]
- With constant dividends the price is D over r, and with constant growth g it is D over (r minus g), so small changes in expected g produce large price shocks and bubbles. "if there are rapid changes in what people expect g to be" [0:51:07]
- Using D over P alone makes every stock look overvalued because growth g is missing; the next-period dividend is D0 times (1 plus g). "Why is it, if you just use D over P, every single stock looks like it's overvalued" [1:02:12]
- Forecasting these cash flows is inherently inaccurate, since firms miss their own next-year numbers, and companies avoid dividend cuts because a cut is read as bad news. "when a company cuts dividends, that's considered bad news" [1:12:16]
