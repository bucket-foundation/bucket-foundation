## Deep Read

Source: open-access NBER Working Paper 15552, December 2009, the version published in the Journal of Financial Economics 97(3), 2010. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

During the 2008 credit crisis, did firms that could not borrow plan deeper cuts to investment, hiring and payouts than firms that could, and did constraints make them skip profitable projects?

### Data and Sample

A survey of 1,050 CFOs in 39 countries in December 2008, run with CFO magazine and Duke University: 574 in the US, 192 in Europe and 284 in Asia. In the US fourth-quarter survey, 244 firms say they are not affected by credit market conditions, 210 somewhat affected and 115 badly affected. Table 2 compares public respondents with Compustat firms; 73 percent of non-financial public respondents have investment-grade ratings.

### Method

Firms are classed as constrained when the CFO reports being somewhat or badly affected by the cost or availability of credit. Table 4 checks what CFOs mean by constraint: 81 percent of badly affected firms report less access to credit. Planned annual percentage changes in technology spending, capital spending, marketing, employment, cash holdings and dividends are compared between constrained and unconstrained firms with matching estimators (Abadie-Imbens and Dehejia-Wahba), matched on size, ownership and credit rating, for the pre-crisis year and the crisis quarter. Table 7 compares reasons for drawing on credit lines. Separate questions ask whether constraints made the firm bypass attractive projects.

### Main Results

Table 5, crisis period, constrained minus unconstrained, Abadie-Imbens estimates: technology spending minus 11.2 points (t = minus 3.09), capital spending minus 8.5 (t = minus 3.79), marketing minus 11.7, employment minus 8.4 (t = minus 4.18), cash holdings minus 8.5 and planned dividends minus 28.4 (t = minus 2.09). Pre-crisis gaps were about half as large.

Table 7: 16.5 percent of constrained firms drew down credit lines in December 2008 for fear their bank would cut access, against 5.9 percent of unconstrained firms.

86 percent of constrained US CFOs say constraints restricted investment in attractive projects, and more than half say they cancelled or postponed planned investment. Constrained firms also sold more assets to raise funds. Results hold in Europe and Asia (Table 6).

### Robustness

Two matching estimators, pre-crisis and crisis comparisons, the three regions, and a comparison of survey answers with "normal times" archival constraint measures (Table 8).

### Stated Limits

Surveys cannot be replicated, responses may suffer selection bias, and answers record plans; actual outcomes may differ. Self-reported constraint is subjective, which the authors address by checking it against observables.

### Retail Systematic Trader

In a credit crunch, firms that depend on external finance cut spending, hiring and dividends far more than firms with access to credit. A rules-based book can tilt away from small, unrated, cash-poor or bank-dependent firms when credit spreads blow out, and expect dividend cuts concentrated in those names. Credit line drawdowns reported in filings during stress mark firms the market should treat as constrained.
