## Deep Read

Source: open-access NBER Working Paper 13565, October 2007, the version published in the Journal of Financial Economics 2011. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

Does financial literacy cause households to hold stocks, or does stock ownership teach literacy?

### Data and Sample

Two modules the authors designed for the DNB Household Survey, a panel representative of the Dutch population with over 2,000 households. 1,508 respondents completed the first module in 2005 and 1,373 the follow-up in 2006. The regression sample has 1,115 observations after merging wealth and income. Participation covers direct stocks and stock mutual funds.

### Method

Two literacy indices built by factor analysis: basic (numeracy, compound interest, inflation, time value of money, money illusion) and advanced (stocks against bonds, bond prices and rates, diversification, mutual funds). To test for guessing, two random groups saw the same questions with inverted wording (Table 3). OLS of stock participation on the indices plus age, education, gender, income and wealth quartiles. GMM uses the respondent's exposure to economics education in school as instruments for advanced literacy, controlling for basic literacy as a proxy for cognition. Extensions in Tables 9A to 9F add peer effects, self-assessed literacy, alternative indices and risk aversion.

### Main Results

Table 2B: only 5 percent get every advanced question right. More than 37 percent get the bond price and interest rate question wrong and another 37.5 percent say they do not know.

Table 5: respondents with low basic literacy rely more on family and friends for financial advice.

Table 7: the advanced literacy index enters at 0.084 in OLS (standard error 0.012) and at 0.163 in GMM (0.069). A one standard deviation rise in advanced literacy raises the probability of owning stocks by more than 8 percentage points in OLS. Basic literacy is insignificant once advanced literacy is in. Income and the third wealth quartile also enter. First-stage F statistics are close to 20, the exogeneity test is not rejected, and the Hansen J test does not reject the overidentifying restrictions.

### Robustness

Respondents over 35 (Table 9A), controls for peer effects (9B), self-assessed literacy (9C), alternative literacy measures (9D, 9E) and stated risk aversion (9F) leave the literacy coefficient positive and significant. The inverted-wording test shows answers shift with wording, a sign of guessing among the least literate.

### Stated Limits

Literacy is measured with error and the effect of guessing cannot be removed entirely. The sample shrinks when risk aversion data are added. The authors leave retirement planning and saving behavior to future work.

### Retail Systematic Trader

The paper describes the investor base more than it offers a signal. Low literacy keeps many households out of equities and makes those who participate rely on informal advice, which feeds the attention-driven and lottery-seeking flows other papers in this set document. A retail systematic trader is on the other side of those flows; the practical rule is to know the bond price and rate relation, diversification and fund costs before adding complexity.
