## Deep Read

Source: open-access MPRA Paper 14081, the November 1998 version of the paper published in the American Economic Review 89(3), 1999. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

How are banking crises and currency crises linked, which comes first, and which macro indicators signal them ahead of time?

### Data and Sample

20 countries from 1970 to mid-1995, monthly data: Argentina, Bolivia, Brazil, Chile, Colombia, Denmark, Finland, Indonesia, Israel, Malaysia, Mexico, Norway, Peru, the Philippines, Spain, Sweden, Thailand, Turkey, Uruguay and Venezuela. 76 currency crises and 26 banking crises. Four Asian crises of 1997 serve as an out-of-sample check. Sixteen indicators grouped into financial liberalization, other financial, current account, capital account, real and fiscal sectors.

### Method

Currency crises are dated by an index of exchange rate and reserve changes; banking crises by bank runs, closures, mergers or large government rescues. The authors compute unconditional and conditional crisis probabilities (Table 3). They then apply the "signals" approach: an indicator signals when it crosses a percentile threshold chosen to minimize the noise-to-signal ratio. A signal within 24 months before a currency crisis, or within 12 months before or after the start of a banking crisis, counts as good; others are false alarms. Table 7 counts the share of indicators signaling in the 24 months before each crisis.

### Main Results

Table 1: currency crises ran at 2.60 a year in the 1970s and 3.13 a year after; banking crises numbered only 3 in the 1970s and more than quadrupled per year afterward. Twin crises are a feature of the 1980s and 1990s.

Table 3: the unconditional probability of a currency crisis within 24 months is 29 percent; conditional on the start of a banking crisis it is 46 percent. Banking problems tend to precede currency crises, and the currency crash deepens the banking crisis. In 18 of the 26 banking crises the financial sector had been liberalized in the prior five years.

Table 6: capital account indicators called about 81 percent of currency crises, financial liberalization indicators 74 percent, current account indicators 68 percent, the fiscal deficit just over a quarter. The real sector called 85 percent of banking crises; output called 89 percent and stock prices 81 percent. For 13 of 16 indicators the share of crises called is higher for twin crises than for currency crises alone.

Table 7: in about 30 percent of currency crises, 80 percent or more of the indicators were signaling in the prior 24 months. Crises follow a boom fed by credit and capital inflows, with an overvalued currency, as the economy enters recession.

### Robustness

Results are reported crisis by crisis in Appendix Tables 1 and 2, before and after liberalization, and for twin against single crises. The Asian crises of 1997 are tracked out of sample.

### Stated Limits

Bank crisis dates rest on events and are imprecise, since data on bank asset quality are scarce. Indicator coverage varies by country, leaving NA entries. Thresholds are chosen in sample.

### Retail Systematic Trader

The paper gives an early-warning checklist for emerging market risk: fast domestic credit growth after liberalization, falling reserves against M2, real exchange rate overvaluation, slowing output and falling local equity prices. A retail book holding emerging market equities, local bonds or carry trades can cut exposure when several indicators signal at once. Signals lead by up to 24 months and carry many false alarms, so they suit position sizing better than market timing.
