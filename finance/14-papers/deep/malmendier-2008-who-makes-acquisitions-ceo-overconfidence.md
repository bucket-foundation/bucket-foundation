## Deep Read

Source: open-access NBER Working Paper 10813, September 2004, the version published in the Journal of Financial Economics 89(1), 2008. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

Do overconfident CEOs make more acquisitions, value-destroying ones among them, and how does the market react to their bids?

### Data and Sample

477 large US firms that appear on Forbes 500 lists from 1980 to 1994, with CEO stock and option holdings from the data used by Hall and Liebman (1998) and Yermack (1995). Merger bids from CRSP and SDC. Press coverage of CEOs in major business publications for an alternative measure.

### Method

A simple model: an overconfident CEO overestimates the returns of the firm and of the merger, so he makes more value-destroying deals when he has internal funds, and fewer when he must raise equity he sees as underpriced. Overconfidence is "Longholder": the CEO held an option to its final year at least once, despite being under-diversified. Logits of whether the firm completes a merger in a year on Longholder, with controls for size, Tobin's Q, cash flow, board size, CEO stock ownership and vested options, plus firm and year effects. Mergers are split into diversifying and same-industry deals (Table 8) and by Kaplan-Zingales equity dependence (Table 9). A press-based measure, TOTALconfident, counts articles calling the CEO confident or optimistic against cautious or reliable. Table 14 regresses three-day announcement abnormal returns on Longholder.

### Main Results

Table 4: the odds ratio on Longholder is 1.68; the odds of a merger in a year are about 0.095 for a rational CEO and 0.159 for an overconfident one. Controls leave the effect in place.

Alternative definitions give odds ratios of 1.81 (CEOs who always hold to expiration), 1.57 and 1.69.

Table 8: the effect comes from diversifying mergers; same-industry deals show no difference. Table 9: overconfidence raises merger odds among the least equity-dependent firms and has no effect among the most dependent.

Table 13: TOTALconfident predicts diversifying deals with an odds ratio of 1.78, significant at 1 percent, and no effect on same-industry deals.

Table 14: announcement returns for Longholder bids are 61 to 99 basis points lower, t between 1.73 and 2.33 across specifications; the conclusion calls it roughly 100 basis points.

Table 6: CEOs who held to expiration would have earned a positive but insignificant return by exercising a year earlier and investing in the S&P 500, and the merger effect comes from CEOs who lost money by holding, which argues against inside information.

### Robustness

Random effects and fixed effects logits, alternative cutoffs for how far in the money options must be (Table 11), controls for past stock returns (Table 7), and the press-based measure.

### Stated Limits

The authors treat late exercise as a revealed belief; risk tolerance, tax motives or board pressure could also explain it, and the paper tests several of these. The sample is large firms in 1980 to 1994.

### Retail Systematic Trader

Acquirers led by CEOs who hoard deep in-the-money options and draw "confident" press coverage earn about 1 percent lower announcement returns, mostly on diversifying deals in cash-rich firms. A retail screen can use public Form 4 option exercise data to flag such CEOs and avoid or underweight their firms around diversifying acquisitions. The effect per deal is small next to retail trading costs on announcement day, so it suits an exclusion filter better than a standalone trade.
