## Deep Read

Source: open-access NBER Working Paper 26549, December 2019, revised June 2020, the version published in the Journal of Financial Economics 142(2), 2021. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

In an equilibrium where some investors like holding green firms and customers may prefer green products, what are expected returns on green and brown stocks, what portfolios do investors hold, and does sustainable investing change real outcomes?

### Data and Sample

None. A one-period equilibrium model with N stocks; the empirical implications are left for testing.

### Method

Investors have exponential utility over wealth plus a nonpecuniary benefit from holding stocks with ESG characteristic g, scaled by each investor's ESG taste delta(i). Firms with positive g impose positive externalities. Section 2 solves for expected returns, portfolio weights and the ESG industry's size; Section 3 adds shocks to customer and investor ESG tastes; Section 4 adds climate risk that green stocks hedge; Section 5 derives social impact through firm decisions on greenness and real investment.

### Main Results

Proposition 1, equation (9): expected excess returns are mu = mu(m) beta(m) minus (average ESG taste over risk aversion) times g. Green stocks have negative CAPM alphas and brown stocks positive ones, larger when risk aversion is low and average ESG taste is strong.

Proposition 3, equation (14): three-fund separation. Each investor holds the riskless asset, the market and an ESG portfolio with weights proportional to Sigma inverse times g. Investors with above-average ESG taste tilt green and earn lower expected returns, but give up less return than they would be willing to. The ESG industry is largest when tastes differ most.

Propositions 5 and 6: realized returns load on the market and an ESG factor that captures surprise changes in customer or investor ESG concerns. When those concerns strengthen unexpectedly, green stocks outperform brown ones despite lower expected returns. A simple ESG factor is a green-minus-brown portfolio weighted by ESG scores.

Propositions 7 and 8: with climate risk, green stocks also hedge it, lowering their expected returns further; four-fund separation adds a climate-hedging portfolio.

Proposition 9: sustainable investing has positive social impact by lowering green firms' cost of capital, pushing firms to become greener and shifting real investment toward green firms.

### Robustness

Results hold when agents disagree about firms' ESG scores (Section 2.3) and under the climate risk extension.

### Stated Limits

The model is static with exogenous ESG characteristics in the asset pricing sections and constant absolute risk aversion. The authors present implications for testing; they do not estimate them.

### Retail Systematic Trader

The model says green stocks should earn lower expected returns over time, while recent green outperformance can come from unexpected shifts toward ESG tastes, a one-off repricing. A retail trader should not extrapolate past green-minus-brown returns. Brown stocks carry a premium for being unwanted and for climate risk exposure; a value-oriented book may hold them and accept that risk. An ESG tilt chosen for values costs some expected return, less than many holders would accept.
