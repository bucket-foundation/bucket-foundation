### Research Question

What do psychological findings on judgment and decisions imply for asset prices, what evidence of mispricing exists, and how can psychology-based pricing theories be told apart from rational risk theories?

### Data and Sample

A survey of theory and empirical work up to 2000, with no new data.

### Method

Section I sorts judgment and decision biases: heuristic simplification (attention and ease of processing, narrow framing and mental accounting, representativeness, belief updating that combines conservatism and overreaction), self-deception (overconfidence and biased self-attribution), emotions and self-control (ambiguity aversion, mood, time preference), and social interaction. Section II reviews evidence: return predictability from factor risk measures, price-to-fundamental ratios, past returns (momentum and reversal), public versus private news, and mood proxies; the equity premium and risk-free rate puzzles; corporate actions that respond to or create misvaluation; and investor behavior. Section III reviews static and dynamic pricing theories: noise trading, positive feedback trading, mistaken beliefs (Barberis-Shleifer-Vishny, Daniel-Hirshleifer-Subrahmanyam, Hong-Stein), alternative preferences, and evolving populations, and proposes tests to separate them for size and value, post-event drift, and momentum and reversal. ### Main Results

The survey's main claim: biases are systematic, so they need not cancel across investors, and limits to arbitrage let them reach prices. Momentum, long-run reversal, post-earnings drift, value and size effects, and post-event drift after issues and repurchases are consistent with models of overconfidence, conservatism and limited attention.

Section II.1.5: mood proxies predict returns, including morning sunshine in the city of a country's main exchange (Hirshleifer and Shumway; Saunders 1993) and sleep disruption around daylight saving changes.

Section III.3: the author proposes tests that separate rational and psychological explanations, such as whether return predictability is stronger where information is vaguer, arbitrage costlier or investor attention lower.

### Stated Limits

The conclusion calls psychology-based asset pricing an early-stage field. Few models allow both risk aversion and many risky securities; translating lab findings into assumptions about real investors is hard; there is no worked set of capital budgeting and risk procedures that account for mispricing; and the field lacks a model of how ideas about securities spread socially.

### Retail Systematic Trader

The survey maps biases to the anomalies a retail systematic book trades: momentum (underreaction and self-attribution), reversal and value (overreaction), post-event drift (limited attention). Its testable lesson is to expect these effects to be strongest where information is vague, attention is scarce and arbitrage is costly, which is also where trading costs are highest. A trader can use that as a prior when choosing universes and setting cost assumptions.
