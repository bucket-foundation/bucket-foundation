## Deep Read

Source: open-access NBER Working Paper 1079, February 1983, the version published in the Journal of Monetary Economics 12(1), 1983. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

Why does a central bank with discretion end up with higher inflation than a rule would give, and can reputation in a repeated game replace a formal rule?

### Data and Sample

None. A theory paper prepared for the 1982 Rochester conference on alternative monetary standards.

### Method

Each period the policymaker picks inflation pi to minimize a cost z = (a/2)pi^2 minus b times the inflation surprise (pi minus expected pi), equation (1), where b > 0 is the benefit of surprise inflation from distortions such as taxes and unemployment insurance, and a measures the cost of inflation. Costs are discounted at rate r, equation (2). Private agents form rational expectations. The authors compare three regimes: a binding rule, discretion, and cheating on a rule that agents believe. They then build a reputational equilibrium in which agents punish one period of cheating by expecting discretionary inflation the next period, a trigger strategy borrowed from oligopoly models.

### Main Results

Under discretion, the policymaker sets pi = b/a, agents expect it, and no surprise occurs. Expected cost is (1/2)b^2/a, equation (5). Under the ideal rule pi = 0 and cost is zero. Cheating on a believed rule gives cost minus (1/2)b^2/a, so the temptation to renege equals (1/2)b^2/a, equation (10). The ranking from low cost to high is cheating, rule, discretion.

With a one-period punishment, the enforcement is the discounted cost of discretion next period. The ideal rule pi = 0 is not enforceable since its temptation exceeds the enforcement. The best enforceable rule, equation (17), is pi* = (b/a)(1 minus q)/(1 plus q), where q is the mean discount factor. Inflation is a weighted average of the ideal rule and discretion. A high discount rate, a small q, pushes inflation toward the discretionary level; q of zero leaves only discretion credible.

Section V extends the model to random shocks to b and to the discount factor, with the same weighted-average form.

### Robustness

The authors show the result holds with stochastic b and r and note it does not collapse to discretion with a known finite horizon under some conditions.

### Stated Limits

The punishment rule is assumed, and the size and length of punishment do not depend on the size of the violation. Many equilibria exist in the repeated game; the authors pick one. The paper does not model how institutions select among them.

### Retail Systematic Trader

No trading signal. The model gives the logic behind inflation expectations and central bank credibility: when policymakers face short horizons or high temptation, expected inflation and its risk premium rise. A trader can read signs of weaker commitment, such as political pressure on a central bank or fiscal dominance, as reasons for higher breakeven inflation and steeper nominal yield curves, and allocate to inflation-linked bonds and real assets.
