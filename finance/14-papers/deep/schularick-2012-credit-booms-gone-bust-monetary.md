## Deep Read

Source: open-access NBER Working Paper 15512, November 2009, revised July 2011, the version published in the American Economic Review 102(2), 2012. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

How have money and bank credit evolved since 1870, how do financial crises affect them and the real economy, and does past credit growth predict crises?

### Data and Sample

A new annual dataset for 14 advanced economies from 1870 to 2008: the US, Canada, Australia, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland and the UK. Variables: broad and narrow money, total bank loans, total bank assets, real GDP, investment, prices, interest rates and, for later tests, stock prices. Crisis dates follow Bordo, Reinhart and Rogoff and Laeven and Valencia. The prediction panel has 1,272 country-years.

### Method

Descriptive series of loans and assets relative to GDP and to broad money. An event study of cumulative effects in the five years after a crisis against the noncrisis trend (Table 2). Crisis prediction with OLS linear probability and logit models of a crisis dummy on five lags of real loan growth, with and without country and year fixed effects (Table 3). Predictive power is scored by the area under the ROC curve (AUROC). Later tables swap in money growth, split pre- and post-1945, add controls, and test stock prices and credit-to-GDP levels.

### Main Results

Section 3: before World War II loans, assets and broad money grew at similar rates, 4.16, 4.33 and 3.65 percent a year. After 1945 credit grew faster than money, through more leverage and nonmonetary funding: the "Age of Credit."

Table 2: after a crisis, post-1945 real GDP sits 7.9 percent below its noncrisis trend over years 0 to 5 and real investment 25.7 percent below; before 1945 the losses are 4.1 and 19 percent. Post-1945 broad money falls less (minus 7.7 percent against minus 13.9) and the price level does not fall, as policy now cushions money and prices.

Table 3: the second lag of real loan growth is the strong predictor. The sum of lag coefficients is 0.425 in OLS without fixed effects and 9.697 in the baseline country fixed effects logit. A one standard deviation rise in five-year real loan growth, about 0.07, raises the crisis probability by about 2.8 percentage points against a base rate just under 4 percent. AUROC is 0.717 in the baseline logit. Country effects are insignificant.

Out-of-sample test on post-1983 data with rolling regressions: AUROC 0.646 (s.e. 0.0695), significant at 5 percent, against 0.763 in sample.

### Robustness

Table 4: credit beats broad money and narrow money as a predictor. Table 5 splits pre- and post-1945 with similar results. Table 6 adds controls for GDP growth, inflation and interest rates. Tables 7 and 8: lagged stock price falls add little, while credit levels relative to GDP interact with growth.

### Stated Limits

Credit is far from a perfect classifier; credit may grow with real gains in periods of financial development. The authors raise reverse causality between government backstops and credit growth and leave it open.

### Retail Systematic Trader

Fast real credit growth over the past two to five years is the best macro warning of a banking crisis in this long sample. A retail allocator can track credit-to-GDP gaps in the countries it holds and reduce bank stocks, cyclical equities and leverage after sustained credit booms. The signal is annual and the out-of-sample AUROC is 0.65, useful for position sizing over years, weak for timing.
