## Deep Read

Source: open-access NBER Working Paper 13420, September 2007, the version published in the Journal of Economic Literature 47(2), 2009. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

What does field evidence, outside the lab, show about deviations from the standard economic model, and how do markets and firms respond to them?

### Data and Sample

A survey of field studies. Its finance examples draw on Odean's 1987 to 1993 discount brokerage accounts, US earnings announcements, 401(k) plan records, Swedish pension choices, and price data around news events.

### Method

The survey sorts evidence into three classes. Non-standard preferences: self-control and present bias (Section 2.1), reference dependence and loss aversion (2.2), social preferences (2.3). Non-standard beliefs: overconfidence (3.1), the law of small numbers (3.2), projection bias (3.3). Non-standard decision making: limited attention (4.1), menu effects (4.2), persuasion and social pressure (4.3), emotions (4.4). Section 5 covers how firms, employers and politicians respond. Section 6 reviews identification: menu choice, natural experiments, field experiments, correlational studies and structural estimation.

### Main Results

Self-control: in DellaVigna and Malmendier (2006), health club members on a monthly contract of about $80 attend 4.8 times a month and pay about $17 a visit when a $10 pay-per-visit option exists; survey evidence points to overestimated future attendance.

Defaults: in Madrian and Shea (2001), automatic 401(k) enrollment raised participation one year after hire to 86 percent from 49 percent. In Sweden's 2000 pension reform, 43.3 percent of new participants took the default fund, rising to 91.6 percent later.

Disposition effect: in Odean (1998), investors realize 14.8 percent of paper gains and 9.8 percent of paper losses, a gap that rebalancing, later returns and transaction costs do not explain.

Limited attention: in DellaVigna and Pollet (2006), the two days around an earnings announcement capture 58 percent of the 75-day response, implying about 42 percent of investors are inattentive; for Friday announcements the share is 41 percent. Firms release worse news on Fridays. Huberman and Regev (2001): EntreMed rose 28 percent on a Nature article and jumped again months later when the New York Times put the same story on its front page.

Salience: individual investors buy attention-grabbing stocks (Barber and Odean).

### Robustness

Not applicable to a survey. The author weighs each finding by the quality of identification described in Section 6.

### Stated Limits

The survey covers a subset of field evidence and leaves out much of behavioral finance and lab work. It discusses two arguments for why biases might wash out, experience and aggregation, and gives reasons each falls short, while granting that experience reduces some biases such as the endowment effect.

### Retail Systematic Trader

Several documented biases map to tradeable patterns: underreaction to earnings news, stronger after Friday announcements and when many firms report at once; delayed response to stale but newly salient news; and the disposition effect, which slows price adjustment. A rules-based trader can hold post-announcement drift positions and weight Friday or crowded-day surprises more. The same evidence argues for automating one's own decisions: defaults, fixed rules and pre-commitment guard against the biases the survey documents.
