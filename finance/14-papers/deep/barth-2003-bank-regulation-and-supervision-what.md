## Deep Read

Source: open-access NBER Working Paper 9323, November 2002, the version published in the Journal of Financial Intermediation 13(2), 2004. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

Which bank regulatory and supervisory practices go with deeper, more efficient and more stable banking systems: capital rules, activity restrictions, entry limits, deposit insurance, official supervisory power, private monitoring, or government ownership?

### Data and Sample

A new World Bank survey of bank regulation and supervision in 107 countries, answered by national regulators around 1998 to 2000 (Tables 1 and 2). Outcomes: bank credit to the private sector over GDP for development, net interest margins and overhead costs for efficiency, nonperforming loans, and a dummy for a major banking crisis. Regressions use 40 to 75 countries depending on data.

### Method

Indices built from survey answers: restrictions on securities, insurance and real estate activities; entry requirements and limits on foreign entry; capital regulatory stringency; official supervisory power; private monitoring (audits, ratings, disclosure, no explicit deposit insurance); government ownership share; and the Demirguc-Kunt and Detragiache moral hazard index of deposit insurance generosity. Cross-country OLS of outcomes on the indices with controls for income, inflation and institutions (Table 3); logits of crisis on the indices (Table 4); instrumental variables using legal origin, latitude and religion (Table 5).

### Main Results

Table 3: the private monitoring index enters bank development at 0.089 (p = 0.003), net interest margin at minus 0.010 (p = 0.012) and nonperforming loans at minus 0.042 (p = 0.007). Restrictions on bank activities enter bank development at minus 0.118 (p = 0.001). Official supervisory power is insignificant across outcomes. Entry requirements raise overhead costs, by 0.003 per standard deviation against a mean of 0.039. Capital stringency is weakly related to lower nonperforming loans (minus 0.035, p = 0.058) and to nothing else.

Table 4: activity restrictions raise the probability of a major banking crisis, coefficients 0.63 to 1.88 with p-values from 0.016 to 0.083 in five of six specifications. Limits on foreign bank entry go with higher crisis risk; foreign ownership itself does not.

Government ownership goes with higher nonperforming loans and lower development. Generous deposit insurance raises crisis risk.

Table 5: IV estimates confirm that activity restrictions hurt development and stability and private monitoring helps.

### Robustness

Controls for income, corruption and institutions; an interaction of activity restrictions with corruption (insignificant); IV estimation; and survey answers measured after crises, which the authors say bias against their results since most crisis countries did not change policy.

### Stated Limits

The authors call the results tentative. Causality is not settled; regulation is measured at one point in time; the sample of crises is small; the survey answers come from regulators and may be imprecise.

### Retail Systematic Trader

No direct signal. For country selection in bank stocks or financial sector ETFs, regimes that rely on disclosure, private monitoring and open entry have deeper, more profitable and more stable banks, while heavy activity restrictions, closed foreign entry, state ownership and generous deposit insurance raise crisis risk. A retail allocator can use these traits as a slow-moving screen for emerging market bank exposure.
