## Deep Read

Source: open-access NBER Working Paper 12149, March 2006, the text of Campbell's American Finance Association presidential address. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

How well do households use financial markets, which mistakes are common, and who makes them?

### Data and Sample

US Survey of Consumer Finances 2001, 4,304 households, for asset participation and portfolio shares (Table I). The 2001 and 2003 American Housing Survey for mortgage refinancing and reported mortgage rates (Table II). Swedish administrative wealth tax records for almost 5 million households, with holdings of every stock and mutual fund at the end of 2002, from joint work with Calvet and Sodini.

### Method

Logit regressions of whether a household owns public equity or a private business on income, wealth, education, age, race and stated risk tolerance, plus OLS of portfolio shares for participants. Logits of whether a household refinanced a 30-year fixed mortgage between 2001 and 2003, and whether it moved. For Sweden, a covariance matrix of all held assets gives each household's portfolio variance, split into a part explained by the MSCI All World index and an idiosyncratic residual. Equations (1) to (3) decompose idiosyncratic risk into asset volatility, concentration and correlation.

### Main Results

Table I: the reference household, a married white man without a high school diploma, owns public equity with probability 56.5 percent. A college diploma raises that by 27.9 points and graduate school by 32.2 points. Stating no tolerance for investment risk lowers it by 40.9 points. A one standard deviation rise in log wealth raises it by 37.3 points.

Table II: a reference household refinanced with probability 27.9 percent between 2001 and 2003, a period of falling rates. A college diploma raises the probability by 9.2 points, being white by 7.7 points. More educated households are less likely to report a rate two or more points below the Freddie Mac benchmark, a sign of reporting error among less educated owners.

Sweden: the median household's risky portfolio has a 21 percent standard deviation, 14 percent systematic and 16 percent idiosyncratic, so more than half of its variance is diversifiable. The 95th percentile portfolio has 51 percent volatility and the 99th has 70 percent, mostly idiosyncratic.

### Robustness

Income and wealth are truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles; results hold with income in levels. The Swedish split is repeated with the krona-denominated MSCI return, where slightly less than half of the median variance is idiosyncratic.

### Stated Limits

The address leaves out savings decisions, credit card debt, fees charged by funds and banks, tax-advantaged retirement saving, payday lending, the wealthiest households and bargaining within families. The Swedish panel is too short to measure performance.

### Retail Systematic Trader

The common retail mistakes are non-participation, concentration in a few stocks and failure to exercise valuable options such as refinancing. A systematic book addresses the second by design: hold enough names, or an index sleeve, that idiosyncratic variance stays small next to the signal. Campbell also notes that some products carry cross-subsidies from naive to sophisticated users, which is where retail edge can come from: reading the terms of embedded options in mortgages, bonuses and fee schedules.
