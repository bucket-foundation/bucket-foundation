## Deep Read

Source: open-access NBER Working Paper 7590, March 2000, the version published in the Journal of Finance 56(1), 2001. The body of the PDF extracts with every character shifted by three code points; the text was decoded before reading. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

Has the volatility of individual stocks risen relative to the market, how do market, industry and firm-level volatility move over the business cycle, and what does that mean for diversification?

### Data and Sample

CRSP daily returns for NYSE, AMEX and NASDAQ stocks, July 1962 to December 1997, and monthly market data from 1926. Industries follow Fama and French's 49 groups. Stocks with daily returns above 200 percent or zero returns on 75 percent of days are excluded.

### Method

A decomposition that needs no betas: each stock's return splits into the market return, an industry-minus-market term and a firm-minus-industry term, with value weights. Monthly variance series MKT, IND and FIRM are built from daily returns within each month. The authors test for unit roots (Table 2), fit linear trends with Vogelsang's PS test robust to serial correlation (Table 3), study cyclical behavior against NBER dates and GDP (Tables 8 and 9), run Granger causality tests among the three series, and simulate random portfolios of 2 to 50 stocks (Figure 6).

### Main Results

Table 3 and Section II: firm-level variance more than doubled from 1962 to 1997, while market and industry variance rose by about a third. The FIRM trend is positive and significant whether or not the 1987 crash is downweighted; market variance shows no significant trend.

Figure 5: average pairwise correlation of stocks, from five years of monthly data, fell from 0.28 in the early 1960s to 0.08 in 1997; from one year of daily data, from 0.12 to between 0.02 and 0.04 in the 1990s. The market model R2 for a typical stock fell in step.

Figure 6: a typical 2-stock portfolio's volatility rose from below 30 percent in the early 1960s to almost 50 percent in 1997. A 20-stock portfolio cut excess standard deviation to about 10 percent in 1963 to 1973 and 1974 to 1985; in 1986 to 1997 the same level needed 50 stocks.

Section IV: market variance is about three times higher in recessions than expansions; industry and firm variance roughly double. Market volatility tends to lead the other series. Volatility measures, industry volatility above all, help forecast GDP growth and reduce the role of other forecasting variables.

### Robustness

Results hold with the crash downweighted, a fixed number of firms, and weekly or monthly returns in place of daily. Table 1 and Table 2 report autocorrelations and unit root tests with and without the crash.

### Stated Limits

The authors suggest causes, among them more firms listing earlier in their life, breakups of conglomerates and compensation tied to stock prices, but do not test them. The trend may not continue; the sample ends at a market peak.

### Retail Systematic Trader

A retail book holding 10 to 20 stocks carries more idiosyncratic risk than older textbook figures suggest; by the 1990s, 50 names were needed for the diversification 20 used to buy. Size single-name positions on firm-level volatility and expect correlations to rise in downturns. Rising market volatility tends to precede rises in firm and industry volatility and weaker growth, which supports cutting gross exposure when market volatility climbs.
