## Deep Read

Source: open-access NBER Working Paper 10504, May 2004, the version prepared for the Journal of Economic Perspectives. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

How well do small markets in event contracts aggregate dispersed information into forecasts, which contract designs reveal which beliefs, and where do these markets fail?

### Data and Sample

Several sources pulled together: Iowa Electronic Markets election contracts, Tradesports and Betfair event contracts, Hollywood Stock Exchange box office and Oscar prices, internal firm markets at Hewlett-Packard, the first year of Goldman Sachs and Deutsche Bank "Economic Derivatives" on US data releases, and Tradesports S&P 500 range contracts in summer 2003 set beside CME December S&P options. The economic data sample is small: 16 payroll releases, 12 retail sales releases and 11 ISM releases.

### Method

A survey with descriptive comparisons. Table 1 maps contract design to the parameter it reveals: a winner-takes-all contract prices a probability, an index contract the mean, and an even-money spread contract the median. Families of contracts trace out the full distribution. The authors compare market forecasts with survey consensus by correlation and mean absolute error, compare implied standard errors with agency figures, and convert option prices into range-contract prices following Leigh, Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2003) to test for cross-market arbitrage.

### Main Results

Table 3: market and consensus forecasts of economic releases track each other, with correlations of 0.91 for payrolls, 0.94 for retail sales and 0.95 for ISM. Correlation with the actual figure is 0.22 for the market and 0.26 for consensus on payrolls, 0.73 against 0.70 on retail sales, and 0.91 against 0.83 on ISM. Mean absolute error for payrolls is 72.2 thousand for the market and 71.1 thousand for consensus. The authors find no meaningful difference in accuracy.

The market-implied standard error of the retail sales forecast is 0.42 percent against an agency estimate near 0.5 percent; for payrolls the implied figure of 81,100 sits close to the authors' estimate of 81,500 for the preliminary release.

Table 4: Tradesports priced S&P 500 tail ranges above the CME option-implied values. "Under 600" bid 5 and asked 8 against an option-implied 1; "600 to 699" bid 4 and asked 7 against 2. The gap left a small arbitrage that persisted through most of summer 2003 and reappeared in 2004, with similar patterns in crude oil, gold and currency contracts. The authors read this as a favorite-longshot bias stronger on small exchanges.

Known attempts to manipulate these markets had little lasting effect on prices. Simple strategies built on past prices showed no profit.

### Robustness

No formal tests. The authors flag that the economic data sample is too small for firm conclusions.

### Stated Limits

Markets can be distorted by the longshot bias and by bubbles when arbitrage capital is limited. Thin markets are open to manipulation. Real-money and play-money exchanges were not linked by arbitrage: in August 2003 Bush was a 67 percent favorite on real-money exchanges and priced differently on play-money ones. Conditional markets reveal correlation; they do not settle causation.

### Retail Systematic Trader

The tradeable finding is the tail mispricing in Table 4: small event exchanges overprice longshots relative to listed options on the same underlying. A retail trader with access to both venues can sell overpriced tails on the event exchange and hedge with listed options, sized to the thin depth and after the exchange fee. For forecasting data releases, event prices add little over consensus. Any modern replication should check whether Kalshi and Polymarket range contracts show the same tail premium.
