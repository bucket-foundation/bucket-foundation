## Abstract

<!-- voice-ignore-next 1 -->
> We analyze the response of small versus large manufacturing firms to monetary policy. The goal is to obtain evidence on the importance of financial propagation mechanisms for aggregate activity. We find that small firms account for a significantly disproportionate share of the manufacturing decline that follows tightening of monetary policy. They play a surprisingly prominent role in the slowdown of inventory demand. Large firms initially borrow to accumulate inventories. After a brief period, small firms quickly shed inventories. We attempt to sort financial from nonfinancial explanations with evidence on asymmetries and on balance sheet effects on inventory demand across size classes.

## Deep Read

### Research Question

Do small manufacturing firms respond more than large ones to tight money and to the business cycle, as credit market frictions predict?

### Data and Sample

The Census Bureau's Quarterly Financial Report for Manufacturing Corporations, which covers all manufacturing firms by asset size class, quarterly from 1958 into the late 1980s. Real sales growth is aggregated into a small-firm and a large-firm series. Table 1: firms under $5 million in assets held 26 percent of manufacturing sales in 1960 and 12 percent by 1990. Monetary indicators: M2 growth, the federal funds rate, the commercial paper to Treasury bill risk spread, the bank loan to commercial paper "mix," and the Romer and Romer (1989) dates of deliberate tightening.

### Method

A partial equilibrium model in which firms facing higher external finance premia react more to interest rate and demand shocks. Empirically: cross-correlations of small and large firm sales with GNP (Table 3); VARs with four lags of small firm sales growth, large firm sales growth, real GNP growth and one monetary variable, with exclusion tests (Table 4); impulse responses to funds rate innovations; and dynamic responses after the Romer dates. Bank loans to small and large firms are compared after tightening.

### Main Results

Table 3: small and large firm sales growth correlate with GNP at similar levels, 0.44 and 0.55 contemporaneously, so the small-firm finding does not come from greater unconditional volatility.

Table 4: lagged GNP growth predicts small-firm sales (chi-square 11.31, p = 0.02) and not large-firm sales (6.22, p = 0.18). M2 growth predicts small firms (p = 0.02) and not large firms (p = 0.19). The bank loan mix predicts small firms (p = 0.01) and not large ones (p = 0.67). The funds rate predicts both. The difference between small and large firm growth responds to GNP, M2, the funds rate and the mix, each at p of 0.02 or below.

After Romer dates of tight money, small-firm sales growth runs more than four percentage points a year below large-firm growth for two and a half years. Bank loans to small firms contract after a funds rate rise while short-term bank loans to large firms rise.

### Robustness

Two identification schemes for policy shocks, funds rate innovations and Romer dates, give the same pattern. Aggregating size classes biases the test toward understating small-firm sensitivity, by the authors' account.

### Stated Limits

Small firms may sit in industries that are more interest sensitive and cyclical, and the data needed to rule out industry composition were incomplete. Nominal size cutoffs shift with inflation, which the authors address by using growth rates and two broad classes.

### Retail Systematic Trader

Small firms take more of the hit from tight money, through bank credit. A rules-based allocator can lower small-cap weight, or favor large caps, during and after monetary tightening cycles, and restore it when credit eases. The effect plays out over quarters to years in sales data and is a tilt input, slow and noisy on its own.
