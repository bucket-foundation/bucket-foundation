## Abstract

<!-- voice-ignore-next 1 -->
> A discretionary policymaker can create surprise inflation, which may reduce employment and raise government revenue. But when people understand the policymaker's objectives, these surprises cannot occur systematically. In equilibrium people form expectations rationally and the policymaker optimizes in each period, subject to the way that people form expectations. Then, we find that (1) the rates of monetary growth and inflation are excessive; (2) these rates depend on the slope of Phillips curve, the natural unemployment rate, and other variables that affect the benefits and costs from inflation; (3) the monetary authority behaves countercyclically; and (4) unemployment is independent of money policy. Outcomes improve if rules commit future policy choices in the appropriate manner. The value of these commitments--which amount to long-term contracts between the government and the private sector--underlies the argument for rules over discretion.

## Deep Read

Source: open-access NBER Working Paper 807, the version published in the Journal of Political Economy 91(4), 1983. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

Natural-rate models say systematic monetary policy cannot move unemployment. Why then do central banks run high, variable money growth and lean against the cycle? Can that behavior arise from a rational policymaker facing rational expectations?

### Data and Sample

None. A theory model, motivated by empirical work that finds countercyclical US money growth.

### Method

Equation (1), an expectations-augmented Phillips curve: unemployment U(t) equals the natural rate minus alpha times unexpected inflation. The natural rate follows an autoregressive process with persistence lambda, equation (2). Equation (3), the policymaker's per-period cost: a times (U minus kU-natural)^2 plus b times inflation^2, with 0 < k < 1, so the target unemployment rate sits below the natural rate because of distortions such as taxes and unemployment insurance. The policymaker cannot commit; each period it takes expectations as given and minimizes expected discounted cost, equation (4). The public knows this problem and forms expectations to match the policymaker's choice. The authors compare this discretionary equilibrium with a rule fixed in advance.

### Main Results

Equations (8) and (9): in the discretionary equilibrium, inflation equals (a alpha / b)(1 minus k) times the expected natural rate. Expectations match actual inflation, so unemployment equals the natural rate and policy has no real effect.

Average inflation is too high: it rises with the slope alpha, with the weight a on unemployment against b on inflation, with the gap 1 minus k between target and natural unemployment, and with the natural rate itself.

Policy looks countercyclical: when the lagged natural rate rises, expected inflation and money growth rise with it, which matches the countercyclical money growth found in data, even though the policy achieves nothing real.

A binding rule that sets expected inflation to zero gives the same unemployment with zero average inflation, so the rule beats discretion. Discretion is the subset of rules that gives no guarantee about future behavior.

### Robustness

The authors extend the model to show the countercyclical pattern holds under several assumptions about information and shocks, and discuss cases where commitment fails because the rule must itself be credible.

### Stated Limits

The model has one policymaker with a quadratic cost and treats alpha as constant. It does not explain how commitment devices are built or kept credible, a gap the companion paper on reputation takes up. Costs of setting up rules are ignored.

### Retail Systematic Trader

No trading signal. The model explains why inflation drifts higher when central banks lack commitment and why money growth rises in downturns. For a rules-based allocator, institutional changes that weaken commitment, such as pressure on central bank independence, point to higher average inflation and favor inflation-linked bonds, commodities and short duration.
