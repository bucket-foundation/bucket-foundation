Source: open-access World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 2059, February 1999, the version published in the Journal of Monetary Economics 2000. Read 2026-11-27.

### Research Question

Does the development of banks and other financial intermediaries cause faster economic growth, and do legal and accounting systems explain why some countries have deeper intermediaries?

### Data and Sample

71 countries for the cross-section, data averaged over 1960 to 1995. A panel of non-overlapping five-year averages over the same years, up to seven observations per country, 359 observations in levels. Three measures of intermediary development: LIQUID LIABILITIES over GDP, COMMERCIAL-CENTRAL BANK (deposit bank assets over deposit bank plus central bank assets) and PRIVATE CREDIT by banks and other institutions over GDP. Legal origin dummies (English, French, German, Scandinavian) from La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes, Shleifer and Vishny, extended from 49 to 71 countries. Creditor rights, contract enforcement and accounting standards for 35 countries.

### Method

Cross-section GMM with legal origin as instruments for financial development, testing the overidentifying restrictions. Dynamic panel GMM: the Arellano and Bond difference estimator and the Arellano-Bover and Blundell-Bond system estimator, with lagged values as instruments, Sargan tests and serial correlation tests. Three conditioning sets: simple (initial income, schooling), policy (adds government size, inflation, black market premium, trade openness) and full (adds revolutions, assassinations and ethnic diversity). OLS of financial development on legal and accounting indicators.

### Main Results

Table 3, cross-section: the coefficient on PRIVATE CREDIT is 2.515 (t = 3.09) with simple controls, 3.222 with policy controls and 3.356 (t = 2.92) with the full set. COMMERCIAL-CENTRAL BANK enters at 9.6 to 11.3 and LIQUID LIABILITIES at 1.7 to 2.8, all significant at 5 percent. The overidentifying restrictions are not rejected, so legal origin affects growth only through financial development.

Section III example: India's PRIVATE CREDIT averaged 19.5 percent of GDP against a developing-country mean of 25 percent. Raising it to the mean implies about 0.6 percentage point more growth a year; Argentina, at 16 percent, would have grown more than 1 point faster.

Table 4, panel: the system estimator gives PRIVATE CREDIT coefficients of 2.237 with simple controls and 1.448 with policy controls, both with p-values of 0.001 and Sargan tests that do not reject.

Table 7: contract enforcement (ENFORCE) enters with a positive sign, significant at 5 percent, in every specification. Accounting standards matter for private credit (0.014, p = 0.009). Creditor rights matter only for liquid liabilities in one specification.

### Robustness

Three conditioning sets, two panel estimators, levels and differences, and Sargan and serial correlation tests. Table 8 uses the legal and accounting variables as instruments in place of legal origin.

### Stated Limits

Data limits prevent a full evaluation of regulatory determinants such as deposit insurance and bank restrictions. The legal and accounting indicators cover only 35 countries.

### Retail Systematic Trader

No trading signal. The link from credit depth and contract enforcement to growth supports a country tilt for long-horizon allocation: markets with improving creditor protection and accounting standards should see deeper credit and faster growth. The effect plays out over decades and gives no timing signal.
