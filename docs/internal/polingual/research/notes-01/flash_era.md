# Free Flash Web Game Era and Its Successors

## Portals: Who They Were, Owners, Status

### Takeaway
The big portals launched between 1995 and 2007, most were bought by media or games companies between 2005 and 2017, and by 2020 to 2022 the survivors either moved to HTML5 with Ruffle for old Flash content (Newgrounds, Armor Games, Coolmath) or left browser games for mobile (Miniclip closed its portal in July 2022; Kongregate stopped taking new games on 1 July 2020).

### Cited Findings
- Newgrounds: founded by Tom Fulp on 6 July 1995; "The Portal" for Flash submissions created 1999, automated by 2000; over 1.5M users and 130,000+ animations by Nov 2008; 2.2M users and 180,000+ games and films by Aug 2010; still active, added controller mapping for Flash games 12 May 2025 ([Wikipedia: Newgrounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newgrounds))
- Miniclip: launched 30 March 2001 by Robert Small and Tihan Presbie on a £40,000 budget; valued over £275M in 2008; Tencent took a majority stake Feb 2015; CEO announced the move from browser to mobile in April 2021; browser portal shut July 2022, keeping only Agar.io and 8 Ball Pool; 8 Ball Pool earned over $400M by 2018; bought SYBO (Subway Surfers) July 2022; agreed to buy Easybrain for $1.2B in Nov 2024 ([Wikipedia: Miniclip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miniclip))
- Kongregate: alpha launch 10 Oct 2006 by siblings Emily and Jim Greer; public Dec 2007; about $9M raised by July 2008 (Reid Hoffman, Jeff Bezos, Greylock); GameStop acquisition announced 23 July 2010; MTG bought it for $55M (deal announced 20 June 2017); stopped accepting new game submissions 1 July 2020; Kartridge store opened Nov 2018 and shut Sep 2023; sold to Monumental in 2024 with MTG keeping 30%; site lists 124,000+ online games ([Wikipedia: Kongregate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kongregate))
- Armor Games: founded 2004 by Daniel McNeely as Games of Gondor, renamed Armor Games Oct 2005; sponsored Fancy Pants (2006), Achievement Unlocked (2008), Crush the Castle (2009), The World's Hardest Game (2008); McNeely CEO 2004 to 2021, John Cooney 2021 to 2023; chose Ruffle Dec 2020; Armor Games Studios publishing team had layoffs Aug 2024; HQ Irvine, California ([Wikipedia: Armor Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armor_Games))
- Addicting Games: acquired by Atom Entertainment (Shockwave.com parent) in 2004 or Nov 2005 (sources conflict); MTV Networks (Viacom) bought Atom for $200M, announced Aug 2006; Defy Media bought Addicting Games and Shockwave from Viacom June 2014; later sold to Enthusiast Gaming in 2021 ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/pc/mtv-networks-acquires-atom-entertainment); [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/46632/mtv-networks-buys-gaming-video-sites.html); [TheWrap](https://thewrap.com/how-gaming-hub-addicting-games-revived-itself-a-decade-after-its-sale-to-viacom); [dinogame.gg](https://dinogame.gg/blog/history-of-addictinggames/))
- Coolmath: site launched 1997; parent Constructive Media sold by H.I.G. Capital to Sandbox & Co. (Sandbox Group) Nov 2018; mid-2019 rumor of a 2020 shutdown drew a ~100,000-signature Change.org petition (the rumor was a hoax); in 2020 began emulating Flash with Ruffle and adding HTML5 games, traffic rose during COVID; funded by Google Ads plus ad-free subscription; screens out violence, gore and freeform chat; released Cool Math Games: The Game on Steam Dec 2024 ([Wikipedia: Coolmath Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolmath_Games))
- Mochi Media's network had about 40,000 Flash developer members who published over 150,000 games ([Construct forum post via search](https://construct.net/en/forum/game-development/distribution-and-publishing-26/r-i-p-mochi-media-hang-onto-74937))
- Portal traffic decline: a 2015 Game Developer analysis tracked Alexa US ranks over three years; top-tier portals held rank better but still declined, second-tier portals fell most; Armor Games' release count halved in the two years to 2015; Not Doppler went from 7 to 6 releases a week in early 2015 ([Game Developer, 2015](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-online-browser-games-industry-flash-and-html5))

### Inferences
- The model of the era: one portal brand, thousands of SWFs, genre tag pages, ratings, and IAB banner units around the game frame. Miniclip had a "Learning" and "Brain Training" category by 2007, so education sat inside the mainstream portal taxonomy as a minor shelf.
- Ownership consolidated into media (Viacom), retail (GameStop) and games companies (Tencent, MTG), then shifted to mobile once Flash declined after 2010 to 2014.

## How Portals and Developers Made Money

### Takeaway
Developers earned mainly from sponsorships (a portal paid a one-off fee to brand the game and its links), then site-locked licenses, then in-game ad networks (MochiAds, CPMStar) and Kongregate's ad revenue share; microtransactions via Kongregate Kreds arrived around 2010. Ads were often under 10% of a game's income. Portals earned from display ads around games and pre-roll ads.

### Cited Findings
- A Mochi-sponsored survey of 1,000+ Flash developers: 48% earned via sponsorship, a quarter via licensing, a fifth via ads on host sites; 20% earned over $1,000 a month ([MCV UK](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/20-of-flash-devs-earn-over-1000-per-month/))
- Primary sponsorship: sponsor's branding goes on the distributable version while the developer keeps the right to sell site-locked versions; FGL said a primary deal pays a slight discount and about 95% of an exclusive license's value ([GameDev.net FGL interview](https://gamedev.net/tutorials/industry/interviews/flashgamelicensecom-interview-r2683))
- Non-exclusive license: selling a rebranded, site-locked copy to one site; FGL called "essentially an eBay for sponsored games"; one portal paid $750 for logo changes and site-lock code ([Stencyl guide](https://stencyl.com/help/viewPrint/134))
- FGL licensed games averaged about $1,428.57 each (rough estimate) ([Dice](https://www.dice.com/career-advice/flash-games))
- SteamBirds case (Mar 2011): original Flash game grossed about $34,000 by Mar 2010 and $45,000 by Mar 2011 with added sitelocks; ads under 10% of revenue; Kongregate's revenue share paid 4 to 5x CPMStar and Mochi combined; Mochi owed $78 unpaid. SteamBirds: Survival got just over $10,000 from AXE as sponsor and about $10,000 from microtransactions ($0.75 to $10 per plane, "Buy ALL" at $15 made ~70% of sales). Franchise total about $200,000 across Flash and mobile ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/steambirds-survival-by-the-numbers))
- Kongregate ad share: 25% of ad revenue by default, +10% for integrating the Statistics and Challenges API, +15% for Kongregate exclusivity, max 50% ([Kongregate support](https://kongregatesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/26759058248973-Determining-my-percentage); [Gamesbrief, 2010](https://www.gamesbrief.com/2010/07/the-death-of-the-portal/); [Game Developer on Greer](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/kongregate-s-greer-details-rise-of-virtual-goods-in-business))
- Kreds (Kongregate virtual currency): one 2010 report says one third of Kongregate revenue came from Kreds while only about 0.5% of games used them ([SemiAccurate](https://semiaccurate.com/?p=16565)); launch date not confirmed
- MochiAds / Mochi Media: blog announced closure 14 March 2014, services off 31 March 2014, owner Shanda; final payments end of April 2014; TechCrunch quoted "no meaningful position beyond Flash" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2014/03/16/with-no-meaningful-position-beyond-flash-gaming-platform-mochi-media-will-close-march-31/); [Wikipedia: Mochi Media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mochi_Media)); MochiAds offered metrics, ads and automatic updates; developer counts dropped further after closure ([Game Developer, 2015](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-online-browser-games-industry-flash-and-html5))
- FGL replacement ad service FGLAds; Install Connect also closed in 2014 ([Emanuele Feronato](https://emanueleferonato.com/2014/07/11/just-some-months-after-its-launch-install-connect-closes/))
- FGL data: Flash market stagnated early 2014, declined late 2014; combined Flash plus HTML5 licensing totals halved in H2 2014; FGL stopped distributing HTML5 games early 2015 ([Game Developer, 2015](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-online-browser-games-industry-flash-and-html5))
- Ad blockers cut impressions; earnings per ad rose but total ad revenue fell ([same](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-online-browser-games-industry-flash-and-html5))

### Inferences
- Typical sponsorship pricing ran from hundreds of dollars for a site-lock to low five figures for a strong primary sponsorship; a hit could gross $45,000 in a year. Kreds-style microtransactions matched a $10,000 brand sponsorship on SteamBirds: Survival.

### Gaps
- No primary-source table of sponsorship prices by year; FGL's own price reports were not retrieved.
- No CPMStar rates or pre-roll CPMs found.
- Kreds launch date unconfirmed.

## How Flash Games Worked

### Takeaway
A Flash game was one compiled SWF file, written in ActionScript 2 (2003) or ActionScript 3 (2006, AVM2), run by the Flash Player browser plugin; portals embedded the SWF with object/embed tags, and because a SWF was self-contained it was copied freely across portals, which is why sponsors paid for in-game branding and links and why site-locking existed.

### Cited Findings
- FutureWave's FutureSplash Player (1996) became Macromedia Flash Player the same year after Macromedia bought FutureWave; Adobe acquired Macromedia 3 Dec 2005 ([Wikipedia: Adobe Flash Player](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_Flash_Player))
- Flash Player 7 (10 Sep 2003) introduced ActionScript 2.0; Flash Player 9 (22 June 2006) introduced ActionScript 3 on the AVM2 virtual machine ([same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_Flash_Player))
- Reach: about 92% of internet users around 2000; in 2013 Adobe said over 400M of more than 1B connected desktops updated within six weeks of a release ([same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_Flash_Player))
- April 2010: Steve Jobs's open letter refusing Flash on iPhone, iPod Touch and iPad ([same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_Flash_Player))
- Distribution: Flash distribution networks included Flash Game Distribution and Talk Arcades; "Flash still has a wider distribution potential" than HTML5 in 2015 ([Game Developer, 2015](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-online-browser-games-industry-flash-and-html5))
- MochiAds let games push automatic updates without redistributing the SWF ([same](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-online-browser-games-industry-flash-and-html5))
- Kongregate's Statistics and Challenges API (high scores, badges) earned developers an extra 10% ad share ([Kongregate support](https://kongregatesupport.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/26759058248973-Determining-my-percentage))
- Miniclip offered "Free Website Games" for webmasters to embed (Wayback snapshot, 1 Jan 2008, https://web.archive.org/web/20080101090847/http://www.miniclip.com/games/en/)

### Inferences
- Site-locking was a check in the SWF of the host domain; a sponsor version carried the sponsor's logo and "more games" links so every copy spreading to other portals sent traffic back to the sponsor.

### Gaps
- No primary technical doc retrieved for site-lock code or the Kongregate API calls.
- 98 to 99% desktop penetration figure often quoted was not confirmed; Wikipedia gives 92% around 2000.

## Language-Learning and Educational Flash Games

### Takeaway
Educational games had a shelf on portals (Miniclip "Learning" and "Brain Training" categories in 2007; Coolmath's whole brand) and in school-oriented sites (Funbrain since 1997, BBC Dance Mat Typing), but no language-learning Flash game appears among the era's known hits. Typing and math dominated.

### Cited Findings
- Miniclip's 2007 category list included "Learning" and "Brain Training"; multiplayer list included the word game "Anagrammatic" (Wayback, https://web.archive.org/web/20080101090847/http://www.miniclip.com/games/en/)
- Coolmath screens games as non-violent and "educational"; Common Sense Media found "some" educational value but many ads ([Wikipedia: Coolmath Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolmath_Games)); kids played entertainment Flash games at school under the cover of the educational brand ([CBR](https://www.cbr.com/flash-games-millennials-played-in-school/))
- Funbrain, free educational games since 1997; its Words section had 15 games for vocabulary, grammar and spelling around 2000 ([TryEngineering](https://tryengineering.org/game/funbrain-games); [search snippet](https://www.education.com/resources/games/?page=19))
- School typing staples: BBC Dance Mat Typing, Fast Typer, PopCap's Typer Shark ([whatculture](https://whatculture.com/gaming/10-games-we-all-used-to-play-in-it-class?page=8))
- Flonga's 2011 homepage had about 45 genre tags with no learning or language tag (Wayback, https://web.archive.org/web/20110102190704/http://www.flonga.com/)

### Inferences
- Language learning was an unoccupied niche on mass-market Flash portals; portals rewarded short action, physics, tower-defense and cooking games. Coolmath shows the "educational wrapper around fun games" brand could draw school traffic at scale.

### Gaps
- No source found naming a popular foreign-language-learning Flash game on Miniclip, Kongregate, Armor Games, Newgrounds or Y8, and no play counts for educational titles.

## Flash End of Life and Preservation

### Takeaway
Adobe announced on 25 July 2017 that Flash would end; it ended 31 Dec 2020 and a kill switch began blocking content 12 Jan 2021. Ruffle (Rust/WebAssembly emulator) became the standard player for Newgrounds, Armor Games, Coolmath and the Internet Archive, and Flashpoint Archive preserved 200,000+ browser games and animations.

### Cited Findings
- Adobe announced end of support 25 July 2017; discontinued 31 Dec 2020; 12 Jan 2021 time bomb disabled installs; Chrome 88 (20 Jan 2021) and Firefox 85 (26 Jan 2021) removed support; a China-only variant via 2144.cn remains ([Wikipedia: Adobe Flash Player](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adobe_Flash_Player))
- Ruffle: begun 2016 by Mike Welsh as "Fluster"; Rust compiled to WebAssembly; as of Aug 2026 AS1/AS2 about 99% language and 82% API implemented; AS3 about 90% language and 82% API; Newgrounds sponsored it 2019; Coolmath adopted 2020; Internet Archive announced Ruffle-based Flash preservation Nov 2020; Armor Games Dec 2020; Neopets explored it July 2023 ([Wikipedia: Ruffle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruffle_(software)))
- Newgrounds released the Newgrounds Player in summer 2019 and added Ruffle in Aug 2019 ([Wikipedia: Newgrounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newgrounds))
- Flashpoint Archive: started late 2017 by Ben "BlueMaxima" Latimore, first release 26 Dec 2017; over 200,000 applications from over 120 browser plugins; 1.68 TB; 126 web technologies supported as of Flashpoint 14; latest version 14.0.3 "Kingfisher" released 21 Nov 2025; Latimore stepped down early 2023 ([Wikipedia: Flashpoint Archive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flashpoint_Archive)); two editions, Infinity (streams on demand) and Ultimate (full offline) ([Mewayz](https://mewayz.com/am/blog/flashpoint-archive-over-200k-web-games-and-animations-preserved))
- Archived Flonga pages on the Wayback Machine now load window.RufflePlayer, meaning the Wayback Machine plays archived SWFs through Ruffle (observed in fetched HTML, https://web.archive.org/web/20110102190704/http://www.flonga.com/)

- Separate game vs animation counts per Flashpoint edition not found.

## Successors: Poki, CrazyGames, Coolmath HTML5, Kongregate Decline

### Takeaway
HTML5 portals funded by ads and revenue shares replaced Flash portals. Poki (Amsterdam, 2014, self-funded) claims 100M monthly players and 1B plays in June 2025; CrazyGames (Leuven, Belgium, ~2014) claims 50M MAU at end of 2025 and 60M+ in 2026. Independent estimates put both nearer 30 to 35M MAU.

### Cited Findings
- Poki: founded Amsterdam 2014 by Michiel van Amerongen and Sebastian Moeys, self-funded ([BeBeez](https://bebeez.eu/?p=71487)); 2025: 625M players, 1B gameplays in a month (June 2025), 100M monthly players, 227 new games, staff 50 to 65; revenue-share deals with developers; Dutch Game Awards "Best in Business" Dec 2025 ([Poki blog, 2025 review](https://poki.com/blog/2025-at-poki-a-year-in-review); [Poki blog, award](https://poki.com/blog/poki-wins-dutch-game-awards-2025); [Tech Funding News](https://techfundingnews.com/browser-gaming-website-poki-won-big-at-the-dutch-game-awards-celebrating-hitting-1-billion-monthly-plays/)); monetisation is ad-based ([Mobidictum interview](https://mobidictum.com/pokis-web-gaming-interview-michiel-van-amerongen)); Similarweb estimates $100M to $200M yearly revenue, unverified ([Similarweb](https://www.similarweb.com/company/poki.com))
- Poki brought Subway Surfers titles to the web with SYBO (Miniclip-owned) ([PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/poki-expands-sybo-partnership-to-bring-more-subway-surfers-titles-to-the-web))
- CrazyGames: started by brothers Raf and Tomas Mertens, Leuven, Belgium, 2014 (some timelines say 2013); Raf is CEO ([Wikipedia: CrazyGames](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/CrazyGames)); year-end 2025 summary cited 50M MAU; 2026 release claims 60M+ monthly users and 3B gameplays ([search summary of LinkedIn and press](https://tr.linkedin.com/in/yagizefemertol)); a 2026 comparison estimates ~35M MAU and ~300M monthly gameplays and says MAU-method analysis puts both platforms near 30M ([tech-insider](https://tech-insider.org/crazygames-vs-poki-vs-coolmath-games-2026/))
- Coolmath moved to Ruffle plus HTML5 in 2020 and grew during COVID; expanded to Steam ([Wikipedia: Coolmath Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coolmath_Games))
- Kongregate: no new submissions after 1 July 2020; pivoted to mobile, Steam help (2016), Kartridge (2018 to 2023), NFT portal plans (2021) and a $40M Immutable X fund (May 2022); sold to Monumental 2024 ([Wikipedia: Kongregate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kongregate)); its 2026 developer program offers 70% of net revenue for a limited time ([Kongregate developer support](https://kongregatedevelopers.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/44203568691341))
- A 2026 State of Web Gaming report exists ([InvestGame](https://investgame.net/news/pdf/2026-06-25-the_2026_state_of_web_gaming_report_4ee6fdc260_wp/)); browser gaming described as heading toward $3B ([Pulse](https://www.pulse.bot/consumer-tech/news/browser-gaming-is-heading-toward-3-billion-here-is-why-it-is-being-taken-seriously-76ac792e-a131-49db-b337-54232c57e6bf/)); neither was read in full

### Inferences
- The successor model keeps the Flash-portal economics (free, ad-funded, curated catalog) but swaps one-off sponsorship for long-term revenue share and adds mobile web traffic. Poki and CrazyGames publish no developer price lists; the revenue share is negotiated.

### Gaps
- No audited traffic numbers; Poki's 100M and CrazyGames' 60M are self-reported and count differently.
- Poki's and CrazyGames' revenue-share percentages not found.
- Spil Games' role in the HTML5 shift (it courted HTML5 developers around 2013) is mentioned only briefly in the 2015 Game Developer piece.
