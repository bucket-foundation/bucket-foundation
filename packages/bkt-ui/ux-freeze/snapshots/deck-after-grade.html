The mole and Avogadro's number

NA=6.022×1023 mol−1N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}NA​=6.022×1023 mol−1

Intuition

Atoms are so tiny that even a pinch of matter holds mind-bending numbers of them — far too many to count one by one. So chemists invented a counting word for a fixed, huge batch: the mole. Just as "a dozen" always means 121212, "a mole" always means about 6.022×10236.022\times10^{23}6.022×1023. With a counting word this large, you can weigh out a known number of atoms on an ordinary balance.

Precisely

A mole is a fixed count of particles equal to Avogadro's number: $NA=6.022×1023 mol−1.N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}.NA​=6.022×1023 mol−1.$ The **molar mass** of a substance — the mass of one mole, in $\text{g/mol}$ — is numerically equal to its average atomic or molecular mass (the periodic-table value). The link between mass, moles, and count is $n=mM,N=n NA.n = \frac{m}{M}, \qquad N = n\,N_A.n=Mm​,N=nNA​.$

Why it's true / how it works

The mole works because molar mass is defined to make one mole of a substance weigh, in grams, what one particle weighs in atomic mass units. Since the 2019 SI redefinition, NAN_ANA​ is an exact, defined constant, so the mole is now simply that fixed particle count rather than something measured. Atomic masses remain weighted averages over an element's naturally occurring isotopes.

The whole scheme hangs together because 1 u1\ \text{u}1 u (the atomic-scale mass unit) times NAN_ANA​ equals exactly 1 g/mol1\ \text{g/mol}1 g/mol — that single numerical coincidence, built into the definitions, is what lets a periodic-table mass in "u" double as a molar mass in "g/mol."

Where it connects

The mole is the gateway to stoichiometry, the nnn in the ideal gas law PV=nRTPV=nRTPV=nRT, and the concentration unit (molarity) in solutions. It bridges the atomic and gram scales.

Practically, the mole is the unit you will use in almost every later calculation — balancing equations, finding limiting reagents, computing concentrations, and applying the gas laws — so fluency in converting between mass, moles, and number of particles pays off on every problem that follows.

Watch out

A mole counts particles, but you must specify which — atoms, molecules, ions, or electrons. One mole of O2\mathrm{O_2}O2​ contains 6.022×10236.022\times10^{23}6.022×1023 molecules but 2×2\times2× that many oxygen atoms.

Avogadro's number is enormous but it is just a count — a mole of marbles would bury the Earth, yet a mole of atoms fits in a spoonful, because atoms are unimaginably small. Don't let the size of NAN_ANA​ obscure that it plays the same role as "dozen."
